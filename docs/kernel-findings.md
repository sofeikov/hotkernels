# The first kernels: results and findings

This records the first pass over the shortlist in [survey-design.md](survey-design.md): 23 kernels for 15 NumPy operations, each with a spec, a naive C reference and an attempt at beating that C in ARM64 assembly. Twenty-two ship as assembly and one as C. It covers the kernel half of phase 3 in [core-design.md](core-design.md). The other half, the binding and `install()`, is in [delivery-design.md](delivery-design.md).

The numbers are the judge's. What is open to review is the set of decisions at the end.

The kernels exist for two architectures: ARM64, and x86-64 on Linux, written afterwards to the plan in [x86-64-design.md](x86-64-design.md). This document holds both.

## How this document is organised

- **What is true of an operation is said once.** Each family under [Results by family](#results-by-family) opens with "The operation": what NumPy requires of the result, why the simple loop is slow, the idea a fast kernel is built on, and any proof it rests on. None of that depends on the processor.
- **What is true of one architecture sits under its name.** "On ARM64" and "On x86-64" hold the instructions chosen, the block sizes, the measured speeds, what was tried and did not help, and what limits the kernel on that processor. A sentence such as "this is the hardware's limit" is only ever about the hardware of its heading.
- **A kernel's verdict is per architecture.** The same kernel can ship as assembly on one, as C on another, and not be installed on a third.
- **The x86-64 sections were written after the ARM64 ones,** from kernels written separately against the same specs. What each family was asked to try is listed in [x86-64-design.md](x86-64-design.md#candidates-per-family), with what became of it.

## Summary

### ARM64

- **Twenty-two of the 23 kernels have assembly that the judge reports as significantly faster than naive C.** The exception, `cast_u8_f32`, ties the compiler and ships as its C. Every kernel is faster than NumPy at every size measured. Twenty-one are bit-identical to NumPy. The two float `cumsum` kernels are within a declared tolerance, which is what makes them faster at all.
- **The survey's prediction for the "C kernels" was wrong.** It expected assembly to tie the compiler on `clip`, `astype`, `np.sum` on `uint8` and `count_nonzero`, because clang vectorises those loops. Tried, assembly beat the C on five of the six: by 5.9x on `sum_u8`, by 1.3x to 1.4x on the counts, and by 1.36x to 1.38x on `clip` for bounds that allow its fast path. A vectorised loop is not necessarily a good one.
- **Three results are narrower than their headline number.** `isin_i64` is fast only for test values that lie close together, and the two `clip` kernels only for some bounds and, with a lower bound of zero, only for data without negative zeros. Each is set out below.
- **The judge has blind spots that mattered.** It tested one value per scalar argument and, from 4,096 elements up, repeats one input per size. Both hid things that were found by other means. The first is now closed: a spec lists several combinations of scalar values, and the `clip`, `histogram` and masked-assignment kernels are checked with them.

### x86-64

- **All 23 kernels have assembly, and every one is faster than its naive C over the eleven sizes.** For four the margin is small enough that shipping the C would be defensible: `bincount_u8` (1.04x), `count_nonzero_f64` (1.06x), `clip_f64` (1.09x) and `cast_u8_f32` (1.21x, level with NumPy in the middle sizes). Those verdicts are a person's to confirm. Twenty-one are bit-identical to NumPy; the two float `cumsum` kernels are within the declared tolerance, as on ARM64.
- **NumPy is a different opponent.** It has AVX2 loops for `argmax`, `clip` and `astype` here. `argmax` and `argmin` still gain 1.7x to 1.9x at a million elements; `clip` and `astype` are level with NumPy there and gain at the two ends.
- **Memory is the ceiling sooner.** A scalar chain costs one cycle per element on this core against three on the M1, and a plain copy in the second-level cache is only 1.65x faster than that. `cumsum_i64` is the kernel that shows it: 1.18x over the naive C.
- **Several ARM64 caveats do not exist in the x86-64 kernels:** the three of `clip`, the density at which the mask kernels are weak, mask bytes other than 0 and 1, the unbounded second scan of `argmax`, the slow last bin of `histogram`, the 1e154 limit of float `cumsum`. They are listed under [What a caller must know](#what-a-caller-must-know). None was removed from the binding or the rules on the kernels' authority.
- **One reference was wrong for this architecture.** `interp_f64`'s fused the multiply and the add, which NumPy's x86-64 build does not. It was corrected in a pull request of its own, and the kernel's row below is a locked run against the corrected reference.
- **Kernels added since the first pass** are the last rows of the table and are described under [Second shortlist](#second-shortlist). Their runs are `--unlocked`, because each kernel's spec and reference arrived in the same pull request as its assembly; the reports in `results/` say so.
- **From Python,** with the binding and NumPy's call overhead on both sides: [delivery/results/intel-r-core-tm-i5-14600kf.md](../delivery/results/intel-r-core-tm-i5-14600kf.md). No hooked call is slower than NumPy at any size timed.

## How the numbers were taken

### ARM64

- **Machine.** Apple M1 Pro, macOS (Darwin 27.2.0), NumPy 2.5.5, Apple clang 21. Native, not emulated.
- **Command.** `uv run judge run <kernel> --unlocked` for each kernel, one after another, with nothing else running. 30 interleaved samples per size.
- **Three single passes.** The `histogram_f64` row is from a second pass of the same command, taken after scalar sets were added to the judge and the faster `histogram` path was adopted. Every other row without a † is from the first pass. `maskfill_f64` was timed again in the second pass, but the machine was no longer idle and its sizes from 1,048,576 elements up varied by a factor of two between runs; its kernel had not changed, so its first-pass row stands. Ten later runs agree with it: at 1,048,576 elements they average 18.2x over naive C with half the mask selected and 4.62x with few, against 19.0x and 4.64x in the row, with one run of the ten at 10.1x for half selected.
- **Rows marked † are the mean of ten runs on a machine that was not fully quiet.** These are the `clip`, `searchsorted_f64` and `interp_f64` rows, and the table under [`searchsorted` and `interp`](#searchsorted-and-interp). Each kernel was run ten times with `uv run judge run <kernel>`, locked, one run at a time. The machine is a desktop that also hosts the project's CI runners, and it could not be made idle: the one-minute load average when a run started was between 2.2 and 9.1, and about 3 for most. Four of the sixty runs were flagged by the judge as noisy and were repeated. Up to about 65,536 elements a figure repeats within 1% from one run to the next. From a million elements up single runs differ by 10 to 15%, more than their own confidence intervals, which is why these rows are means and carry the lowest and highest run in brackets. The `clip` rows they replace came from one pass at a load average of about 6 and read low at large sizes: 1.13x over naive C for float32 at 1,048,576 elements with bounds (-0.5, 0.5), where the mean of ten is 1.44x. The `clip` kernels are also timed on bounds (-0.5, 0.5) with the two kinds of data that hold negative zeros; those two tables match the random one and are left out of the table above, though the "all six" row includes them. [Issue 51](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/51) asks for a dedicated machine to take every figure on.
- **Unlocked.** The judge refuses to time a kernel whose `spec.py`, `naive.c` or judge differs from `main`, unless told otherwise. The specs and the judge changes are new in the same pull request as the assembly, so every single-pass run here needed `--unlocked` and the judge marks the results as untrusted. The rows marked † were run locked. Nothing is recorded under `results/` for that reason. Once this is merged, the same commands without the flag give results that can be recorded.
- **A second machine.** Every kernel was also run once on an Apple M4 Max (NumPy 2.5.5, Apple clang 21), locked and recorded, at a load average of 1.3 to 1.9. These are the first results under `results/`. The figures at 1,048,576 elements are beside the M1 Pro's in [README.md](../README.md#results). Against naive C most are within 20% of the M1 Pro's; the float `cumsum` kernels are about a third lower (5.27x and 3.99x against 7.83x and 5.48x). Against NumPy, `clip` falls by about half and `searchsorted_f64` with 1,000 needles from 6.40x to 1.48x, while their lead over the C changes little (7.60x against 8.83x for `searchsorted_f64`), so NumPy is what got faster for those calls.
- **How the kernels were written.** One agent per family of kernels, each in its own worktree, following the rule in [AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md): keep an edit only if `check` passes and `compare --against HEAD` reports it significantly faster. Each kernel's `attempts.md` lists every attempt, kept or not. Attempts labelled "local" or "private" there were timed outside the judge and are not judge results.

### x86-64

- **Machine.** Intel Core i5-14600KF (Raptor Lake Refresh), one performance core, Linux 7.0, NumPy 2.5.5, clang 21.1.8 for the naive C, GNU `as` 2.46 for the kernels. Native. A desktop session was running; the judge takes 300 samples of one millisecond per size and pins its thread, which is what makes that tolerable ([judge-design.md](judge-design.md#short-samples)).
- **Command.** `uv run judge run <kernel> --record`, **locked**, on `main` after every kernel was merged: one pass over the 23 kernels, one at a time, at a load average of 0.5 to 1.2, with every run passing its correctness check first. Each row is one such run, and its report is `results/<kernel>/x86_64-intel-r-core-tm-i5-14600kf.json`. The per-size tables in each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` under "Where it stands" are from the run after that kernel's last kept change, a few hours earlier on the same machine.
- **The pass agrees with the runs it replaces.** Against each kernel's earlier run, the figure over all of a kernel's inputs is within 5% for every kernel, over the naive C and over NumPy; the largest moves over the naive C are `clip_f32` (1.30x, from 1.25x) and `compress_f32` (2.62x, from 2.52x). For one kind of input the figure against NumPy moves by up to 12% for `clip_f32` (13.2x from 14.9x with a lower bound of zero, 11.0x from 10.0x on rounded data) and by less than 7% for every other kernel. At single sizes from 1,048,576 elements up, where the data no longer fits the second-level cache, single runs differ by up to 20% either way: over NumPy at 1,048,576, `cumsum_f32` read 11.2x before and 13.3x in this pass, `argmax_f64` 1.87x and 1.72x, and `sum_u8` 51.3x in the run in its log and 42.1x here. `searchsorted_f64` was recorded three times. The first pass had one size out of line (19.4 µs for 1,000 needles at 1,048,576 elements where five further runs gave 12.7 to 13.0), and the second was flagged by the judge as taken on a busy machine; the third is the one kept.
- **`interp_f64` was written unlocked.** Its reference on `main` did not match NumPy on x86-64, the correction was a pull request of its own, and the judge refuses a locked run of a kernel whose reference differs from `main`. Its row is from the locked pass on `main`; the unlocked run it replaces read 4.57x over the naive C and 6.96x over NumPy overall, against 4.46x and 6.75x locked.
- **How the kernels were written.** One agent, one family after another, in the order of the survey's x86-64 shortlist, under the rule in [AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md). Kernels with several variants (`argmax` and `argmin`, the two `searchsorted`, the two `clip`, the two `count_nonzero`) are generated from one source per family and were judged separately. The ARM64 kernels and logs were read for the idea and the proofs and not used as a template.
- **Outside the judge.** Where a path is not reached by the judge's correctness cases (stores past the caches from 24 MB of output, 512 searches in step from 262,144 elements, a `cumsum` chunk redone and followed by the fast path) or where the judge's data seldom lands (bin edges), a differential test against the reference C was written and run, and is described in the kernel's log. Micro-benchmarks outside the judge are labelled as such there and decided nothing: every kept change has a `compare` verdict or is a first version judged by `run`.
- **A calibration of the core** was taken with a small harness and is used throughout the logs: a `lea` with a scaled index takes two cycles; `vblendvpd` and `vpblendvb` run once per cycle; 256-bit compares, maxima and float adds run twice per cycle on two of the three vector ports, integer adds and logic three times; two 32-byte loads per cycle; one store per cycle unless two go to the same cache line; a 32-byte load that straddles two lines costs a cycle more and a store two; `vgatherqpd` on four elements can start every 2.2 cycles; `vdivpd` on four doubles takes eight cycles.

## What the judge had to learn

The two fixtures needed nothing beyond arrays and a size. The real kernels needed scalar arguments, several combinations of values for them, fixed-size buffers and buffers sized by a scalar, buffers modified in place, inputs that must be sorted, kernels without assembly, and a tolerance measured at a scale other than the result. These are described in [judge-design.md](judge-design.md#what-a-spec-can-express). All of them change the judge; its selftest passes with them.

## Results by family

### `argmax` and `argmin` on floats

#### The operation

- **What NumPy requires.** The index of the first element equal to the maximum (or minimum). A NaN wins over every number, and among several NaNs the first one wins.
- **The idea.** The position does not have to be tracked element by element. Reduce each block of elements to its maximum, remember which block held the best value so far, and search only that block for the position at the end. A NaN has to be noticed block by block, because it decides the result wherever it sits.

#### On ARM64

- **Technique.** One pass that reduces each block of elements (64 for float32, 32 for float64) to its maximum with `fmax`, and remembers which block held the best value so far with one scalar compare and a `csel`. No index vector is carried, and no branch depends on the data unless it holds a NaN. At the end only the winning block is scanned for the first position that equals the maximum. `fmax` returns NaN when either operand is NaN, so a block that holds one shows up in the same compare as unordered, and a scalar loop then finds the first NaN. Short arrays take straight-line code with no loop instead: 8 to 64 and 129 to 256 elements for float32, 4 to 64 for float64. Below those sizes a scalar loop runs.
- **Why it is this far ahead.** NumPy's loop is scalar for floats. The float32 kernel runs at about 0.03 ns per element in the first-level cache and 0.05 to 0.07 beyond it. On input without a NaN its speed does not depend on the data. A NaN ends the block loop early, but sends the straight-line sizes to a scalar scan of the whole array: 256 float32 elements take 12 ns without a NaN and 112 ns with one in the last position.
- **What did not help:** software prefetch (slower), aligning the loop, a vector search for the first NaN, cheaper reductions of a block to a scalar.

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **The opponent is NumPy's own AVX2 loop,** 0.083 ns per `float32` and 0.163 ns per `float64` at 1,048,576 elements, not a scalar loop. The compiler leaves the reference scalar, 0.77 ns per element, so the column to read is NumPy's.
- **Technique.** One pass that does not track positions. The extreme so far is kept in every lane of a register; a block of eight vectors is reduced with `vmaxps` into four accumulators that are never reset, so a lane of an accumulator is beyond that register exactly when the block holds a new extreme. One branch per block covers "a new extreme or a NaN" and is taken about as often as the logarithm of the number of blocks. When it is taken the block is looked at again: the first NaN ends the call, otherwise the block becomes the remembered one. The answer is the first element equal to the extreme in the remembered block: `vcmpeqps`, `vmovmskps`, `tzcnt`.
- **NaN is not free here.** `vmaxps` returns its second operand when either is a NaN, so a NaN can be lost. `vcmpunordps` of two vectors is true where either holds one, so four compares cover a block.
- **Result.** 1.9x over NumPy at 1,048,576 for all four kernels, 3x in the second-level cache, 1.3x from memory; 6.0x to 7.5x over the eleven sizes, most of that being NumPy's cost per call. 6.7x to 13.2x over the naive C.
- **Limit.** Seventeen instructions per block for the two ports that take a maximum or a compare: 0.030 ns per `float32`. Beyond 262,144 elements, the caches.
- **What differs for a caller.** The second look is at one block and is bounded, nothing outside the array is read whatever a concurrent writer does, and the result is always an index inside the array; [issue 56](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/56) does not arise. New: `vmaxps` and `vminps` set the invalid flag for any NaN operand, so these kernels leave it set after a call on an array with a NaN.
- **Not tried:** aligned loads and a prefetch, which is where a second run should start.

### Mask selection: `compress` and `nonzero`

#### The operation

- **What NumPy requires.** The selected elements (for `nonzero`, their positions) in their original order, and how many there are.
- **Why the simple loop is slow.** It asks "is this element selected?" before each store. On a mask with no pattern the processor guesses that answer wrong about half the time.
- **The idea.** Do not ask. Store at the current output position and move the position forward by the number selected, a block of elements per store. A mask that selects few elements is better served by finding the selected positions directly, so a kernel may have a path for each.
- **It relies on scratch output.** The stores write past the elements that are kept, inside the buffer's capacity. That is what `scratch=True` permits; the trade-offs are in [judge-design.md](judge-design.md#scratch-output-writing-inside-the-capacity).

#### On ARM64

- **Technique.** Dense masks are handled eight mask bytes at a time, and each kernel compacts differently. `compress_f32` packs the eight bytes into an index into a 256-entry table of shuffle patterns, and `tbl` moves the selected elements to the front of a block that is stored whole. `nonzero_bool` uses the same index into a table that lists the positions of the true bytes. `compress_f64` has no table and no `tbl`: it stores the elements in pairs, and the mask decides the address each pair is loaded from. In all three the write pointer advances by the number selected. Sparse masks: 64 mask bytes are reduced to a 64-bit mask and the set bits are extracted with `clz`, a fixed number at a time, so there is no data-dependent branch per element. The kernel switches between the two by measured density.
- **A weak spot the judge does not time.** Around 10% density the switch between the two paths mispredicts. Measured outside the judge, `nonzero_bool` costs 0.127 ns per element at 10% against 0.045 at 5% and 0.110 at 20%. The judge times 50% and 5%.
- **Limit.** With half the elements selected, `compress_f64` writes as fast as memory allows from 16,384 elements up.

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **Technique.** One `vpcmpeqb` against zero and one `vpmovmskb` turn 32 mask bytes into 32 bits; a mask byte is true when it is not zero, whatever its value. `compress`: each byte (`float32`) or four bits (`float64`) of that word indexes a table of permutations, `vpermps` or `vpermd` moves the selected elements to the front, all lanes are stored and `popcnt` advances the write position. `nonzero`: each byte indexes a table of the positions of its set bits, widened with `vpmovzxbq`.
- **A sparse block path, per block and with no state.** In `compress_f64` and `nonzero_bool` a block of 32 with at most four true elements skips the table: `tzcnt` and `blsr` take a fixed four positions out of the mask word. 1.24x and 1.40x overall, all of it at 5% selected. In `compress_f32` it was a wash and is not used.
- **There is no weak spot near 10% density.** The ARM64 kernels are slower than their dense path there. A micro-benchmark from 1% to 100% finds no density at which the per-block switch is slower than the table path alone, because that path is held by its stores and not by the front end. `compress_f32` has no switch at all.
- **Result.** Over NumPy at 1,048,576, half and 5% selected: `compress_f32` 32.6x and 12.6x, `compress_f64` 16.7x and 9.4x, `nonzero_bool` 3.2x and 19.6x. Over the naive C overall: 2.52x, 1.86x, 2.81x.
- **Limit.** Inside the first-level cache, the instruction count: 35 per 32 elements for `compress_f32` on a core that renames six per cycle. Beyond it, the cache lines of the output, each fetched before it is written and written back after: at 65,536 elements the same loop runs at first-level-cache speed once the store is removed.
- **What did not help:** aligning the loads of x, a prefetch.
- **Found on the way, and used by every later kernel:** a `lea` with a scaled index takes two cycles on this processor, so a write position that advances every step is kept as an element count and advanced with `add`.
- **What differs for a caller.** Mask bytes other than 0 and 1 are handled: any non-zero byte is true, so [issue 8](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/8) does not arise on x86-64.

### Masked assignment

#### The operation

- **What NumPy requires.** Selected elements become the value. NumPy's `x[mask] = v` does not touch the others.
- **Why the simple loop is slow.** It is a store under a condition, element by element. Compiled as a branch, it is guessed wrong about half the time on a mask with no pattern.
- **The idea.** Work on whole blocks. Either write the block back with the value blended in, which also writes the unselected elements with their own bits, or use a store that leaves them alone where the architecture has one. Which is available, and what it asks of the caller, is per architecture.

#### On ARM64

- **Technique.** Dense blocks: load 16 elements, blend with the value through `bsl` under a mask widened by `tbl`, store all 16. Sparse blocks: no load; one store per selected element at an address chosen without branching.
- **It rewrites elements it does not change.** A dense block stores unselected elements back with their own bits. The array must be writable throughout, and a write by another thread to an unselected element between the load and the store would be undone. NumPy's `x[mask] = v` does not touch unselected elements. This is why the compiler cannot do it and why it has to be a decision: see "Decisions".
- **The jump at 65,536 elements is naive C slowing down**, not the kernel speeding up. Below that size the branch predictor memorises the judge's repeated input and naive C looks better than it is.

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **Technique.** Dense blocks blend, as on ARM64: four elements are loaded, combined with the value by three logical instructions under a lane mask from a 16-entry table, and stored back. `vblendvpd` is not used: measured, it runs once per cycle here, and three logical instructions run three per cycle. A block of 32 with at most four selected elements is not blended: `tzcnt` and `blsr` find the positions and the value is stored to each, so no element is loaded and a cache line with no selected element is not touched (1.37x overall, 1.92x at 5% selected).
- **Result.** 18.4x over NumPy at 1,048,576 with half selected and 7.3x with 5%; 3.96x over the naive C overall.
- **The masked store, measured and not adopted.** `vmaskmovpd` writes only the selected elements, which would make decision 1 below unnecessary on x86-64. Against the blend with half selected it is 1.55x to 1.64x faster from 64 to 4,096 elements and 0.86x at 65,536 and 1,048,576: without the load nothing tells the processor which lines the stores are about to need. A candidate that removes a caveat is a person's to adopt: [issue 70](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/70).
- **What did not help:** lane masks read back from the block's stored byte mask; aligning the blocks.

### `isin` with eight test values

#### The operation

- **What NumPy requires.** One boolean per element: whether it equals any of the eight test values.
- **The work.** Done literally, eight compares per element. A kernel gets ahead of a compiler only where the test values allow something cheaper than comparing against each one.
- **The idea.** When the test values lie close together, an element can be narrowed to a small key and looked up in a table, with every value outside the window made unable to match.

#### On ARM64

- **Technique.** When the test values allow it, each element is narrowed to a 16-bit key (with saturation, so values outside the window can never match) and membership is four `tbl` lookups on the nibbles of the key, or three when every test value is below 4,096. They allow it when all eight lie between 0 and 65,534, or all lie within 32,767 of the first test value. Otherwise the kernel compares at 64 bits, eight compares per vector, which is what clang already does.
- **The speedup depends on the test values, and on their order.** For small or closely spaced test values the kernel is several times faster than naive C. Because the second window is centred on the first test value, the same eight values can be fast in one order and slow in another: eight values spanning 60,000 ran at 0.17 ns per element with a middle value first and 0.62 with the lowest first. For unrelated 64-bit values it ties naive C, and at 16 elements it is slower, because it has tried and rejected the narrow set-ups first. The judge's original distribution only ever took the fastest path, so a second one (`wide values`) was added to show the other.
- **Why the general case cannot be beaten:** eight 64-bit compares per vector of two elements is the work, the compiler emits exactly that, and there is no wider compare on this hardware.

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **The general case is settled the other way round from ARM64.** A compare of four 64-bit elements exists here, and the compiler already emits it: the naive C is vectorised, 0.30 ns per element for any values, 22x to 30x ahead of NumPy on its own. Written by hand the same loop gains 8%.
- **Narrow keys.** When every test value is from 0 to 32,767, sixteen elements are narrowed from 64 to 16 bits with three `vpackusdw` and compared with eight `vpcmpeqw`, where the wide compare needs eight for four elements: 0.64 cycles per element against the compiler's 1.6. A block that holds an element outside that range cannot be narrowed and is compared at 64 bits. The nibble tables of the ARM64 kernel were not needed: with sixteen keys per register, eight compares cost the same fifteen instructions.
- **Result.** On values and test values from 0 to 999: 1.95x over the naive C and 40x over NumPy at 1,048,576. On values spread over the int64 range: 1.08x and 3.8x.
- **A first version was 0.77x against the naive C** and was not committed: its window began at the smallest test value, so data spread over the same range as the test values sent nearly every block the slow way.
- **What differs for a caller.** The narrow path needs test values between 0 and 32,767. The ARM64 kernel also takes any eight values within 32,767 of the first one. Negative sets, and large values close together, get the wide path here.

### `searchsorted` and `interp`

#### The operation

- **What NumPy requires.** For each needle, the first position in the sorted array whose element is not less than it, with NaN ordered after every number. That is `side='left'`. With `side='right'` it is the first position whose element is greater than the needle, which differs only for a needle that equals an element (the position after the last equal one) and for a NaN needle (the length of the array). Each side is a kernel of its own. `interp` runs the same search over `xp` and interpolates between the two neighbours. Its result is bit-identical only if the division and the multiply-add are done the way NumPy's build for that architecture does them.
- **Why the simple loop is slow.** It runs one search at a time. Each probe waits for the compare before it, and on a large array each probe is a cache miss the next one queues behind.
- **The idea.** A branch-free binary search run for many needles at once. The step size depends only on the array length, so all searches stay in step and their cache misses overlap instead of queueing.

#### On ARM64

- **Two lengths.** `searchsorted_f64(a, v, out, n, needles)` and `interp_f64(x, xp, fp, out, n, queries)`: `n` is the length of the sorted array (of `xp` and `fp`), and the number of needles or queries is separate. The first selects the code path and the depth of the search; the second is how many times the loop runs.
- **Technique.** Every step loads one probe per needle, compares, and moves each needle's base by the compare mask. Sixteen needles at a time when the array has fewer than 64 elements, 64 at a time up to 1,048,576, and blocks of 128 beyond.
- **A path needs one full group of needles.** Leftover needles are covered by redoing the last full group, so a call with fewer needles than its path's group takes a narrower one: 64 to 127 needles against an array of 1,048,576 or more use the 64-needle path, and anything smaller is searched one needle at a time. That last case is correct and untuned: a handful of needles against a large array is mostly the cost of the call.
- **`interp`** runs the same search over `xp`, then the interpolation for two queries per vector with a real `fdiv` and a fused multiply-add, which is what NumPy's build uses. Any group whose result contains a NaN is redone by a line-by-line copy of the reference, which keeps the output bit-identical.
- **What limits it.** In cache, which elements are probed, not the instruction count: making every search probe the same elements ran 1.25x to 1.33x faster with identical instructions. Beyond the cache, the number of cache and TLB misses, which the kernel overlaps but cannot reduce.
- **The number of needles is verified for the values the specs list:** 0, 1, and the counts either side of 16, 64 and 128 (15, 16, 17, 63, 64, 65, 127, 128, 129), and 1,000. Fresh random cases draw from the same list.
- **Timed with 64 and with 1,000 needles per call**, across the sweep of array lengths. The summary table shows the rows for 1,000. Each figure is the mean of ten locked runs on a machine that was not fully quiet (load average about 3; see [How the numbers were taken](#how-the-numbers-were-taken)), with the lowest and highest of the ten in brackets:

  | Kernel | Needles | vs naive C | at 16 | at 4,096 | at 1,048,576 | at 16,777,216 | vs NumPy | at 1,048,576 |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | `searchsorted_f64` | 64 | 9.06x [8.89 – 9.27] | 5.24x | 9.59x | 10.2x | 12.2x | 5.20x | 2.34x |

  The sizes are lengths of the sorted array. With 64 needles a call is short enough that NumPy's fixed cost per call is part of its figure. The first figures published for these kernels, 8.84x and 22.7x over NumPy at 1,048,576 elements, were taken with as many needles as elements, a shape the judge no longer times.
- **The right side** is `searchsorted_right_f64`: this kernel with every `a[i] < key` turned into `a[i] <= key`, and the length of the array stored for a NaN needle where the left side searches for the first NaN. Nothing else differs, and from Python the two sides take the same time (2.23 ms and 2.22 ms for a million needles against 1,000 elements). Its rows in the table above are not comparable with the left side's against naive C: clang compiles the right side's reference with a branch per probe and the left side's without one, so the two baselines differ, by most where the judge's inputs are unpredictable.
- **Needles that equal an element are generated at every size.** They are the only input on which the two sides differ. The judge draws needles from the array, sorted and with repeated values, at every size it checks and in every scalar set ([judge-design.md](judge-design.md#values-drawn-from-another-argument)). Before it did, they came only from inputs of up to 5,000 elements, and a mutation run showed that a wrong comparison on the path for 1,048,576 elements or more passed the judge, for either side; the same mutations now fail it. `delivery check` searches arrays of 1,048,576 and 1,048,579 elements for needles drawn from the array as well, from Python. `interp_f64` draws its queries from its sample points the same way, up to the 65,537 points it is checked at.
- **Not verified by the judge:** `interp_f64` is checked up to 65,537 sample points (the NumPy oracle is too slow beyond), so its path from 1,048,576 was compared with the reference outside the judge, without guard pages. Neither kernel is run on unsorted input by the judge, where the result is as meaningless as NumPy's and the kernel is required only to stay inside its buffers. That holds by construction, and a one-off run of 2,952 calls against guard pages found no fault ([issue 40](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/40) asks the judge to check it).

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **Technique.** A search is a fixed number of steps with no branch on the data: `half = (len + 1) / 2; if a[lo + half - 1] < needle: lo += half; len -= half`. Every probe is inside the array whatever the data, so the kernels stay inside their buffers on unsorted input by construction. Four searches share one `vgatherqpd`. Up to 64 run in step, with their positions kept in the output buffer, which is one 64-bit slot per needle; 512 when the array does not fit the second-level cache.
- **How many in step is the finding.** The chain from one step of a search to its next, through a gather, is about thirty cycles on this processor, and a gather can start every two to three. Sixteen searches in four registers left most of that idle: going to 64 was 1.92x. Beyond the second-level cache 512 against 64 is 1.4x to 1.8x, because the number in step is the number of cache misses in flight.
- **NaN.** A NaN in the array compares false and is never passed. A NaN needle is detected as the needles are read, and only then is its answer found (the first NaN of the array for the left side, n for the right) and written over.
- **`interp`** runs the right-side search over `xp`, then the values as the reference's cases read backwards, each a blend that overrides the ones before, with a real `vdivpd` and four gathers per four queries.
- **The multiply and the add of `interp` are not fused here.** NumPy's x86-64 build rounds the product and then the sum: of 20,000 random two-point interpolations the two forms differ in 6,131, and `np.interp` equals the unfused one in all of them. The kernel does the same. The shared reference fused on every architecture and failed its own check on x86-64 ([issue 73](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/73)); it now rounds as NumPy's build for the architecture does. Results on the two architectures can differ in the last bit, as NumPy's own do.
- **Result.** With 1,000 needles, over NumPy at 1,048,576 elements: `searchsorted_f64` 6.2x, `searchsorted_right_f64` 6.6x, `interp_f64` 10.6x. Over the naive C overall: 5.7x, 5.5x, 4.5x. With 64 needles the judge repeats the same needles, the naive C's and NumPy's branches are learnt, and the kernels are 3x over NumPy at that size and level with it at 16,777,216.
- **What differs for a caller.** No padded short group: one to three needles are searched one at a time, four and more as a thousand are, so [issue 39](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/39) and [issue 67](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/67) do not arise in that form. No limit of 2^32 sample points in `interp`. The output buffer is working storage during the call. `vgatherqpd` is fast on this processor and slow on older AMD processors and on Intel processors with the gather mitigation; untested elsewhere.

### `histogram` and `bincount`

#### The operation

- **What NumPy does for `histogram`.** It computes a bin estimate by divide, multiply and truncate, then corrects it against the two neighbouring bin edges. A bit-identical kernel either performs those operations in the same order, each rounded on its own, or proves for the element in hand that neither correction can fire.
- **The proof.** An element whose position inside its bin is far enough from both edges needs no correction, provided the bin count and range pass a check made once per call. That check has to reject a product that overflowed. The bound was derived for the operations as one kernel performs them, so it is rechecked for every architecture; the ARM64 one is below and in its `kernel.s` header.
- **`bincount`.** One counter is loaded and stored per element, and that is the limit whatever surrounds it. Equal neighbouring values wait on each other unless they are counted into separate tables.

#### On ARM64

- **`histogram_f64`, the literal path.** NumPy computes a bin estimate by divide, multiply and truncate, then corrects it against the two neighbouring bin edges. The kernel can do those operations two elements per vector, in the same order and individually rounded, falling back to scalar code for a lane that is out of range, NaN or in the last bin. That takes 14 vector operations per two elements and about 0.88 ns per element, and nothing cheaper computes the same thing.
- **The path the kernel takes instead.** It computes the estimate exactly as NumPy does and skips the two edge corrections for an element whose position inside its bin is at least 2^-12 of a bin away from either edge: about 2,047 elements in 2,048. An error bound in the `kernel.s` header shows that neither correction can fire for such an element, provided the bin count and range pass a check made once per call; when they do not, the literal path is used. The check compares two products and has to reject one that overflowed: as first written it took two infinities for equal, so narrow ranges near the largest float64 values got the fast path where the bound is false, and elements near a bin edge could land in the neighbouring bin. Every other element goes to a scalar copy of the reference. Four vector operations per two elements, 0.69 ns per element, 1.30x [1.30 – 1.30] over the literal path.
- **Why it was held back, and what changed.** It replaces operations with a proof, and the proof depends on the bin count and range, of which the judge could test one combination. The spec now lists sixteen, and the kernel passes all of them. The judge's data seldom lands near a bin edge, so the evidence about edges is differential tests outside the judge: the author's against the reference (410 million elements), and one against `np.histogram` itself with values on and within three ulps of NumPy's edges and around the 2^-12 threshold (430,000 rounds, 126 million elements, no mismatch; a histogram without the corrections fails 2,086 of 3,000 rounds of the same inputs). Neither reached the top of the float64 range, which is where the overflow above was found in review, by a third test of the same kind. With the check corrected, 6,000 ranges of magnitude 2^960 and up, 636 of them with both products infinite, give no mismatch over 1.6 billion elements; before, 28 of 3,000 did.
- **`bincount_u8`** is limited by one counter load and store per element. Reading eight input bytes per load and, for large arrays, counting into four tables on the stack so that equal neighbouring bytes do not wait on each other, is worth 1.24x. A 65,536-entry pair table and `dc zva` for zeroing were slower.

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **`histogram_f64`: no divide per element.** A divide of four doubles takes eight cycles on this processor, so a kernel that computes the reference's estimate as the reference does cannot go below two cycles per element. The proof never needed that estimate, only one within `4 u N` of the real position. The kernel computes `(v - lo) * S` with `S = nbins / width` taken once, which is within `3.01 u N`.
- **Fixed point through `vcvttpd2dq`.** `S` is scaled by 4,096, so the conversion gives the bin in the upper bits and twelve bits of fraction below. An element is accepted when the fraction bits are neither all zero nor all one, which is the ARM64 kernel's 2^-12 on either side of an edge, and when the whole number is within the limit as an unsigned number: a NaN and anything too large convert to 0x80000000 and fail that test with no test of their own. Everything else, and every call whose bin count and range fail the once-per-call check, goes through a scalar copy of the reference.
- **The last bin is on the fast path.** The reference never takes its last correction there, so only the lower edge has to be certain. With one or two bins the ARM64 kernel sends all or half of the elements to its scalar route.
- **Evidence.** The proof is in the `kernel.s` header. A differential test outside the judge against the reference, with elements on bin edges, within three units in the last place of them, on the 2^-12 threshold, at `lo` and `hi`, and ranges up to the top of float64: 60,000 rounds, 213 million elements, no mismatch. A copy of the kernel without the edge test fails 2,264 of 3,000 rounds of it.
- **Result.** 7.5x over NumPy at 1,048,576, 6.98x over the naive C overall: 0.42 to 0.60 ns per element against 3.9.
- **Limit.** The three vector ports: eleven vector instructions per four elements before the bins are taken out, four more to take them out, then four counter updates.
- **`bincount_u8`.** One counter update per byte at one store per cycle is the limit, and the compiler's loop is at it: 1.04x to 1.2x over the naive C on the judge's uniformly random bytes, depending on the run, because the naive C's own time moved by up to 2x at single sizes. Byte i is counted in table i mod 4 from 32,768 bytes, which is what pays on bytes that repeat: all bytes equal, 0.35 ns per byte against 1.39.
- **What did not help:** two or four count tables for `histogram_f64`.
- **What differs for a caller.** From 2^19 bins `histogram_f64` runs at the speed of the naive C (ARM64: 2^31).

### `cumsum`

#### The operation

- **Integers are exact in any order.** So the additions can be regrouped: prefix sums inside each block are built from the inputs alone, and the running total is added to a whole block at a time, so the chain from one block to the next is one add per block instead of one per element.
- **Floats cannot be both exact and fast.** Bit-identical output requires NumPy's order of additions, each depending on the one before, so n elements take n times the latency of one floating-point add. No exact kernel can beat that, on any processor.
- **So float `cumsum` is in tolerance mode**, as [survey-design.md](survey-design.md) decided.
- **Infinities and NaN must still land where NumPy has them.** A regrouped sum can overflow where the sequential one does not, and can return to a finite value where the sequential one stays infinite. A kernel has to prove, for each stretch of output, that neither happened, and redo the stretch in NumPy's order when it cannot.
- **The tolerance needed a scale.** The judge's tolerance was measured at the size of the result. A regrouped running sum over data that cancels differs from NumPy's by millions of such units while being exactly as accurate, because a small result carries the rounding error of its large terms. The allowed error of element i is now 4 units in the last place of `(i + 1) * (|x[0]| + ... + |x[i]|)`, the standard bound for adding in any order.

#### On ARM64

- **Integers.** In cache the scheme runs at about 0.13 ns per element; from 4,194,304 elements it runs at the speed of a plain copy.
- **The exact float chain, measured.** The latency of one floating-point add is 0.93 ns here. Naive C and an exact assembly kernel both measured 0.95 to 0.97 ns per element from 4,096 elements up. The first version of these specs was exact by mistake and produced that tie; the specs now declare a tolerance.
- **The float kernels** use the integer scheme, with two running sums per block for float64 and four for float32, and are limited by the number of vector operations per block, not by the latency of an add.
- **Infinities and NaN still land where NumPy has them.** A regrouped sum can overflow where the sequential one does not, and can return to a finite value where the sequential one stays infinite. The kernel works in chunks of 1,024 elements and accumulates the square of every output; a finite sum of squares proves every output of the chunk was finite and well below the overflow threshold, so the sequential sum cannot have overflowed either. A chunk that fails the test is redone one element at a time in NumPy's order. The argument is in each `kernel.s` header.
- **Accuracy, measured outside the judge** against an exactly computed sum over 80,303 inputs per kernel: the kernels are at least as accurate as NumPy's sequential sum in most classes tried. On all-equal values, the worst case for a sequential sum, NumPy's error was 7 to 16 times the kernel's. They are less accurate in two. Where cancellation makes every one of NumPy's partial sums exact, NumPy's error is zero and the kernel's is not: on the repeating float32 pattern `-2^24 + 2, 1, 2^24, 1` the kernel loses 1 per repetition, 250,000 at a million elements where the true sum is 1,000,000, and float64 does the same with 2^53. And on magnitudes that grow quickly the kernel's error was up to about 3 times NumPy's in review, at most about 2.5 units in the last place. Both are inside the declared tolerance; small integers stored as floats, where any order of additions is exact, come out exact.
- **Costs of the scheme.** Running sums above about 1e153 (float64) or 4e18 (float32) fail the squares test and run at the speed of naive C, with correct results. Fewer than 32 (float64) or 20 (float32) elements use the exact sequential chain. Float32 arrays of 2^29 elements or more use the sequential loop throughout, because the proof does not hold there.

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **The ceiling is lower here, and it was measured first.** With the data in the second-level cache a plain copy loop takes 0.115 ns per `int64` and the naive C 0.19, one cycle per element. So no integer running sum can beat the compiler's by more than 1.65x beyond the first-level cache. The float chains cost two cycles per element, which leaves room.
- **Technique.** The ARM64 regrouping. What one output vector adds to the one before it is built from the input alone: the input plus an overlapping load one element back, then one `vperm2f128` for the shift across the halves of the register, so the chain from vector to vector is one add. `float32` builds its window of eight with two more overlapping loads. `cumsum_i64` also has a 16-byte version, used from 512 to 131,071 elements: its accesses rarely straddle a cache line, and a 32-byte access that does costs a cycle or two.
- **The proof, with the design's candidate.** Non-finite values land where NumPy has them by the ARM64 kernels' argument, chunk by chunk. The test is a compare of magnitudes in place of a sum of squares: `vpand` and `vpmaxud` on the bit patterns per vector, one compare per chunk. For `float64` that lifts the fast path's limit from running sums of about 1e154 to 2^1023; for `float32` the limit stays 2^64, because the proof for `float32` needs it. The test raises no floating-point flag of its own.
- **Loads ahead of the stores.** The first `cumsum_f64` lost 2.5x at 262,144 and 1,048,576 elements with the output 16 bytes behind the input. Loading the next vector's input before storing the current output removed it, and keeps `cumsum_i64` right when the output is the input.
- **Stores past the caches from 24 MB of output:** 1.2x to 1.26x at 4,194,304 and 16,777,216 elements.
- **Result.** Over NumPy at 1,048,576: `int64` 5.3x, `float64` 6.1x, `float32` 13.3x, a third to a half of the ARM64 figures, as the design expected. Over the naive C overall: 1.18x, 1.81x, 2.96x.
- **Evidence beyond the judge.** 20,990 runs per float kernel against the left-to-right sum with infinities, NaN, values near the largest finite one and near the fast path's limit, at sizes that reach the cache-bypassing stores: positions of non-finite values exact, finite values within the tolerance. `cumsum_i64`: every path, and in place.
- **What did not help:** aligning the stores as a general step (the judge puts the output 16 bytes after the input within a cache line, so one of the two is always off); adds done as fused multiply-adds by one to use a third port.

### `sum`, `count_nonzero`, `clip`, `astype`

#### The operation

- **`sum` on `uint8`.** The result is a 64-bit sum, so the bytes have to be widened somewhere. Where, and how often, is the whole design.
- **`count_nonzero` on floats.** A NaN counts as non-zero and both zeros count as zero.
- **`clip`.** NumPy's result is what compare-and-select gives, `v < lo ? lo : (v > hi ? hi : v)`: an element equal to a bound keeps its own sign of zero, and a NaN passes through. A shorter sequence built from a minimum and a maximum is usable only where it gives the same bits, and that depends on how the architecture's minimum and maximum treat zeros and NaN. The reference is defined for ordered bounds only; see [below](#where-the-result-is-narrower-than-the-number).

#### On ARM64

These were expected to ship as C. Five ship as assembly.

- **`sum_u8`.** clang widens every byte to 64 bits before adding: 24 vector instructions per 32 bytes. The kernel adds bytes pairwise into 16-bit accumulators with `uadalp`, sixteen accumulators per step, and widens every 128 steps. In cache it runs at three 16-byte loads per cycle, which is the core's limit.
- **`count_nonzero_i64`.** A 64-bit element is non-zero exactly when the larger of its two halves is, so three levels of `umaxp` reduce eight vectors to sixteen bytes before one test and one accumulate: nine vector instructions per eight vectors against clang's sixteen. From 65,536 to 1,048,576 elements both run at the rate the second-level cache delivers and tie.
- **`count_nonzero_f64`.** One bit test against a mask that ignores the sign handles NaN and both zeros, then the same narrowing.
- **`clip`.** Compare-and-select needs four vector operations per vector and clang is already there. `fmin(fmax(x, lo), hi)` needs two, but differs from NumPy on signed zeros at the bounds and on signalling NaNs, so the kernel takes it only when the bounds rule the first out and detects the second afterwards. For a lower bound of +0.0, the bound `np.clip(x, 0, 1)` has, the only element the two operations get wrong is -0.0, so a third operation per vector watches for one and a chunk that held one is done again by compare and select. See the caveats below.
- **`cast_u8_f32` stays C.** clang's loop is already at the limit of one 16-byte store per cycle. The assembly gained at 16 and 64 elements (no entry checks, no scalar remainder) and at 16,777,216 (non-temporal loads), and tied at the eight sizes between: 1.05x [0.99 – 1.08] overall in one run and 1.06x [1.00 – 1.07] in the next. That is a tie, and the design ships C on a tie. The assembly is in the git history and its attempts log is kept.

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **`sum_u8`.** `vpsadbw` against zeros adds 32 bytes into four 64-bit sums, on one port; mixed with `vpmaddubsw` on the other two the loop reaches 45 bytes per cycle. Aligned loads were worth 1.5x beyond the first-level cache, and a prefetch and four streams, both rejected on the M1, pay here. 6.15x over the naive C, 51x over NumPy at 1,048,576.
- **`count_nonzero`.** A compare with zero and a subtract into an accumulator, two instructions per four elements, loads aligned from 64 elements. The compiler vectorises this loop and both are at memory's speed from about a thousand elements: 1.21x over the naive C for `int64` and 1.06x for `float64`; 1.26x and 4.4x over NumPy at 1,048,576.
- **`clip`: two instructions, one path, none of the ARM64 caveats.** `vmaxps a, b` computes `a > b ? a : b`: it returns its second operand when the two are unordered or equal, zeros of either sign included. With the bound first, `vmaxps lo, v` then `vminps hi, t` is the reference bit for bit for every pair of bounds with `lo <= hi`. No path depends on the bounds or the data, no status register is read, and the output may be the input. The compiler does not emit this: it emits a minimum, a compare and a blend that runs once per cycle, which is why the assembly is 1.25x over it for `float32` and 1.08x for `float64`. With the loads a block ahead of the stores there is no stall with the output just behind the input, where the survey found NumPy 8x slower. Level with NumPy at 1,048,576, 1.7x at 65,536, 2.0x at 16,777,216.
- **`cast_u8_f32`.** `vpmovzxbd` from memory and `vcvtdq2ps`, with aligned stores and stores past the caches from 24 MB. The naive C ties NumPy, as the design expected. The assembly is 1.21x over the C: level with NumPy from 16,384 to 1,048,576 elements and 3.39x at 16,777,216.
- **The invalid flag.** `vmaxps` and `vminps` set it for any NaN operand, quiet or signalling. The binding's clearing of flags after `clip` is needed on x86-64 as on ARM64.

### Second shortlist

Kernels from [second-shortlist-design.md](second-shortlist-design.md), which took its candidates from the NumPy calls of a program in use. They are written for x86-64 first; on ARM64 each ships as its naive C until it has assembly there.

#### `cumsum` on booleans

`np.cumsum(mask)` gives a running count of the True elements as int64. NumPy converts the mask to int64 in chunks and adds: 1.9 ns per element at 4,096 and 3.5 at 65,536 on the Intel Core i5-14600KF. The naive C is the scalar chain at one cycle per element, 0.19 ns.

- **Technique (x86-64).** Eight elements are one step. Their eight mask bits index a table of 256 entries, each the running count within the group as eight bytes; `vpmovzxbq` from memory widens the two halves to four 64-bit lanes each. A second table holds the group's total, loaded into every lane with `vpbroadcastq`. With T the count before the group, the two output vectors are the widened halves plus T and the next T is T plus the total: a chain of one add per eight elements. The bits of 32 elements come from one `vpmovmskb`.
- **It writes eight bytes for every byte it reads,** so the stores decide. Up to three elements go one at a time first, until the output is a multiple of 32 bytes and no store straddles two cache lines; the mask being a byte per element, any number can. Inside the first-level cache it runs at 0.062 ns per element, 3.0x over the naive C. Once the output is in the second-level cache it runs at 0.10 ns per element whatever the arithmetic: 1.9x.
- **Stores past the caches from 24 MB of output** are worth 2.6x at 4,194,304 and 16,777,216 elements, far more than for `cumsum_i64` (1.25x): here nearly all the memory traffic is the output, and an ordinary store reads each line before overwriting it.
- **At 1,048,576 elements the judge's first table is misleading.** It shows the kernel 0.75x of the naive C. The same run's second table, and a micro-benchmark of the two alone, have the kernel 1.17x faster there (174 µs against 203 µs). The first table times NumPy between them, and NumPy allocates 8 MB for its result on every call, which pushes the kernel's own output out of the third-level cache just before the kernel is timed.
- **`pext` in one place:** a group of eight outside a block of 32 gets its bits from an 8-byte load and `pext`, which is fast on Intel and on AMD from Zen 3 and slow on earlier AMD processors. Blocks do not use it.
- **What a caller must know:** every mask byte is 0 or 1. The spec says so, and the kernel uses the bytes as numbers. The binding reads the mask for other bytes first.
- **From Python,** `np.cumsum(mask)` is 3.1x faster at 16 elements, 9.7x at 4,096 and 9.5x at 1,048,576 with half the mask True (recorded on a quiet machine at the top of the stack: 3.0x, 9.6x and 13.9x). At 16 elements nearly all of the 338 ns left is `np.cumsum`'s own Python wrapper, which the method hook sits below.

#### `np.where(c, a, b)`

`np.where(c, a, b)` with a boolean condition takes each element from `a` where the condition is True and from `b` where it is not. NumPy branches on every element: 2.7 ns per element at 65,536 with half the condition True, 0.54 ns with 95% True, on the Intel Core i5-14600KF. The naive C, which clang vectorises four elements wide, takes 0.21 to 0.24 ns from 1,024 to 65,536. The kernel serves int64 as well as float64: it only moves 64-bit elements.

- **Technique (x86-64).** Sixteen elements are one block. Their sixteen condition bytes are loaded once into both halves of a register and compared with zero, and one `vpshufb` per group of four spreads four of those bytes over four 64-bit lanes, giving the lane masks. A group's output is (mask and `b`) or (`a` and not mask): no floating-point instruction touches the data, so a NaN keeps its payload and its signalling bit and a zero its sign. The elements left after the whole blocks are one more block that ends at the end of the buffers, writing some elements a second time with the same values. From 32 elements one group of four is done where the output is and the blocks start where it is at a multiple of 32 bytes, so no store straddles two cache lines. The blocks ask for the cache lines 512 bytes ahead while those are inside the buffers.
- **Every element is 17 bytes read and 8 written,** so beyond the first-level cache the kernel moves memory. 1.4x to 1.65x over the naive C from 16 to 65,536 elements, where the data comes from the second-level cache; level with it at 262,144, in the third-level cache; and 1.2x to 1.47x from 1,048,576 up. From 3,145,728 elements, 24 MB of output, the output is stored past the caches. The two timing distributions take the same code; where they differ, the buffers were placed differently.
- **Two attempts were left for a person to decide** ([attempts.md](../kernels/where_f64/x86_64/attempts.md), 6 and 9): a select by a masked store, half a percent faster but with an instruction that is slow on older AMD processors, and stores past the caches from 762,600 elements, where everything the call moves fills the third-level cache rather than its output alone. The second is 1.36x to 1.46x faster at 1,048,576 elements, and leaves the result in no cache, unlike the rule the other kernels follow.
- **What a caller must know:** the output must not overlap the condition, `a` or `b`. The x86-64 kernel takes any non-zero condition byte as True, as NumPy does, but the spec promises only 0 and 1 and ARM64 runs the naive C, so the binding reads the condition for other bytes first. The binding writes into a new array, so the output cannot overlap. A scalar side, as in `np.where(c, a, np.nan)`, is spread over a buffer of 256 elements on the stack and the kernel runs a block of 256 at a time: such a call never stores past the caches.
- **From Python,** at a load average of about 3.3 (other judges were running), `np.where(c, a, b)` on float64 with half the condition True is 4.7x faster at 16 elements, 3.2x at 4,096 and 6.0x at 1,048,576; `np.where(c, a, np.nan)` with 95% True 4.4x, 3.0x and 2.5x. A first version of the binding spread the scalar over a buffer of all n elements, from `malloc` above 256: at 1,048,576 elements that was 1.01x, writing and reading back 8 MB more than the call needs.
- **The judge's run was on a busy machine** (load average 4.6): the sizes from 1,048,576 up share the third-level cache and memory with other work.

#### Running maxima: `np.maximum.accumulate` and `np.fmax.accumulate`

`np.maximum.accumulate(x)` gives each element the largest of the elements up to it; `np.fmax.accumulate(x)` the same, ignoring NaN. On the Intel Core i5-14600KF NumPy takes 1.5 ns per element for int64 and 1.8 to 2.3 for float64. The naive C is very different for the two. For int64 clang writes a compare and a branch per element, which costs 0.15 ns while the branches are predicted, as they are on data the judge repeats; for float64 it keeps a chain through the running value, 2.0 ns per element. Two kernels serve three calls: for integers `np.maximum` and `np.fmax` are one function, and both go to `cummax_i64`.

- **Technique, `cummax_i64` (x86-64).** AVX2 has no 64-bit integer maximum, and one built from `vpcmpgtq` and a blend costs more than the naive C's predicted branch. So the kernel avoids the maximum where it can see there is nothing to compute. In blocks of 16 elements, with M the maximum so far, a block is *flat* when no element is above M (one compare per vector; the block is M sixteen times), *rising* when no element is below the one before it and the element before the block is M (one compare per vector against the same load one element back; the block is a copy), and otherwise done four elements at a time in the integer registers with compares and conditional moves, the chain from group to group one compare and one move. Which way the next block is tried depends on how the last one went; no path has a branch that depends on a single element.
- **Technique, `cumfmax_f64` (x86-64).** `vmaxpd a, b` keeps b when the two are equal, which is NumPy's rule for zeros (the later of `+0.0` and `-0.0`), but returns a NaN b. So every element first goes through `vmaxpd` against minus infinity, which turns a NaN into minus infinity and changes nothing else; once the running value is a number that changes no output. The elements before the first number go one at a time. Then blocks of eight: the running maximum inside each vector in two steps, the second vector against the first's last lane, and both against the running value. The chain from block to block is one `vmaxpd` per eight elements, and the next block is loaded before this one is stored.
- **Both** bring the output to a multiple of 32 bytes first, so no store straddles two cache lines, and store past the caches from 24 MB of output.
- **What limits them.** `cummax_i64` runs at the speed of a copy in the second-level cache, 1.1x to 1.35x over the naive C up to 65,536 elements, level with it in the third-level cache, and 1.4x to 1.5x in memory, with a third less traffic. On data whose maxima a branch predictor cannot learn the naive C is far slower than the judge shows: 2.6 ns per element against the kernel's 0.72 with a new maximum at a random half of 2,000,000 elements ([attempts.md](../kernels/cummax_i64/x86_64/attempts.md)). The worst found is the other way: on 1 0 2 1 3 2 ..., which the branches predict, the kernel is 0.7x of the naive C. `cumfmax_f64` is held by its arithmetic, ten `vmaxpd` and six shuffles per block: 0.14 to 0.15 ns per element from 64 to 65,536 elements on every distribution, 13x to 14x over the naive C, 4x to 4.4x in memory.
- **At 1,048,576 elements the first table is misleading for `cummax_i64`**, as for `cumsum_bool`: 0.98x and 0.92x of the naive C there, where NumPy, timed between them, allocates 8 MB for its result on every call. The same run's second table has the kernel at 432 µs with the output 16 bytes behind the input and the naive C at 1.30 ms.
- **What a caller must know:** `out` must not overlap `x`, for both. `cumfmax_f64` must not see a signalling NaN. NumPy, through the C library's `fmax`, answers one with a quiet NaN and forgets the maximum so far; the kernel ignores it as it ignores any NaN. The spec leaves signalling NaNs out, and the binding reads a float64 array for them first: one that holds any goes to NumPy. That read costs about 0.05 ns per element on this processor, a third of the kernel's own 0.15. It took 0.17 until the binding of `np.fmax.reduceat` found a NaN by comparing it with itself, compiled for AVX2, in place of comparing magnitudes as integers with SSE2, which has no 64-bit compare. `np.maximum.accumulate` on floats propagates NaN, which neither kernel does, and stays with NumPy. For two elements NumPy computes the pair with its operands the other way round (the earlier of two zeros, the later of two NaNs); the spec and the kernel follow it.
- **From Python,** `uv run delivery time --only accumulate` at a load average of about 1: `np.maximum.accumulate(x)` on int64 is 2.6x faster at 16 elements, 11.6x at 4,096 and 6.6x at 1,048,576 (5.3x in the recorded run on a quiet machine); `np.fmax.accumulate(x)` on float64 2.8x, 7.8x and 5.2x (5.8x and 4.6x at the two larger sizes with the first read for signalling NaNs). At 16 elements most of what is left is the method call; NumPy takes about 220 ns there whatever the size. The float64 figures include the read for signalling NaNs, about a quarter of the 0.94 µs at 4,096 elements (the judge has the kernel alone at 0.59 µs). The flags the kernel's `vmaxpd` sets for a NaN are cleared after it, as after `clip`, since NumPy's own loop leaves none set; the first version of the binding did not, and `delivery check` now has cases that read the invalid flag after the call. Other ufuncs' `accumulate` pass through the hook after two pointer comparisons: `np.add.accumulate(x)` on int64 within 2% of NumPy at every size.
- **The judge's runs were on a quiet machine** (load average about 1).

#### Maximum by runs: `np.fmax.reduceat`

`np.fmax.reduceat(x, starts)` gives, for each start, the NaN-ignoring maximum of the run from it to the next start. NumPy calls its inner loop once per run, at about 13 ns a run on data without NaN and 50 ns with 5% NaN, so on runs of 16 elements it takes 0.8 to 3.4 ns per element whatever the array's size. The kernel, `fmax_reduce_f64`, is the maximum of one run (`np.fmax.reduce(x)`, n at least 1), and the binding calls it once per run: the judge cannot describe a kernel whose second buffer holds indices into the first, and a call costs a few nanoseconds against a run of 16. NumPy gives a run of `reduceat` the bits `np.fmax.reduce` gives the same elements.

- **What NumPy returns is fixed by its loop in two cases.** The value of a maximum does not depend on the order of the elements; the sign of a zero maximum when zeros of both signs are present does, and so does which NaN a run of NaNs returns. NumPy's AVX2 loop for this reduction has four running maxima, one per lane, all starting at `x[0]`, takes the elements from `x[1]` four at a time, combines the lanes, and takes the `(n - 1) mod 4` leftover elements one at a time through the C library's `fmax`. In a lane the later of two equal values is kept, among the leftover elements the earlier. The spec and the naive reference reproduce that loop, found by experiment on this machine ([attempts.md](../kernels/fmax_reduce_f64/x86_64/attempts.md)), and the kernel matches it. **A NumPy that runs its AVX-512 loop, with eight lanes, answers those two cases differently, and NumPy picks the loop when it starts, by the processor.** So the binding does not rely on the kernel there: when `x` holds a negative zero and a result is a zero, or holds NaNs of more than one bit pattern and a result is a NaN, the whole call goes to NumPy. Everywhere else every correct maximum is one bit pattern, and the result is NumPy's whichever loop it runs.
- **Technique (x86-64).** Two ways through the array. The quick way, from five elements, takes the elements in any order, some twice: `vmaxpd v, m` returns m when v is a NaN, so with the maximum so far as m and minus infinity to start, one instruction per vector ignores NaN, and the bits of every element are or-ed together beside it. Its result is NumPy's unless it is a zero and some element has its sign bit set, or it is minus infinity (perhaps a run of NaNs); then NumPy's way, the four lanes, is taken from the start. 5 to 16 elements are four loads, two at the ends and two between; 17 to 127 blocks of four vectors, the last ending with the array; from 128 blocks of eight vectors into eight maxima, placed on 32-byte boundaries after the first. Up to four elements a masked load (`vmaskmovpd`) and a reduction keeping the earlier of equal values. None of these paths branches on n within its range, which matters when every call has another length.
- **What limits it.** Up to 16 elements the count of instructions, about 30: 1.5 ns. From 128 to 4,096 elements the ports, 16 `vmaxpd` and `vorpd` per 64 elements, 0.033 ns per element; 0.040 in the second-level cache, where NumPy is at 0.10. Data whose maximum is a zero with a sign bit somewhere, and runs of NaN, take both ways: twice the time. 3.4x over the naive C at 16 elements, 13x to 14x from 1,024 to 4,096, 2.2x in memory; 6.63x across the judge's sizes.
- **The binding.** The starts are read once each, in the loop over the runs, and tested there against `[0, n)`; one outside sends the call to NumPy, which raises. A run of one element, and a start not below the next, is copied without a call. The array is read for signalling NaNs (below) 2,048 elements at a time, just ahead of the runs that use them, so the kernel then reads them from the first-level cache; reading the whole array first made runs of 1,024 in 262,144 elements 0.6x of NumPy. The two other guards read the results, one per run, and read the array again only if a result is a zero or a NaN.
- **What a caller must know.** n at least 1. No signalling NaN: NumPy's leftover elements go through the C library's `fmax`, which answers one with a quiet NaN, and the kernel skips it; the binding sends an array that holds one to NumPy. The invalid-operation flag is set after any NaN, quiet ones included, and the binding clears MXCSR's flags after the runs. The masked load is fast on this processor and slow on some other x86-64 processors, and slow anywhere when a lane it masks off lies in a page that cannot be read (an array of at most three elements ending within 24 bytes of unmapped memory).
- **From Python,** `uv run delivery time --only reduceat` at a load average of about 0.5, on runs of 16 with 5% NaN: 2.7x faster at 16 elements (one run, 350 ns to 132 ns), 12.7x at 4,096 and 10.9x at 1,048,576. NumPy is slow on NaN here; on the same runs without NaN, measured the same way outside the tool, 2.9x at 4,096 and 2.2x at 1,048,576. The gain falls with the length of the runs: at 4,096 elements 2.2x on runs of 64 and 1.5x on runs of 1,024; at 262,144 elements 1.7x on runs of 64, level on runs of 512, and 0.8x on runs of 4,096, where NumPy reads the array once at 0.1 ns per element and the binding reads it once for signalling NaNs and once more from the first-level cache. `np.add.reduceat` passes through the hook after one pointer comparison, within 1.3% of NumPy.
- **The judge's run was on a quiet machine** (load average about 0.9).

#### Any by rows: `m.any(axis=1)`

`m.any(axis=1)` says for each row of a two-dimensional boolean array whether it holds a True. NumPy pays a cost per row: on rows of 22 it takes 0.6 to 0.7 ns per element at every size on the Intel Core i5-14600KF, and 700 ns for a call with no row at all. The kernel, `any_rows_bool`, takes the matrix as `n` elements and a row width `cols`, writes one result per whole row and returns how many rows there were; the judge needed no new shape for it, the width being a scalar argument with a set of values the judge sweeps. The naive C is clang's loop over a row, vectorised 32 bytes wide, which pays its entry and exit on every row: 0.082 ns per element on rows of 22, 0.20 on rows of 4.

- **Technique (x86-64).** No division: a row is taken while `cols` elements are left, and the count returned is how far the output has advanced. The path is chosen once per call by the width. Rows of 1 to 32: one 32-byte load at the row's start, `vptest` against a vector whose first `cols` bytes are set, and `setnz` to the output, eight rows per step; the rows that start in the last 31 bytes are read from the bits of the last 32 bytes (`vpmovmskb`), and a matrix of fewer than 32 elements is gathered a byte at a time. Rows of 4 have a path of their own: a row is a 32-bit lane, and four vectors of rows are packed to one of results by three packs with signed saturation and a minimum with 1, each vector put together from two 16-byte loads 64 bytes apart so that no permute is needed. Rows of 33 to 64: the row's first 32 bytes or'ed with its last 32, four rows per step. Longer rows: 32-byte chunks or'ed together, the last ending at the row's end.
- **What limits it.** Rows of up to 32: one `vptest` per row, which needs port 0, and one `setnz` per row, which needs port 0 or 6 as the loop's branch does; 0.26 ns per row on rows of 22 in the first-level cache, about 1.4 cycles. Two ways around one flag per row (two rows per `vptest` through the zero and carry flags, and byte sums with `pext` and `pdep`) cost more vector instructions than they saved. Rows of 4 run at about four cycles per 128 elements: eight loads, or three packs on port 5. Over the naive C: 5.0x on rows of 22, 17.6x on rows of 4 and 3.8x on rows of 64 across the judge's sizes, 6.94x over all six tables. Rows of 1, 2 and 8, and narrow odd widths such as 3, 5 and 7, have no path of their own and take the one of rows up to 32, at about 1.4 cycles per row whatever the width: still far ahead of NumPy, but the judge times only widths of 22, 4 and 64, so a path for them could not be kept by `compare`. The kept attempt for rows of 4 that packs without a compare (attempt 6 in [attempts.md](../kernels/any_rows_bool/x86_64/attempts.md)) is a small gain, 1.002x, whose verdict was taken twice.
- **The binding.** Two hooks reach it, because NumPy routes the two spellings differently: the method hook of `any` takes `m.any(axis=1)`, and a function hook takes `np.any(m, axis=1)`, which for a plain `ndarray` calls `np.logical_or.reduce` itself and never reaches the method. `m.any()` with no argument, one of the commonest NumPy calls, goes to NumPy at the method hook's first test. The matrix is read for bytes other than 0 and 1 before the kernel sees it, 16,384 bytes at a time, each block just before the kernel reads it from the first-level cache; the check is compiled for AVX2 (the rule of `hk_has_signalling_nan`). It costs about 0.007 ns per byte where the kernel costs about 0.012 on rows of 22: from Python, `m.any(axis=1)` on a million elements takes 18.4 µs with it and 11.5 µs without it. It is bound by reading the second-level cache: a version with four accumulators of 32 bytes in place of the one the compiler makes was under 2% faster, and was not kept.
- **What a caller must know.** `cols` at least 1; `out` does not overlap `m`. Every mask byte is 0 or 1, and on x86-64 this is required, not a formality: a byte above 1 counts as True on every path but one, the path of fewer than 32 elements with a width other than 4, where the bits are gathered by shifting the bytes themselves and a byte above 1 spills into the bits of the rows beside it. The binding sends a matrix holding such a byte to NumPy.
- **From Python,** `uv run delivery time --only any` at a load average of about 0.4, on rows of 22 with 5% of the elements True: `m.any(axis=1)` is 5.1x faster at 16 elements (one row of 22, 510 ns to 99 ns), 16.2x at 4,096 and 42.2x at 1,048,576; `np.any(m, axis=1)` 8.0x, 16.8x and 42.2x. `m.any()` on a one-dimensional array passes through the hook 4 to 5 ns slower than NumPy (1.3% to 1.9%), as `x.sum()` passes through its own; `m.any(axis=0)` within 1.3% from 4,096 elements, and 20 ns at 16.

#### Counting codes: `np.bincount` on int16 and int64

`np.bincount(x)` counts how often each value from 0 to the largest appears. NumPy converts an int16 array to 64-bit integers, reads it for its smallest and largest values, and counts one element at a time: 0.78 ns per element at 262,144 int16 codes on the Intel Core i5-14600KF. The kernels, `bincount_i16` and `bincount_i64`, take the number of counters `bins` as an argument, zero the counters themselves and ignore any element outside `[0, bins)`, so they are safe on any input; the binding sizes `bins` from the data, so every element it sends is inside. The naive C is clang's loop, unrolled four times, of a load, a compare with `bins`, a branch and an increment in memory: 0.20 to 0.25 ns per element on uniform codes, 0.37 on four distinct values, where an increment waits for the previous one of the same counter.

- **Technique (x86-64).** Three paths, chosen once per call. Below 8,192 elements, and with more than 1,024 counters, the naive C's loop, unrolled four times, with the counters cleared two 32-byte stores at a time. From 8,192 elements with at most 64 counters, a table of pairs: 64 by 64 counters of 32 bits, 16 KB below the stack pointer, one increment per two elements, so that equal neighbouring elements wait for one another a sixteenth as often; an 8-byte load of int16 (four `or` of int64) tests four elements at once for being in 0..63. From 8,192 elements with 65 to 1,024 counters, a second table of `bins` counters below the stack pointer takes every other element, as `bincount_u8` does with four, but only when at least 32 of the first 256 elements equal their successor: on data that does not repeat the second table was slower than the C (0.90x to 0.95x). The pair table is kept below 2^32 elements, where no 32-bit counter can overflow.
- **What limits it.** One counter load and store per element on the paths without the pair table, where the kernel ties the C (1.00x to 1.13x). The pair table takes 0.15 ns per element for int16 and about 0.21 for int64 on both kinds of data; from 4,194,304 int64 elements reading 32 MB and more of input is most of the time, and the gain shrinks to 1.1x to 1.4x. Over the naive C across the four tables: 1.26x for int16 and 1.16x for int64; uniform codes into 1,000 counters 1.04x and 1.01x.
- **A verdict for a person.** The margin over the C is small: 1.26x and 1.16x overall, nothing below 8,192 elements, and most of it from the pair table on at most 64 counters. As for `bincount_u8`, keeping the assembly rather than shipping the C is for a reviewer to confirm.
- **The binding.** The function hook of `np.bincount`, beside `bincount_u8`. A contiguous one-dimensional int16 or int64 array of at least one element, no `weights`, `minlength` absent or a Python int of at least 0. NumPy sizes its result by the largest element and raises for a negative one, so the array is read once first for its largest element as an unsigned number, where a negative element reads as too large: one maximum answers both questions. The scan cannot go a block at a time ahead of the kernel, as the guards of `np.fmax.reduceat` and `m.any(axis=1)` do, because the kernel needs `bins` before its first element. It is compiled for AVX2 with four running maxima in 32-byte vectors: written as a loop over elements the compiler kept one maximum, and for int64, which has no vector maximum in AVX2, a compare and a blend in a chain through every four elements held it to about 0.3 ns per element, as long as the kernel; with four it costs about 0.1 ns per int64 element from the third-level cache. A result longer than 65,536 counts and longer than the array is left to NumPy: NumPy takes it from `calloc`, whose large blocks are zeroed by the system only when touched, so `np.bincount(np.array([10**9]))` costs NumPy almost nothing and would have the kernel write 8 GB. NumPy 2.5.5 itself writes outside its result for an array whose largest element is the largest int64 with no negative one; the binding leaves that call to NumPy unchanged, and `delivery check` cannot run it.
- **What a caller must know.** The pair path uses 16.6 KB below the stack pointer and the second table 12.3 KB, whose first store can land up to 12 KB below the caller's frame (`bincount_u8` uses 8.2 KB). The pair path is slower than the C, 0.63x to 0.70x, when many groups of four hold an element outside 0..63, which the binding never sends. `n` of 2^32 or more with at most 64 counters takes the paths with 64-bit counters; it was never run (8 GB and 32 GB of input). No `pext`, `pdep`, gather or masked store; `popcnt` in the sample that chooses the second table.
- **From Python,** `uv run delivery time --only bincount` at a load average of about 1.0, on codes uniform in 0..63: `np.bincount(x)` on int16 is 3.9x faster at 16 elements (358 ns to 92 ns), 3.7x at 4,096 and 4.6x at 1,048,576 (835 µs to 184 µs); on int64 2.3x, 1.5x and 1.9x (659 µs to 347 µs); the recorded run on a quiet machine has 1.9x at 4,096. The int64 call reads 8 MB twice, once for the scan, and NumPy does not convert an int64 array first. `np.bincount(x)` on int32 passes through the hook within 0.4% to 2.7% of NumPy.

#### Hypotenuse: `np.hypot` on float64

`np.hypot(a, b)` on float64 calls the C library's `hypot` for every element: 4.7 to 7.3 ns per element with glibc 2.43 on the Intel Core i5-14600KF, and NumPy adds nothing to that but its call. `hypot` is not correctly rounded, and C libraries round it differently: an algorithm that differs from glibc's does so on a fraction of a percent to about ten percent of results ([issue 96](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/96)). So the reference, `naive.c`, is a loop that calls the C library's `hypot`, which is NumPy's result on every platform, and the x86-64 kernel computes the corrected algorithm of glibc's `e_hypot.c` (glibc from about 2.35) step for step, without fused multiply-add: it passes `judge check` only where the C library is that algorithm, as glibc 2.43 here is.

- **Technique (x86-64).** Four elements per group, two groups per step. Both inputs are tested on their bits before any floating-point instruction touches them (an integer add and compare per input) for every element lying in [2^-459, 2^511], where the algorithm needs no scaling; `vmaxpd` and `vminpd` come after the test because they raise the invalid flag on a quiet NaN. Such a group computes `h = sqrt(ax*ax + ay*ay)`, both branches of the correction, a blend on `h <= 2*ay`, the divide, and a blend of `ax + ay` where `ax >= ay * 2^54`. A group holding a zero, or any pair whose smaller input is negligible beside the larger, is computed the same way with 1.0 in place of those pairs' inputs, so that nothing is computed on a zero; a group holding an infinity, a NaN, an input above 2^511 or a pair below 2^-459 that needs scaling goes to a scalar routine in the same file that computes the whole algorithm one element at a time, as do the last one to three elements.
- **What limits it.** The divider: `vsqrtpd` and `vdivpd` on 256 bits share one unit, and a pair of them takes 3.78 ns on this core; the kernel takes 4.0 to 4.1 ns per group of four, 94% of that bound, at every size from 256 to 262,144 elements, and about the same beyond the caches (stores past the caches gained nothing). Over the naive C: 5.63x on standard normal values and 4.90x on magnitudes spread over 2^-80 to 2^80, where the C library returns early more often; 5.25x over both. Over NumPy 6.6x and 5.9x at a million elements.
- **Exactness outside the judge.** 36 million results against glibc 2.43's `hypot` over the whole range of doubles, with the output clear of the inputs and equal to each of them, every NaN payload included: no difference. The floating-point flags the kernel raises were compared with the library's on two million calls of 1 to 11 elements: it never lacks one the library raises, and raises underflow where the library raises nothing in one case: a pair whose smaller input is negligible beside the larger and whose square over it is below 2^-1022, in 2% of random bit patterns and in none of the pairs with exponents from -200 to 200 ([attempts.md](../kernels/hypot_f64/x86_64/attempts.md)).
- **The binding.** The inner loop of the `hypot` ufunc for float64, beside `clip`'s. It takes the loop's calls whose three operands are contiguous doubles and whose output is clear of both inputs or starts where one of them starts; scalar operands, strided arrays, `np.hypot.reduce` and `np.hypot.accumulate` (a partial overlap) are NumPy's. The flags already set are saved, the kernel runs with them clear, and if it raised overflow, underflow, invalid or divide by zero its result is dropped and NumPy's loop computes the same elements again, warning as NumPy does; an output that is an input is computed 256 elements at a time into a buffer on the stack, so that NumPy's loop still has the input of a flagged block. **The probe decides whether the kernel ships on a machine:** `install()` computes `np.hypot` on 20,000 fixed pairs (14,000 ordinary, then infinities, NaNs with payloads, zeros, denormals, inputs above 2^511 and below 2^-459), once on strided views, which NumPy's own loop and so the C library compute, and once contiguous, which the kernel takes, and keeps the hook only if the kernel's counter did not move for the first call, moved for the second, and every bit agrees. A kernel changed to fuse its first multiply and add, the closest other algorithm tried, differs on 16 of those pairs and is removed; one that returned the wrong NaN payload, on 375.
- **What a caller must know.** The results are NumPy's only where the C library's `hypot` is the algorithm the kernel computes; the binding's probe is what makes that safe. The output may be `a` or `b` exactly and must not overlap them otherwise. A call whose data raise a flag (an overflowing result, a denormal one, a signalling NaN, or the negligible pair above) is computed twice. On ARM64 the kernel is the naive C, which is the C library's `hypot`: no gain and nothing to verify. No `pext`, `pdep`, gather or masked store.
- **From Python,** `uv run delivery time --only hypot` at a load average of 0.35, standard normal values: `np.hypot(a, b)` 1.18x at 16 elements (251 ns to 212 ns), 4.65x at 4,096 (22 µs to 4.73 µs) and 6.75x at 1,048,576 (7.46 ms to 1.11 ms). A strided call, which reaches the hook and goes on to NumPy, is within 1.1% of NumPy. On 16 elements nearly all of the call is NumPy's own call path above the loop.

## Where the result is narrower than the number

The second item is about the reference and holds on every architecture. The others describe the ARM64 kernels and do not arise on x86-64, which has its own, listed after them.

- **`clip`: how fast depends on the bounds and, for a zero lower bound, on the data.** The two-operation path is taken when neither `lo` is `+0.0` nor `hi` is `-0.0`, because `fmax(-0.0, +0.0)` is `+0.0` where NumPy keeps `-0.0`. A lower bound of `+0.0`, as in `np.clip(x, 0, 1)`, has a three-operation path of its own: 1.32x to 1.34x over naive C at 4,096 elements on data without negative zeros, against 1.55x to 1.60x for the two-operation path. That path cannot be exact on its own, and no exact form in fewer than the four operations of compare-and-select was found, so it checks each chunk for a -0.0 and redoes the chunk when it finds one. A redo costs about 75 ns, so the path is quick to give up, and it is never tried again in the same call: one -0.0 among the first 7,264 float32 elements (3,632 for float64) sends the rest of the array to compare-and-select, however long the array is. Later than that, a single one costs one redo and the path carries on. At 65,536 float32 elements a call takes 6.1 microseconds with no negative zero, 7.5 with one at index 7,000, which is the speed of naive C, and 6.2 with one at index 8,000. On data with a negative zero that early the kernel runs at the speed of naive C from about 1,000 elements up, and up to about 14% slower at the smallest sizes that take the path, 256 elements for float64 and 512 for float32. Rounded data is such data: a value between -0.5 and 0 rounds to -0.0. The specs now time both kinds, which is where the figures come from. The judge also gained a value class with a single negative zero among ordinary values: without it nothing reached the redo of a chunk followed by more fast chunks, and a kernel whose redo stored nothing passed the check. An upper bound of `-0.0` still takes compare-and-select.
- **`clip`: defined for ordered bounds only.** For `lo > hi` NumPy returns `hi`, and for a NaN bound it returns NaN. The reference does neither. Compared outside the judge over 100 pairs of bounds per type, the reference matches NumPy bit for bit on every ordered pair and on none of the others. The specs now say so, the judge checks twelve ordered pairs (zeros of both signs, equal bounds, infinite, denormal and near-largest bounds), and the binding must send any other call to NumPy.
- **`clip`: reads and writes the floating-point status register.** To stay exact when the data holds a signalling NaN, the kernel reads FPSR after the fast loop and redoes the array if the invalid-operation flag was raised. If the caller left that flag set, it is cleared first and set again afterwards. No rule forbids this, flags are only ever added, and it is how a two-operation path can be exact at all; it is still more than "a pure function over its buffers" suggests, so it is listed under "Decisions".
- **`isin_i64`: fast only for test values between 0 and 65,534, or within 32,767 of the first one**, as above.

**On x86-64:**

- **`isin_i64`: fast only for test values from 0 to 32,767.** Any other set takes the 64-bit compare, which is level with the naive C (1.08x). The ARM64 kernel's second window, around the first test value, is not there.
- **`searchsorted` and `interp` with 64 needles against a large array.** The judge repeats the same needles, so a branching search learns them: at 16,777,216 elements the kernels are level with NumPy with 64 needles, and 2.6x (searches) and 6.9x (`interp`) ahead with 1,000.
- **`bincount_u8`: the gain on the judge's data is small** (1.04x to 1.2x over the naive C) and is 4x on bytes that are all equal, which the judge does not time.
- **`clip`: none of the three ARM64 items.** One path for every pair of ordered bounds and every kind of data.
- **`histogram_f64`: from 2^19 bins** the kernel is the naive C in assembly.

## What the judge does not see

Items 1, 2, 4 and 5 are about the judge and hold on every architecture. The measurements quoted in them, and items 3 and 6, come from the ARM64 kernels and from the references as compiled for ARM64.

1. **Scalar values nobody listed.** The judge used to pass one value per scalar. That hid the clip reference being wrong for reversed and NaN bounds and the clip kernel's dependence on the bounds, and it was the reason the faster `histogram` path could not be judged. A spec now lists further combinations ([judge-design.md](judge-design.md#scalar-sets)): twelve pairs of bounds for `clip`, sixteen bin counts and ranges for `histogram`, six values for masked assignment. All four kernels and their references pass them. What remains unseen is any combination that is not listed, and data that depends on the scalars: nothing places elements on a histogram's bin edges. Both let the `histogram_f64` overflow through. No listed range was near the largest float64 values, and the two sets added there since would not have caught it either, because the only values the judge generates inside such a range sit on its outer edge.
2. **One input per size from 4,096 elements up.** Small sizes cycle through a pool of inputs; large sizes repeat one. A branch predictor memorises a repeated input, so code with data-dependent branches looks faster than it is up to about 16,384 elements. Among the references only naive `maskfill_f64` branches on every element, so the speedup reported for masked assignment at those sizes is understated. The naive `compress` and `nonzero` loops are branchless and are not affected. It also hides the mask kernels' own mispredictions when they switch between their dense and sparse paths.
3. **Densities and distributions between the ones declared.** The mask kernels are timed at 50% and 5%, and are weakest near 10%. `bincount_u8` is timed on uniformly random bytes; all-equal bytes run at 2.1 ns per byte below 8,192 elements.
4. **Paths behind a size cap.** `interp_f64` above 65,537 elements.
5. **A wide tolerance at large sizes for float32.** The `cumsum` bound grows with the element count. Past some tens of thousands of float32 elements it no longer notices a single skipped element. Small sizes are not affected.
6. **Prefetch addresses.** The two count kernels prefetch up to 256 KB past the end of the array when it is larger than 16 MB. A prefetch never faults and reads nothing, so the judge passes it.

**On x86-64**, beyond the items above that hold everywhere:

1. **Paths behind a size.** The stores past the caches (from 24 MB of output, in `cumsum`, `clip` and `cast`) and the 512 searches in step (from 262,144 elements, in the three search kernels) are timed by the judge and not reached by its correctness cases, which stop far below. Each was tested outside the judge against the reference C.
2. **Densities between the ones declared.** The mask kernels' per-block switch was measured outside the judge from 1% to 100% and has no weak spot; a second sparse tier for 15% to 25% could not be judged, because `compare` times 5% and 50%.
3. **The naive C's own time varies between runs** for `bincount_u8`, by up to 2x at single sizes, with where its buffers land. The kernel's did not.
4. **One reference could not be judged locked:** `interp_f64`, above.

| `cumsum_f32`, `cumsum_f64` | Adjacent outputs come from separate running sums, so non-negative input does not give a non-decreasing output, and the steps back are not confined to the last bit: on uniform [0, 1) float32 data at a million elements about one adjacent pair in six decreases, by up to about 5, and after `2^24, 0.9, 0.2` a run of zeros alternates between 2^24 and 2^24 + 2 | ARM64. Any kernel that regroups the additions can do the same |
| `clip_*`, `cumsum_f32`, `cumsum_f64` | Leave floating-point status flags set where NumPy's loops leave them clear, and NumPy turns flags into `RuntimeWarning`s. `clip` leaves invalid operation set for a signalling NaN on any path, and for a quiet NaN on the compare-and-select path, which short arrays (under 192 float32 or 96 float64 elements), some bounds and some data take. Float `cumsum` raises overflow on finite data once the sum of squares of a 1,024-element chunk of outputs overflows: from running sums of about 1e18 (float32) or 4e152 (float64) when the whole chunk is that large, measured on this M1 Pro, up to the square root of the largest finite value when one output is. It raises invalid operation for a single infinite element. A left-to-right sum raises neither. The binding clears the flags after `clip`, as NumPy's clip loop does. For `cumsum` it clears them before the kernel and, if any is set after it, discards the kernel's output and lets NumPy compute the result and issue its own warnings, so such data runs at NumPy's speed | ARM64 |
| `histogram_f64` | Written for uniform bins with a finite range, `lo < hi` and at least one bin; the judge tests sixteen bin counts and ranges | Every architecture |
| `histogram_f64` | A finite range with `lo < hi` is not enough. Where the edges NumPy builds, `np.linspace(lo, hi, bins + 1)`, are not strictly increasing, `np.histogram` raises `ValueError: Too many bins for data range` and the kernel and the reference return counts. That happens for a range narrow against its distance from zero: 10 bins over `(1e16, 1e16 + 4)`, or 818 of 15,606 random bin counts and ranges in the fuzz run of [issue 9](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/9). The binding builds the edges as NumPy does and sends the call to NumPy unless each edge is above the one before | Every architecture: the spec admits such ranges and the reference has no way to fail |
| `compress_*`, `nonzero_bool`, `maskfill_f64`, `where_f64` | A mask byte is true when it is not zero, whatever its value; the kernels stay inside their buffers for any mask bytes ([issue 8](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/8) does not arise) | x86-64 |
| `maskfill_f64` | Rewrites unselected elements with their own bits in blocks of 32 with more than four selected elements, as on ARM64. A version that never does exists and was not adopted ([issue 70](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/70)) | x86-64 |
| `argmax_*`, `argmin_*` | Look at one block of the array a second time, bounded by its length. Nothing outside the array is read and the result is an index inside it, whatever a concurrent writer does ([issue 56](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/56) does not arise). Leave the invalid flag set after a call on an array that holds a NaN | x86-64 |
| `clip_*` | One path for every ordered pair of bounds and every kind of data; no status register is read; `out` may be `x` exactly. A partial overlap is wrong. Leaves the invalid flag set whenever an element is a NaN, quiet or signalling | x86-64 |
| `cumsum_i64` | `out` may be `x` exactly, as on ARM64 | x86-64 |
| `cumsum_f32`, `cumsum_f64` | `out` must not overlap `x`. Adjacent outputs come from eight (`float32`) or four (`float64`) separate running sums. The fast path runs up to running sums of 2^64 (`float32`, as on ARM64) and 2^1023 (`float64`; ARM64 about 1e154). The test for it raises no flag; the regrouped additions can raise overflow or invalid where NumPy's sequential sum does not, as on ARM64. `float32` arrays of 2^29 elements or more take the sequential loop | x86-64 |
| `cumsum_*` (the boolean one included), `clip_*`, `cast_u8_f32`, `where_f64`, `cummax_i64`, `cumfmax_f64` | From 24 MB of output the result is written past the caches and is in none of them when the call returns | x86-64 |

| `isin_i64` | The fast path needs every test value from 0 to 32,767 | x86-64 |

## Decisions

Each decision says which architecture it applies to. One taken for an ARM64 kernel is not taken for x86-64 by default.

Decided since the first version of this document:

- **`histogram_f64` takes the faster path.** 1.30x more, relying on an error-bound proof instead of performing NumPy's corrections on every element. It was adopted once the judge could test other bin counts and ranges. *ARM64. The proof is rechecked before an x86-64 kernel relies on it.*
- **The judge has scalar sets.** Several combinations of scalar values per spec, checked on kernel and reference alike and optionally timed. *Every architecture.*
- **`clip` has a path for a zero lower bound**, timed by the judge on data with and without negative zeros. *ARM64.*

Still open:

1. **`maskfill_f64`: is rewriting unselected elements acceptable?** It is the whole of the gain on dense masks. Recommended: yes for arrays the caller owns exclusively, which is NumPy's normal case, with the caveat documented. *ARM64. On x86-64 a store that leaves unselected elements alone may make the question unnecessary.*
2. **`clip`: is using FPSR acceptable?** Without it the kernel is the compare-and-select loop, which ties the C in cache. Recommended: yes, since the register is per thread and flags are only added, but it should be a stated exception to the kernel rules. *ARM64. On x86-64 the question may not arise.*
3. **Float `cumsum`: one overflow test per block instead of four?** About 12% faster from 256 to 16,384 elements. The proof that infinities land where NumPy has them would no longer hold for a running sum within rounding distance of the largest finite value. Recommended: no. *ARM64. It comes up again for any kernel that keeps the scheme.*

Open, from the x86-64 kernels. Each is a person's to decide; the kernels as committed take the conservative side of each.

4. **`maskfill_f64` on x86-64: adopt the masked store?** 1.6x faster up to 4,096 elements with half selected, 0.86x at 65,536 and 1,048,576, and unselected elements are never written. [Issue 70](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/70).
5. **`clip` on x86-64: record that the three ARM64 constraints do not hold, and let the binding pass in-place calls?** The kernel has one path, reads no status register and is right when `out` is `x`. The binding sends in-place `clip` to NumPy on both platforms today.
6. **Four borderline verdicts:** `bincount_u8` (1.04x over the naive C; 4x on repeated bytes), `count_nonzero_f64` (1.06x), `clip_f64` (1.09x) and `cast_u8_f32` (1.21x; level with NumPy in the middle sizes). Assembly is committed for all four. If one is turned back into C, the Linux wheel has to be built with clang ([release-design.md](release-design.md#more-than-one-platform)).
7. **Stores past the caches below 24 MB of output?** They are 1.3x faster from 2 MB in a micro-benchmark, and leave an output that would otherwise sit in the third-level cache out of every cache. The kernels use them from 24 MB, the size of that cache.
8. **`interp_f64`'s reference.** Corrected in a pull request of its own ([issue 73](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/73)) and merged. CI checks it on both architectures (157,062 cases pass on each), and the x86-64 kernel's figures are from a locked run. Nothing is left to decide.
