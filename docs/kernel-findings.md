# The first kernels: results and findings

This records the first pass over the shortlist in [survey-design.md](survey-design.md): 23 kernels for 15 NumPy operations, each with a spec, a naive C reference and an attempt at beating that C in ARM64 assembly. Twenty-two ship as assembly and one as C. It covers the kernel half of phase 3 in [core-design.md](core-design.md). The other half, the binding and `install()`, is in [delivery-design.md](delivery-design.md).

The numbers are the judge's. What is open to review is the set of decisions at the end.

The kernels exist for two architectures: ARM64, and x86-64 on Linux, written afterwards to the plan in [x86-64-design.md](x86-64-design.md). This document holds both.

## How this document is organised

- **What is true of an operation is said once.** Each family under [Results by family](#results-by-family) opens with "The operation": what NumPy requires of the result, why the simple loop is slow, the idea a fast kernel is built on, and any proof it rests on. None of that depends on the processor.
- **What is true of one architecture sits under its name.** "On ARM64" and "On x86-64" hold the instructions chosen, the block sizes, the measured speeds, what was tried and did not help, and what limits the kernel on that processor. A sentence such as "this is the hardware's limit" is only ever about the hardware of its heading.
- **A kernel's verdict is per architecture.** The same kernel can ship as assembly on one, as C on another, and not be installed on a third.
- **The x86-64 sections were written after the ARM64 ones,** from kernels written separately against the same specs. What each family was asked to try is listed in [x86-64-design.md](x86-64-design.md#candidates-per-family), with what became of it.

## Summary

### ARM64

- **Twenty-two of the 23 kernels have assembly that the judge reports as significantly faster than naive C.** The exception, `cast_u8_f32`, ties the compiler and ships as its C. Every kernel is faster than NumPy at every size measured. Twenty-one are bit-identical to NumPy. The two float `cumsum` kernels are within a declared tolerance, which is what makes them faster at all.
- **The second shortlist added six kernels with ARM64 assembly:** `cummax_i64`, `cumsum_bool`, `any_rows_bool`, `bincount_i16`, `bincount_i64` and `where_f64`, 3.27x, 2.90x, 7.71x, 2.45x, 1.60x and 1.82x over naive C across the judge's sizes, all bit-identical to NumPy, recorded on a quiet machine at the top of their stack. Their rows come before the last two kernels'. The other two have ARM64 assembly since their specs were narrowed to what the binding passes ([fmax-references-design.md](fmax-references-design.md)), both recorded `--unlocked` (their specs differ from `main` until the narrowed specs merge) on a quiet machine at the top of their stack, in the last rows: `fmax_reduce_f64` 8.24x over naive C and `cumfmax_f64` 13.8x. The counts in this section are of the first 23.
- **`any_cols_bool`, for `m.any(axis=0)`, has ARM64 assembly** ([issue 160](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/160)): 8.77x over naive C and 73.6x over NumPy across its eight timed tables, bit-identical, recorded `--unlocked` at a load average of 2.1 to 2.3; its rows are the last of the table. See [Any by columns](#any-by-columns-manyaxis0).
- **`unique_i64` was added for `np.unique` on int64 labels** ([issue 189](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/189)): 1.44x over naive C and 41.6x over NumPy across its four timed cases, bit-identical, on a busy machine. See [Distinct labels](#distinct-labels-npunique-on-int64).
- **`sum_f32` was added for `np.sum` on float32** ([issue 160](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/160), [coverage-gaps-arm64.md](coverage-gaps-arm64.md#built-on-arm64)): NumPy's own result bit for bit, by making its additions in its order, several of its blocks at a time. 2.11x over naive C and 18.7x over NumPy across the judge's sizes, recorded `--unlocked` on a quiet machine. See [`np.sum` on floats](#npsum-on-floats-in-numpys-order).
- **`sum_f64`, its sibling on float64:** 1.31x over naive C and 10.6x over NumPy across the judge's sizes, no size slower than the C, recorded `--unlocked` on a quiet machine. Beyond the first-level cache it was slower than the C until the walk stopped mispredicting. See [`np.sum` on floats](#npsum-on-floats-in-numpys-order).
- **Three kernels are a different algorithm, not NumPy's loop made faster:** `argsort_f64`, `argsort_i64` and `argsort_u64`, a stable `argsort` by radix sort where NumPy merges. They ship as C: 13x to 18x faster than NumPy at a million elements on keys that differ in all their bits, 65x to 78x on small integers. A merge sort in C only ties NumPy, and the one hot loop of the radix sort, written by hand, tied the compiler's. See [Stable `argsort`](#stable-argsort).
- **The survey's prediction for the "C kernels" was wrong.** It expected assembly to tie the compiler on `clip`, `astype`, `np.sum` on `uint8` and `count_nonzero`, because clang vectorises those loops. Tried, assembly beat the C on five of the six: by 5.9x on `sum_u8`, by 1.3x to 1.4x on the counts, and by 1.36x to 1.38x on `clip` for bounds that allow its fast path. A vectorised loop is not necessarily a good one.
- **Three results are narrower than their headline number.** `isin_i64` is fast only for test values that lie close together, and the two `clip` kernels only for some bounds and, with a lower bound of zero, only for data without negative zeros. Each is set out below.
- **The judge has blind spots that mattered.** It tested one value per scalar argument and, from 4,096 elements up, repeats one input per size. Both hid things that were found by other means. The first is now closed: a spec lists several combinations of scalar values, and the `clip`, `histogram` and masked-assignment kernels are checked with them.

Every figure is how many times faster the kernel is. "vs naive C" and "vs NumPy" are geometric means over the eleven sizes from 16 to 16,777,216 elements, with the 95% interval for the first; the four columns after it are the speedup over naive C at single sizes. "n.s." marks a difference that is not statistically significant. Kernels timed on two kinds of input, or with two sets of scalar values, have a row for each and one for both. The NumPy mean is dominated by NumPy's fixed cost per call at small sizes (for example 1.3 µs for `np.sum` on 16 bytes), which is a property of the call path and not of the loop. The column at 1,048,576 elements shows the loops alone. A row marked † is the mean of ten runs on a machine that was not fully quiet: its brackets hold the lowest and the highest of the ten, not a confidence interval, and "n.s." is not marked on it. A row marked § is from one `--unlocked` run on a quiet machine on 20 November 2026, at a load average of 1.6; its spec is not yet on `main`. [How the numbers were taken](#how-the-numbers-were-taken) has the conditions.

### x86-64

- **All 23 kernels have assembly, and every one is faster than its naive C over the eleven sizes.** For four the margin is small enough that shipping the C would be defensible: `bincount_u8` (1.04x), `count_nonzero_f64` (1.06x), `clip_f64` (1.09x) and `cast_u8_f32` (1.21x, level with NumPy in the middle sizes). Those verdicts are a person's to confirm. Twenty-one are bit-identical to NumPy; the two float `cumsum` kernels are within the declared tolerance, as on ARM64.
- **NumPy is a different opponent.** It has AVX2 loops for `argmax`, `clip` and `astype` here. `argmax` and `argmin` still gain 1.7x to 1.9x at a million elements; `clip` and `astype` are level with NumPy there and gain at the two ends.
- **Memory is the ceiling sooner.** A scalar chain costs one cycle per element on this core against three on the M1, and a plain copy in the second-level cache is only 1.65x faster than that. `cumsum_i64` is the kernel that shows it: 1.18x over the naive C.
- **Several ARM64 caveats do not exist in the x86-64 kernels:** the three of `clip`, the density at which the mask kernels are weak, mask bytes other than 0 and 1, the unbounded second scan of `argmax`, the slow last bin of `histogram`, the 1e154 limit of float `cumsum`. They are listed under [What a caller must know](#what-a-caller-must-know). None was removed from the binding or the rules on the kernels' authority.
- **One reference was wrong for this architecture.** `interp_f64`'s fused the multiply and the add, which NumPy's x86-64 build does not. It was corrected in a pull request of its own, and the kernel's row below is a locked run against the corrected reference.
- **Kernels added since the first pass** are the last rows of the table and are described under [Second shortlist](#second-shortlist). Their runs are `--unlocked`, because each kernel's spec and reference arrived in the same pull request as its assembly; the reports in `results/` say so. The last row, `unique_i64`, came later still ([Distinct labels](#distinct-labels-npunique-on-int64)): its run is locked, and was taken at a load average of 2.6, with other work on the machine. The two rows after it are the search on int64, the first kernels of [Coverage gaps](#coverage-gaps): `--unlocked`, at a load average of 0.7 to 1.0.
- **From Python,** with the binding and NumPy's call overhead on both sides: [delivery/results/intel-r-core-tm-i5-14600kf.md](../delivery/results/intel-r-core-tm-i5-14600kf.md). No hooked call is slower than NumPy at any size timed.

## What the judge had to learn

The two fixtures needed nothing beyond arrays and a size. The real kernels needed scalar arguments, several combinations of values for them, fixed-size buffers and buffers sized by a scalar, buffers modified in place, inputs that must be sorted, kernels without assembly, and a tolerance measured at a scale other than the result. ## Results by family

### `argmax` and `argmin` on floats

#### The operation

- **What NumPy requires.** The index of the first element equal to the maximum (or minimum). A NaN wins over every number, and among several NaNs the first one wins.
- **The idea.** The position does not have to be tracked element by element. Reduce each block of elements to its maximum, remember which block held the best value so far, and search only that block for the position at the end. A NaN has to be noticed block by block, because it decides the result wherever it sits.

#### On ARM64

- **Technique.** One pass that reduces each block of elements (64 for float32, 32 for float64) to its maximum with `fmax`, and remembers which block held the best value so far with one scalar compare and a `csel`. No index vector is carried, and no branch depends on the data unless it holds a NaN. At the end only the winning block is scanned for the first position that equals the maximum. `fmax` returns NaN when either operand is NaN, so a block that holds one shows up in the same compare as unordered, and a scalar loop then finds the first NaN. Short arrays take straight-line code with no loop instead: 8 to 64 and 129 to 256 elements for float32, 4 to 64 for float64. Below those sizes a scalar loop runs.
- **Why it is this far ahead.** NumPy's loop is scalar for floats. The float32 kernel runs at about 0.03 ns per element in the first-level cache and 0.05 to 0.07 beyond it. On input without a NaN its speed does not depend on the data. A NaN ends the block loop early, but sends the straight-line sizes to a scalar scan of the whole array: 256 float32 elements take 12 ns without a NaN and 112 ns with one in the last position.
- **What did not help:** software prefetch (slower), aligning the loop, a vector search for the first NaN, cheaper reductions of a block to a scalar.

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **The opponent is NumPy's own AVX2 loop,** 0.083 ns per `float32` and 0.163 ns per `float64` at 1,048,576 elements, not a scalar loop. The compiler leaves the reference scalar, 0.77 ns per element, so the column to read is NumPy's.
- **Technique.** One pass that does not track positions. The extreme so far is kept in every lane of a register; a block of eight vectors is reduced with `vmaxps` into four accumulators that are never reset, so a lane of an accumulator is beyond that register exactly when the block holds a new extreme. One branch per block covers "a new extreme or a NaN" and is taken about as often as the logarithm of the number of blocks. When it is taken the block is looked at again: the first NaN ends the call, otherwise the block becomes the remembered one. The answer is the first element equal to the extreme in the remembered block: `vcmpeqps`, `vmovmskps`, `tzcnt`.
- **NaN is not free here.** `vmaxps` returns its second operand when either is a NaN, so a NaN can be lost. `vcmpunordps` of two vectors is true where either holds one, so four compares cover a block.
- **Result.** 1.9x over NumPy at 1,048,576 for all four kernels, 3x in the second-level cache, 1.3x from memory; 6.0x to 7.5x over the eleven sizes, most of that being NumPy's cost per call. 6.7x to 13.2x over the naive C.
- **Limit.** Seventeen instructions per block for the two ports that take a maximum or a compare: 0.030 ns per `float32`. Beyond 262,144 elements, the caches.
- **What differs for a caller.** The second look is at one block and is bounded, nothing outside the array is read whatever a concurrent writer does, and the result is always an index inside the array; [issue 56](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/56) does not arise. New: `vmaxps` and `vminps` set the invalid flag for any NaN operand, so these kernels leave it set after a call on an array with a NaN.
- **Not tried:** aligned loads and a prefetch, which is where a second run should start.

#### Along the rows of a matrix

`np.argmax(m, axis=1)` makes NumPy call the type's argmax function once per row, at 6 to 20 ns a row whatever its length. The binding takes the call above NumPy and walks the rows itself ([argmax-rows-design.md](argmax-rows-design.md)). Measured on ARM64 only (Apple M1 Pro, from Python):

- **From six float64 or nine float32 elements, the kernels once per row.** The ARM64 kernels take about 1.6 ns a row from four float64 or eight float32 elements; below that they read the elements one at a time and take 5 to 12 ns, little better than NumPy. On 300,000 rows of ten float64, 0.69 ms against NumPy's 2.43 ms (3.5x); of ten float32, 0.49 against 6.68 (13.7x).
- **Narrower rows: a loop of the binding's own, one row per lane** of a 32-byte vector, written with the compiler's vector types. It takes a column when the best so far is a number and the element is not at most the best (not at least, for `argmin`): NumPy's NaN and tie rules with no branch. 0.37 to 0.49 ns a row on rows of two, where NumPy takes 7, and about 0.25 (float64) or 0.18 (float32) ns more per column, which meets the kernels at the thresholds above.
- **A loop that branches loses.** `naive.c`'s loop, one row after another, took 11.5 ns a row on random rows of ten float64 against NumPy's 8.3, from mispredicted branches.
- **What a caller must know.** The kernel path holds the interpreter lock for the whole call, where NumPy releases it: the kernels read a row twice ([issue 9](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/9)). The binding's loop reads each element once and releases it above 8,192 elements. On x86-64 the same thresholds are used and neither path has been timed.

### Mask selection: `compress` and `nonzero`

#### The operation

- **What NumPy requires.** The selected elements (for `nonzero`, their positions) in their original order, and how many there are.
- **Why the simple loop is slow.** It asks "is this element selected?" before each store. On a mask with no pattern the processor guesses that answer wrong about half the time.
- **The idea.** Do not ask. Store at the current output position and move the position forward by the number selected, a block of elements per store. A mask that selects few elements is better served by finding the selected positions directly, so a kernel may have a path for each.
- **It relies on scratch output.** The stores write past the elements that are kept, inside the buffer's capacity. That is what `scratch=True` permits; the trade-offs are in [judge-design.md](judge-design.md#scratch-output-writing-inside-the-capacity).

#### On ARM64

- **Technique.** Dense masks are handled eight mask bytes at a time, and each kernel compacts differently. `compress_f32` packs the eight bytes into an index into a 256-entry table of shuffle patterns, and `tbl` moves the selected elements to the front of a block that is stored whole. `nonzero_bool` uses the same index into a table that lists the positions of the true bytes. `compress_f64` has no table and no `tbl`: it stores the elements in pairs, and the mask decides the address each pair is loaded from. In all three the write pointer advances by the number selected. Sparse masks: 64 mask bytes are reduced to a 64-bit mask and the set bits are extracted with `clz`, a fixed number at a time, so there is no data-dependent branch per element. The kernel switches between the two by measured density.
- **A weak spot the judge does not time.** Around 10% density the switch between the two paths mispredicts. Measured outside the judge, `nonzero_bool` costs 0.127 ns per element at 10% against 0.045 at 5% and 0.110 at 20%. The judge times 50% and 5%.
- **Limit.** With half the elements selected, `compress_f64` writes as fast as memory allows from 16,384 elements up.

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **Technique.** One `vpcmpeqb` against zero and one `vpmovmskb` turn 32 mask bytes into 32 bits; a mask byte is true when it is not zero, whatever its value. `compress`: each byte (`float32`) or four bits (`float64`) of that word indexes a table of permutations, `vpermps` or `vpermd` moves the selected elements to the front, all lanes are stored and `popcnt` advances the write position. `nonzero`: each byte indexes a table of the positions of its set bits, widened with `vpmovzxbq`.
- **A sparse block path, per block and with no state.** In `compress_f64` and `nonzero_bool` a block of 32 with at most four true elements skips the table: `tzcnt` and `blsr` take a fixed four positions out of the mask word. 1.24x and 1.40x overall, all of it at 5% selected. In `compress_f32` it was a wash and is not used.
- **There is no weak spot near 10% density.** The ARM64 kernels are slower than their dense path there. A micro-benchmark from 1% to 100% finds no density at which the per-block switch is slower than the table path alone, because that path is held by its stores and not by the front end. `compress_f32` has no switch at all.
- **Result.** Over NumPy at 1,048,576, half and 5% selected: `compress_f32` 32.6x and 12.6x, `compress_f64` 16.7x and 9.4x, `nonzero_bool` 3.2x and 19.6x. Over the naive C overall: 2.52x, 1.86x, 2.81x.
- **Limit.** Inside the first-level cache, the instruction count: 35 per 32 elements for `compress_f32` on a core that renames six per cycle. Beyond it, the cache lines of the output, each fetched before it is written and written back after: at 65,536 elements the same loop runs at first-level-cache speed once the store is removed.
- **What did not help:** aligning the loads of x, a prefetch.
- **Found on the way, and used by every later kernel:** a `lea` with a scaled index takes two cycles on this processor, so a write position that advances every step is kept as an element count and advanced with `add`.
- **What differs for a caller.** Mask bytes other than 0 and 1 are handled: any non-zero byte is true, so [issue 8](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/8) does not arise on x86-64.

#### `nonzero` on a matrix, on ARM64

`nonzero_2d_bool` serves `np.nonzero(m)` on a C-ordered boolean matrix ([coverage-gaps-arm64.md](coverage-gaps-arm64.md#built-on-arm64)). NumPy's result for a matrix is one (count, 2) int64 array of (row, column) pairs with two views of its columns, so the kernel writes the pairs: two int64 per True element, into a scratch output of 2n. A partial last row is a row like the others. The log is `kernels/nonzero_2d_bool/arm64/attempts.md`.

- **Technique.** Three paths, each without a branch per element. Sparse, any width: `nonzero_bool`'s 64-byte bitmask and its `clz` extraction (6 positions, or 12), with each flat position f turned into its row without a division, as the high half of 2f times ceil(2^63 / w) (`umulh`; exact while f × w < 2^63, so the path runs below 2^31 elements), and its column as f − row × w. In rows of 512 or more a block inside one row skips the multiplication. Dense, rows of 8 or more: a loop over rows whose chunks of 8 bytes never cross a row, the last one masked; the 256-entry position table, the positions widened and added to the chunk's column, and paired with the row by `zip`. Dense, rows under 8: chunks of as many whole rows as fit in 16 bytes, whose positions index two small tables of the row within the chunk and the column (`tbl`). After a dense stretch, at most one true byte in eight goes back to the sparse path.
- **Result.** Over the naive C, which stores a pair for every element and is 1.1 ns per element whatever the share of True: 10.5x to 14.0x at 5% True and 3.4x to 4.0x at half, at the four widths timed (3, 22, 32 and 1,000); over NumPy 26x to 58x (20 November 2026, load average 1.6). About 0.08 ns per element at 5% True (0.055 in rows of 1,000) and 0.27 to 0.31 at half, at a million elements. A timer outside the judge found no width from 1 to 1,000 slower than the C: the weakest is 17 at half True, 3.0x at a million elements, where every row ends in a chunk of one byte.
- **Limit.** The dense path stores eight 16-byte pairs per 8 mask bytes, about six cycles per chunk, which looks like the limit of the stores. A 32 KB table of ready-made pairs was slower than building them (0.92x).
- **What a caller must know.** Every mask byte must be 0 or 1: the count is a multiplication that sums the bytes. The binding reads the mask first, as for `nonzero_bool`. From Python the call copies the pairs out of the binding's buffer; at a million elements, half True, that copy is about 0.13 ms of a 0.44 ms call, and shrinking the buffer in place with `realloc` instead was slower on macOS (0.91 ms). Not yet built for x86-64, where these calls stay with NumPy.

### Masked assignment

#### The operation

- **What NumPy requires.** Selected elements become the value. NumPy's `x[mask] = v` does not touch the others.
- **Why the simple loop is slow.** It is a store under a condition, element by element. Compiled as a branch, it is guessed wrong about half the time on a mask with no pattern.
- **The idea.** Work on whole blocks. Either write the block back with the value blended in, which also writes the unselected elements with their own bits, or use a store that leaves them alone where the architecture has one. Which is available, and what it asks of the caller, is per architecture.

#### On ARM64

- **Technique.** Dense blocks: load 16 elements, blend with the value through `bsl` under a mask widened by `tbl`, store all 16. Sparse blocks: no load; one store per selected element at an address chosen without branching.
- **It rewrites elements it does not change.** A dense block stores unselected elements back with their own bits. The array must be writable throughout, and a write by another thread to an unselected element between the load and the store would be undone. NumPy's `x[mask] = v` does not touch unselected elements. This is why the compiler cannot do it and why it has to be a decision: see "Decisions".
- **The jump at 65,536 elements is naive C slowing down**, not the kernel speeding up. Below that size the branch predictor memorises the judge's repeated input and naive C looks better than it is.

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **Technique.** Dense blocks blend, as on ARM64: four elements are loaded, combined with the value by three logical instructions under a lane mask from a 16-entry table, and stored back. `vblendvpd` is not used: measured, it runs once per cycle here, and three logical instructions run three per cycle. A block of 32 with at most four selected elements is not blended: `tzcnt` and `blsr` find the positions and the value is stored to each, so no element is loaded and a cache line with no selected element is not touched (1.37x overall, 1.92x at 5% selected).
- **Result.** 18.4x over NumPy at 1,048,576 with half selected and 7.3x with 5%; 3.96x over the naive C overall.
- **The masked store, measured and not adopted.** `vmaskmovpd` writes only the selected elements, which would make decision 1 below unnecessary on x86-64. Against the blend with half selected it is 1.55x to 1.64x faster from 64 to 4,096 elements and 0.86x at 65,536 and 1,048,576: without the load nothing tells the processor which lines the stores are about to need. A candidate that removes a caveat is a person's to adopt: [issue 70](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/70).
- **What did not help:** lane masks read back from the block's stored byte mask; aligning the blocks.

### `isin` with eight test values

#### The operation

- **What NumPy requires.** One boolean per element: whether it equals any of the eight test values.
- **The work.** Done literally, eight compares per element. A kernel gets ahead of a compiler only where the test values allow something cheaper than comparing against each one.
- **The idea.** When the test values lie close together, an element can be narrowed to a small key and looked up in a table, with every value outside the window made unable to match.

#### On ARM64

- **Technique.** When the test values allow it, each element is narrowed to a 16-bit key (with saturation, so values outside the window can never match) and membership is four `tbl` lookups on the nibbles of the key, or three when every test value is below 4,096. They allow it when all eight lie between 0 and 65,534, or all lie within 32,767 of the first test value. Otherwise the kernel compares at 64 bits, eight compares per vector, which is what clang already does.
- **The speedup depends on the test values, and on their order.** For small or closely spaced test values the kernel is several times faster than naive C. Because the second window is centred on the first test value, the same eight values can be fast in one order and slow in another: eight values spanning 60,000 ran at 0.17 ns per element with a middle value first and 0.62 with the lowest first. For unrelated 64-bit values it ties naive C, and at 16 elements it is slower, because it has tried and rejected the narrow set-ups first. The judge's original distribution only ever took the fastest path, so a second one (`wide values`) was added to show the other.
- **Why the general case cannot be beaten:** eight 64-bit compares per vector of two elements is the work, the compiler emits exactly that, and there is no wider compare on this hardware.

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **The general case is settled the other way round from ARM64.** A compare of four 64-bit elements exists here, and the compiler already emits it: the naive C is vectorised, 0.30 ns per element for any values, 22x to 30x ahead of NumPy on its own. Written by hand the same loop gains 8%.
- **Narrow keys.** When every test value is from 0 to 32,767, sixteen elements are narrowed from 64 to 16 bits with three `vpackusdw` and compared with eight `vpcmpeqw`, where the wide compare needs eight for four elements: 0.64 cycles per element against the compiler's 1.6. A block that holds an element outside that range cannot be narrowed and is compared at 64 bits. The nibble tables of the ARM64 kernel were not needed: with sixteen keys per register, eight compares cost the same fifteen instructions.
- **Result.** On values and test values from 0 to 999: 1.95x over the naive C and 40x over NumPy at 1,048,576. On values spread over the int64 range: 1.08x and 3.8x.
- **A first version was 0.77x against the naive C** and was not committed: its window began at the smallest test value, so data spread over the same range as the test values sent nearly every block the slow way.
- **What differs for a caller.** The narrow path needs test values between 0 and 32,767. The ARM64 kernel also takes any eight values within 32,767 of the first one. Negative sets, and large values close together, get the wide path here.

### `searchsorted` and `interp`

#### The operation

- **What NumPy requires.** For each needle, the first position in the sorted array whose element is not less than it, with NaN ordered after every number. That is `side='left'`. With `side='right'` it is the first position whose element is greater than the needle, which differs only for a needle that equals an element (the position after the last equal one) and for a NaN needle (the length of the array). Each side is a kernel of its own. `interp` runs the same search over `xp` and interpolates between the two neighbours. Its result is bit-identical only if the division and the multiply-add are done the way NumPy's build for that architecture does them.
- **Why the simple loop is slow.** It runs one search at a time. Each probe waits for the compare before it, and on a large array each probe is a cache miss the next one queues behind.
- **The idea.** A branch-free binary search run for many needles at once. The step size depends only on the array length, so all searches stay in step and their cache misses overlap instead of queueing.

#### On ARM64

- **Two lengths.** `searchsorted_f64(a, v, out, n, needles)` and `interp_f64(x, xp, fp, out, n, queries)`: `n` is the length of the sorted array (of `xp` and `fp`), and the number of needles or queries is separate. The first selects the code path and the depth of the search; the second is how many times the loop runs.
- **Technique.** Every step loads one probe per needle, compares, and moves each needle's base by the compare mask. Sixteen needles at a time when the array has fewer than 64 elements, 64 at a time up to 1,048,576, and blocks of 128 beyond.
- **A path needs one full group of needles.** Leftover needles are covered by redoing the last full group, so a call with fewer needles than its path's group takes a narrower one: 64 to 127 needles against an array of 1,048,576 or more use the 64-needle path, and anything smaller is searched one needle at a time. That last case is correct and untuned: a handful of needles against a large array is mostly the cost of the call.
- **`interp`** runs the same search over `xp`, then the interpolation for two queries per vector with a real `fdiv` and a fused multiply-add, which is what NumPy's build uses. Any group whose result contains a NaN is redone by a line-by-line copy of the reference, which keeps the output bit-identical.
- **What limits it.** In cache, which elements are probed, not the instruction count: making every search probe the same elements ran 1.25x to 1.33x faster with identical instructions. Beyond the cache, the number of cache and TLB misses, which the kernel overlaps but cannot reduce.
- **The number of needles is verified for the values the specs list:** 0, 1, and the counts either side of 16, 64 and 128 (15, 16, 17, 63, 64, 65, 127, 128, 129), and 1,000. Fresh random cases draw from the same list.
- **Timed with 64 and with 1,000 needles per call**, across the sweep of array lengths. The summary table shows the rows for 1,000. Each figure is the mean of ten locked runs on a machine that was not fully quiet (load average about 3; see [How the numbers were taken](#how-the-numbers-were-taken)), with the lowest and highest of the ten in brackets:

  | Kernel | Needles | vs naive C | at 16 | at 4,096 | at 1,048,576 | at 16,777,216 | vs NumPy | at 1,048,576 |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | `searchsorted_f64` | 64 | 9.06x [8.89 – 9.27] | 5.24x | 9.59x | 10.2x | 12.2x | 5.20x | 2.34x |
  | `searchsorted_f64` | 1,000 | 9.08x [8.98 – 9.14] | 5.42x | 9.47x | 8.83x | 15.5x | 9.31x | 6.40x |

The first figures published for these kernels, 8.84x and 22.7x over NumPy at 1,048,576 elements, were taken with as many needles as elements, a shape the judge no longer times.
- **The right side** is `searchsorted_right_f64`: this kernel with every `a[i] < key` turned into `a[i] <= key`, and the length of the array stored for a NaN needle where the left side searches for the first NaN. Nothing else differs, and from Python the two sides take the same time (2.23 ms and 2.22 ms for a million needles against 1,000 elements). Its rows in the table above are not comparable with the left side's against naive C: clang compiles the right side's reference with a branch per probe and the left side's without one, so the two baselines differ, by most where the judge's inputs are unpredictable.
- **Needles that equal an element are generated at every size.** They are the only input on which the two sides differ. The judge draws needles from the array, sorted and with repeated values, at every size it checks and in every scalar set ([judge-design.md](judge-design.md#values-drawn-from-another-argument)). Before it did, they came only from inputs of up to 5,000 elements, and a mutation run showed that a wrong comparison on the path for 1,048,576 elements or more passed the judge, for either side; the same mutations now fail it. `delivery check` searches arrays of 1,048,576 and 1,048,579 elements for needles drawn from the array as well, from Python. `interp_f64` draws its queries from its sample points the same way, up to the 65,537 points it is checked at.
- **Not verified by the judge:** `interp_f64` is checked up to 65,537 sample points (the NumPy oracle is too slow beyond), so its path from 1,048,576 was compared with the reference outside the judge, without guard pages. Neither kernel is run on unsorted input by the judge, where the result is as meaningless as NumPy's and the kernel is required only to stay inside its buffers. That holds by construction, and a one-off run of 2,952 calls against guard pages found no fault ([issue 40](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/40) asks the judge to check it).

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **Technique.** A search is a fixed number of steps with no branch on the data: `half = (len + 1) / 2; if a[lo + half - 1] < needle: lo += half; len -= half`. Every probe is inside the array whatever the data, so the kernels stay inside their buffers on unsorted input by construction. Four searches share one `vgatherqpd`. Up to 64 run in step, with their positions kept in the output buffer, which is one 64-bit slot per needle; 512 when the array does not fit the second-level cache.
- **How many in step is the finding.** The chain from one step of a search to its next, through a gather, is about thirty cycles on this processor, and a gather can start every two to three. Sixteen searches in four registers left most of that idle: going to 64 was 1.92x. Beyond the second-level cache 512 against 64 is 1.4x to 1.8x, because the number in step is the number of cache misses in flight.
- **NaN.** A NaN in the array compares false and is never passed. A NaN needle is detected as the needles are read, and only then is its answer found (the first NaN of the array for the left side, n for the right) and written over.
- **`interp`** runs the right-side search over `xp`, then the values as the reference's cases read backwards, each a blend that overrides the ones before, with a real `vdivpd` and four gathers per four queries.
- **The multiply and the add of `interp` are not fused here.** NumPy's x86-64 build rounds the product and then the sum: of 20,000 random two-point interpolations the two forms differ in 6,131, and `np.interp` equals the unfused one in all of them. The kernel does the same. The shared reference fused on every architecture and failed its own check on x86-64 ([issue 73](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/73)); it now rounds as NumPy's build for the architecture does. Results on the two architectures can differ in the last bit, as NumPy's own do.
- **Result.** With 1,000 needles, over NumPy at 1,048,576 elements: `searchsorted_f64` 6.2x, `searchsorted_right_f64` 6.6x, `interp_f64` 10.6x. Over the naive C overall: 5.7x, 5.5x, 4.5x. With 64 needles the judge repeats the same needles, the naive C's and NumPy's branches are learnt, and the kernels are 3x over NumPy at that size and level with it at 16,777,216.
- **What differs for a caller.** No padded short group: one to three needles are searched one at a time, four and more as a thousand are, so [issue 39](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/39) and [issue 67](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/67) do not arise in that form. No limit of 2^32 sample points in `interp`. The output buffer is working storage during the call. `vgatherqpd` is fast on this processor and slow on older AMD processors and on Intel processors with the gather mitigation; untested elsewhere.

### `histogram` and `bincount`

#### The operation

- **What NumPy does for `histogram`.** It computes a bin estimate by divide, multiply and truncate, then corrects it against the two neighbouring bin edges. A bit-identical kernel either performs those operations in the same order, each rounded on its own, or proves for the element in hand that neither correction can fire.
- **The proof.** An element whose position inside its bin is far enough from both edges needs no correction, provided the bin count and range pass a check made once per call. That check has to reject a product that overflowed. The bound was derived for the operations as one kernel performs them, so it is rechecked for every architecture; the ARM64 one is below and in its `kernel.s` header.
- **`bincount`.** One counter is loaded and stored per element, and that is the limit whatever surrounds it. Equal neighbouring values wait on each other unless they are counted into separate tables.

#### On ARM64

- **`histogram_f64`, the literal path.** NumPy computes a bin estimate by divide, multiply and truncate, then corrects it against the two neighbouring bin edges. The kernel can do those operations two elements per vector, in the same order and individually rounded, falling back to scalar code for a lane that is out of range, NaN or in the last bin. That takes 14 vector operations per two elements and about 0.88 ns per element, and nothing cheaper computes the same thing.
- **The path the kernel takes instead.** It computes the estimate exactly as NumPy does and skips the two edge corrections for an element whose position inside its bin is at least 2^-12 of a bin away from either edge: about 2,047 elements in 2,048. An error bound in the `kernel.s` header shows that neither correction can fire for such an element, provided the bin count and range pass a check made once per call; when they do not, the literal path is used. The check compares two products and has to reject one that overflowed: as first written it took two infinities for equal, so narrow ranges near the largest float64 values got the fast path where the bound is false, and elements near a bin edge could land in the neighbouring bin. Every other element goes to a scalar copy of the reference. Four vector operations per two elements, 0.69 ns per element, 1.30x [1.30 – 1.30] over the literal path.
- **Why it was held back, and what changed.** It replaces operations with a proof, and the proof depends on the bin count and range, of which the judge could test one combination. The spec now lists sixteen, and the kernel passes all of them. The judge's data seldom lands near a bin edge, so the evidence about edges is differential tests outside the judge: the author's against the reference (410 million elements), and one against `np.histogram` itself with values on and within three ulps of NumPy's edges and around the 2^-12 threshold (430,000 rounds, 126 million elements, no mismatch; a histogram without the corrections fails 2,086 of 3,000 rounds of the same inputs). Neither reached the top of the float64 range, which is where the overflow above was found in review, by a third test of the same kind. With the check corrected, 6,000 ranges of magnitude 2^960 and up, 636 of them with both products infinite, give no mismatch over 1.6 billion elements; before, 28 of 3,000 did.
- **`bincount_u8`** is limited by one counter load and store per element. Reading eight input bytes per load and, for large arrays, counting into four tables on the stack so that equal neighbouring bytes do not wait on each other, is worth 1.24x. A 65,536-entry pair table and `dc zva` for zeroing were slower.

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **`histogram_f64`: no divide per element.** A divide of four doubles takes eight cycles on this processor, so a kernel that computes the reference's estimate as the reference does cannot go below two cycles per element. The proof never needed that estimate, only one within `4 u N` of the real position. The kernel computes `(v - lo) * S` with `S = nbins / width` taken once, which is within `3.01 u N`.
- **Fixed point through `vcvttpd2dq`.** `S` is scaled by 4,096, so the conversion gives the bin in the upper bits and twelve bits of fraction below. An element is accepted when the fraction bits are neither all zero nor all one, which is the ARM64 kernel's 2^-12 on either side of an edge, and when the whole number is within the limit as an unsigned number: a NaN and anything too large convert to 0x80000000 and fail that test with no test of their own. Everything else, and every call whose bin count and range fail the once-per-call check, goes through a scalar copy of the reference.
- **The last bin is on the fast path.** The reference never takes its last correction there, so only the lower edge has to be certain. With one or two bins the ARM64 kernel sends all or half of the elements to its scalar route.
- **Evidence.** The proof is in the `kernel.s` header. A differential test outside the judge against the reference, with elements on bin edges, within three units in the last place of them, on the 2^-12 threshold, at `lo` and `hi`, and ranges up to the top of float64: 60,000 rounds, 213 million elements, no mismatch. A copy of the kernel without the edge test fails 2,264 of 3,000 rounds of it.
- **Result.** 7.5x over NumPy at 1,048,576, 6.98x over the naive C overall: 0.42 to 0.60 ns per element against 3.9.
- **Limit.** The three vector ports: eleven vector instructions per four elements before the bins are taken out, four more to take them out, then four counter updates.
- **`bincount_u8`.** One counter update per byte at one store per cycle is the limit, and the compiler's loop is at it: 1.04x to 1.2x over the naive C on the judge's uniformly random bytes, depending on the run, because the naive C's own time moved by up to 2x at single sizes. Byte i is counted in table i mod 4 from 32,768 bytes, which is what pays on bytes that repeat: all bytes equal, 0.35 ns per byte against 1.39.
- **What did not help:** two or four count tables for `histogram_f64`.
- **What differs for a caller.** From 2^19 bins `histogram_f64` runs at the speed of the naive C (ARM64: 2^31).

### `cumsum`

#### The operation

- **Integers are exact in any order.** So the additions can be regrouped: prefix sums inside each block are built from the inputs alone, and the running total is added to a whole block at a time, so the chain from one block to the next is one add per block instead of one per element.
- **Floats cannot be both exact and fast.** Bit-identical output requires NumPy's order of additions, each depending on the one before, so n elements take n times the latency of one floating-point add. No exact kernel can beat that, on any processor.
- **So float `cumsum` is in tolerance mode**, as [survey-design.md](survey-design.md) decided.
- **Infinities and NaN must still land where NumPy has them.** A regrouped sum can overflow where the sequential one does not, and can return to a finite value where the sequential one stays infinite. A kernel has to prove, for each stretch of output, that neither happened, and redo the stretch in NumPy's order when it cannot.
- **The tolerance needed a scale.** The judge's tolerance was measured at the size of the result. A regrouped running sum over data that cancels differs from NumPy's by millions of such units while being exactly as accurate, because a small result carries the rounding error of its large terms. The allowed error of element i is now 4 units in the last place of `(i + 1) * (|x[0]| + ... + |x[i]|)`, the standard bound for adding in any order.

#### On ARM64

- **Integers.** In cache the scheme runs at about 0.13 ns per element; from 4,194,304 elements it runs at the speed of a plain copy.
- **The exact float chain, measured.** The latency of one floating-point add is 0.93 ns here. Naive C and an exact assembly kernel both measured 0.95 to 0.97 ns per element from 4,096 elements up. The first version of these specs was exact by mistake and produced that tie; the specs now declare a tolerance.
- **The float kernels** use the integer scheme, with two running sums per block for float64 and four for float32, and are limited by the number of vector operations per block, not by the latency of an add.
- **Infinities and NaN still land where NumPy has them.** A regrouped sum can overflow where the sequential one does not, and can return to a finite value where the sequential one stays infinite. The kernel works in chunks of 1,024 elements and accumulates the square of every output; a finite sum of squares proves every output of the chunk was finite and well below the overflow threshold, so the sequential sum cannot have overflowed either. A chunk that fails the test is redone one element at a time in NumPy's order. The argument is in each `kernel.s` header.
- **Accuracy, measured outside the judge** against an exactly computed sum over 80,303 inputs per kernel: the kernels are at least as accurate as NumPy's sequential sum in most classes tried. On all-equal values, the worst case for a sequential sum, NumPy's error was 7 to 16 times the kernel's. They are less accurate in two. Where cancellation makes every one of NumPy's partial sums exact, NumPy's error is zero and the kernel's is not: on the repeating float32 pattern `-2^24 + 2, 1, 2^24, 1` the kernel loses 1 per repetition, 250,000 at a million elements where the true sum is 1,000,000, and float64 does the same with 2^53. And on magnitudes that grow quickly the kernel's error was up to about 3 times NumPy's in review, at most about 2.5 units in the last place. Both are inside the declared tolerance; small integers stored as floats, where any order of additions is exact, come out exact.
- **Costs of the scheme.** Running sums above about 1e153 (float64) or 4e18 (float32) fail the squares test and run at the speed of naive C, with correct results. Fewer than 32 (float64) or 20 (float32) elements use the exact sequential chain. Float32 arrays of 2^29 elements or more use the sequential loop throughout, because the proof does not hold there.

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **The ceiling is lower here, and it was measured first.** With the data in the second-level cache a plain copy loop takes 0.115 ns per `int64` and the naive C 0.19, one cycle per element. So no integer running sum can beat the compiler's by more than 1.65x beyond the first-level cache. The float chains cost two cycles per element, which leaves room.
- **Technique.** The ARM64 regrouping. What one output vector adds to the one before it is built from the input alone: the input plus an overlapping load one element back, then one `vperm2f128` for the shift across the halves of the register, so the chain from vector to vector is one add. `float32` builds its window of eight with two more overlapping loads. `cumsum_i64` also has a 16-byte version, used from 512 to 131,071 elements: its accesses rarely straddle a cache line, and a 32-byte access that does costs a cycle or two.
- **The proof, with the design's candidate.** Non-finite values land where NumPy has them by the ARM64 kernels' argument, chunk by chunk. The test is a compare of magnitudes in place of a sum of squares: `vpand` and `vpmaxud` on the bit patterns per vector, one compare per chunk. For `float64` that lifts the fast path's limit from running sums of about 1e154 to 2^1023; for `float32` the limit stays 2^64, because the proof for `float32` needs it. The test raises no floating-point flag of its own.
- **Loads ahead of the stores.** The first `cumsum_f64` lost 2.5x at 262,144 and 1,048,576 elements with the output 16 bytes behind the input. Loading the next vector's input before storing the current output removed it, and keeps `cumsum_i64` right when the output is the input.
- **Stores past the caches from 24 MB of output:** 1.2x to 1.26x at 4,194,304 and 16,777,216 elements.
- **Result.** Over NumPy at 1,048,576: `int64` 5.3x, `float64` 6.1x, `float32` 13.3x, a third to a half of the ARM64 figures, as the design expected. Over the naive C overall: 1.18x, 1.81x, 2.96x.
- **Evidence beyond the judge.** 20,990 runs per float kernel against the left-to-right sum with infinities, NaN, values near the largest finite one and near the fast path's limit, at sizes that reach the cache-bypassing stores: positions of non-finite values exact, finite values within the tolerance. `cumsum_i64`: every path, and in place.
- **What did not help:** aligning the stores as a general step (the judge puts the output 16 bytes after the input within a cache line, so one of the two is always off); adds done as fused multiply-adds by one to use a third port.

### `sum`, `count_nonzero`, `clip`, `astype`

#### The operation

- **`sum` on `uint8`.** The result is a 64-bit sum, so the bytes have to be widened somewhere. Where, and how often, is the whole design.
- **`count_nonzero` on floats.** A NaN counts as non-zero and both zeros count as zero.
- **`clip`.** NumPy's result is what compare-and-select gives, `v < lo ? lo : (v > hi ? hi : v)`: an element equal to a bound keeps its own sign of zero, and a NaN passes through. A shorter sequence built from a minimum and a maximum is usable only where it gives the same bits, and that depends on how the architecture's minimum and maximum treat zeros and NaN. The reference is defined for ordered bounds only; see [below](#where-the-result-is-narrower-than-the-number).

#### On ARM64

These were expected to ship as C. Five ship as assembly.

- **`sum_u8`.** clang widens every byte to 64 bits before adding: 24 vector instructions per 32 bytes. The kernel adds bytes pairwise into 16-bit accumulators with `uadalp`, sixteen accumulators per step, and widens every 128 steps. In cache it runs at three 16-byte loads per cycle, which is the core's limit.
- **`count_nonzero_i64`.** A 64-bit element is non-zero exactly when the larger of its two halves is, so three levels of `umaxp` reduce eight vectors to sixteen bytes before one test and one accumulate: nine vector instructions per eight vectors against clang's sixteen. From 65,536 to 1,048,576 elements both run at the rate the second-level cache delivers and tie.
- **`count_nonzero_f64`.** One bit test against a mask that ignores the sign handles NaN and both zeros, then the same narrowing.
- **`clip`.** Compare-and-select needs four vector operations per vector and clang is already there. `fmin(fmax(x, lo), hi)` needs two, but differs from NumPy on signed zeros at the bounds and on signalling NaNs, so the kernel takes it only when the bounds rule the first out and detects the second afterwards. For a lower bound of +0.0, the bound `np.clip(x, 0, 1)` has, the only element the two operations get wrong is -0.0, so a third operation per vector watches for one and a chunk that held one is done again by compare and select. See the caveats below.
- **`cast_u8_f32` stays C.** clang's loop is already at the limit of one 16-byte store per cycle. The assembly gained at 16 and 64 elements (no entry checks, no scalar remainder) and at 16,777,216 (non-temporal loads), and tied at the eight sizes between: 1.05x [0.99 – 1.08] overall in one run and 1.06x [1.00 – 1.07] in the next. That is a tie, and the design ships C on a tie. The assembly is in the git history and its attempts log is kept.

#### On x86-64

Each kernel's `x86_64/attempts.md` has every attempt, the judge's tables and what was left untried.

- **`sum_u8`.** `vpsadbw` against zeros adds 32 bytes into four 64-bit sums, on one port; mixed with `vpmaddubsw` on the other two the loop reaches 45 bytes per cycle. Aligned loads were worth 1.5x beyond the first-level cache, and a prefetch and four streams, both rejected on the M1, pay here. 6.15x over the naive C, 51x over NumPy at 1,048,576.
- **`count_nonzero`.** A compare with zero and a subtract into an accumulator, two instructions per four elements, loads aligned from 64 elements. The compiler vectorises this loop and both are at memory's speed from about a thousand elements: 1.21x over the naive C for `int64` and 1.06x for `float64`; 1.26x and 4.4x over NumPy at 1,048,576.
- **`clip`: two instructions, one path, none of the ARM64 caveats.** `vmaxps a, b` computes `a > b ? a : b`: it returns its second operand when the two are unordered or equal, zeros of either sign included. With the bound first, `vmaxps lo, v` then `vminps hi, t` is the reference bit for bit for every pair of bounds with `lo <= hi`. No path depends on the bounds or the data, no status register is read, and the output may be the input. The compiler does not emit this: it emits a minimum, a compare and a blend that runs once per cycle, which is why the assembly is 1.25x over it for `float32` and 1.08x for `float64`. With the loads a block ahead of the stores there is no stall with the output just behind the input, where the survey found NumPy 8x slower. Level with NumPy at 1,048,576, 1.7x at 65,536, 2.0x at 16,777,216.
- **`cast_u8_f32`.** `vpmovzxbd` from memory and `vcvtdq2ps`, with aligned stores and stores past the caches from 24 MB. The naive C ties NumPy, as the design expected. The assembly is 1.21x over the C: level with NumPy from 16,384 to 1,048,576 elements and 3.39x at 16,777,216.
- **The invalid flag.** `vmaxps` and `vminps` set it for any NaN operand, quiet or signalling. The binding's clearing of flags after `clip` is needed on x86-64 as on ARM64.

### Second shortlist

Kernels from [second-shortlist-design.md](second-shortlist-design.md), which took its candidates from the NumPy calls of a program in use. They are written for x86-64 first; on ARM64 each ships as its naive C until it has assembly there. `cummax_i64`, `cumsum_bool`, `any_rows_bool`, `bincount_i16`, `bincount_i64`, `where_f64`, `fmax_reduce_f64` and `cumfmax_f64` have ARM64 assembly: all eight.

#### `cumsum` on booleans

`np.cumsum(mask)` gives a running count of the True elements as int64. NumPy converts the mask to int64 in chunks and adds: 1.9 ns per element at 4,096 and 3.5 at 65,536 on the Intel Core i5-14600KF. The naive C is the scalar chain at one cycle per element, 0.19 ns.

- **Technique (x86-64).** Eight elements are one step. Their eight mask bits index a table of 256 entries, each the running count within the group as eight bytes; `vpmovzxbq` from memory widens the two halves to four 64-bit lanes each. A second table holds the group's total, loaded into every lane with `vpbroadcastq`. With T the count before the group, the two output vectors are the widened halves plus T and the next T is T plus the total: a chain of one add per eight elements. The bits of 32 elements come from one `vpmovmskb`.
- **It writes eight bytes for every byte it reads,** so the stores decide. Up to three elements go one at a time first, until the output is a multiple of 32 bytes and no store straddles two cache lines; the mask being a byte per element, any number can. Inside the first-level cache it runs at 0.062 ns per element, 3.0x over the naive C. Once the output is in the second-level cache it runs at 0.10 ns per element whatever the arithmetic: 1.9x.
- **Stores past the caches from 24 MB of output** are worth 2.6x at 4,194,304 and 16,777,216 elements, far more than for `cumsum_i64` (1.25x): here nearly all the memory traffic is the output, and an ordinary store reads each line before overwriting it.
- **At 1,048,576 elements the judge's first table is misleading.** It shows the kernel 0.75x of the naive C. The same run's second table, and a micro-benchmark of the two alone, have the kernel 1.17x faster there (174 µs against 203 µs). The first table times NumPy between them, and NumPy allocates 8 MB for its result on every call, which pushes the kernel's own output out of the third-level cache just before the kernel is timed.
- **`pext` in one place:** a group of eight outside a block of 32 gets its bits from an 8-byte load and `pext`, which is fast on Intel and on AMD from Zen 3 and slow on earlier AMD processors. Blocks do not use it.
- **What a caller must know:** every mask byte is 0 or 1. The spec says so, and the kernel uses the bytes as numbers. The binding reads the mask for other bytes first.
- **From Python,** `np.cumsum(mask)` is 3.1x faster at 16 elements, 9.7x at 4,096 and 9.5x at 1,048,576 with half the mask True (recorded on a quiet machine at the top of the stack: 3.0x, 9.6x and 13.9x, and 9.1x in the run recorded for release 0.3.0: this figure moves between runs). At 16 elements nearly all of the 338 ns left is `np.cumsum`'s own Python wrapper, which the method hook sits below.
- **Technique (ARM64).** On the Apple M1 Pro NumPy takes 2.6 ns per element at 4,096 and 2.4 from 65,536 up, and the naive C, which clang leaves as the scalar chain, 0.38 to 0.39 ns, about 1.2 cycles. The kernel takes sixteen elements a step, as two groups of eight, and uses no table. A group is loaded as one 64-bit number and multiplied by 0x0101010101010101: byte k of the product is the number of true bytes up to and including position k of the group, and its top byte is the group's total. The count before the group, T, is carried in a general register, one add of a cycle per group. The product and T are moved to vector registers with `fmov` and `dup`; two `tbl` spread the product's bytes four at a time over 32-bit lanes, and `uaddw` and `uaddw2` widen those to 64 bits while adding T. That is four output vectors a group and twelve vector operations a block, two deep. The loop loads and multiplies the next block between widening the current one and storing it. The last 0 to 15 elements, and every array under 16, go one at a time.
- **Figures (ARM64),** recorded with `uv run judge run cumsum_bool --record` on the Apple M1 Pro on a quiet machine, at a load average of 2.6 to 2.9, at the top of the stack that added the ARM64 kernels: 2.90x [2.89 – 2.94] over the naive C and 34.5x [34.45 – 35.00] over NumPy across the judge's sizes. 3.06x and 3.04x at 16 and 64 elements; 3.75x to 3.97x from 256 to 16,384 (0.093 ns per element at 4,096); 3.56x at 65,536; 1.80x at 262,144; 2.06x from 1,048,576 up. Of two runs on the quiet machine this is the second; the first read 2.82x [2.79 – 2.83], with 2.65x at 65,536 and 2.23x at 1,048,576. The kernel's pull request recorded 3.20x [3.17 – 3.21] and 37.6x at a load average of about 11, with 3.04x at 262,144 and 2.5x to 2.6x from 1,048,576 up; the rows from 65,536 up move with what else uses the caches, and with what NumPy, timed between the two, leaves in them (below).
- **What limits it (ARM64).** The stores. Sixteen elements are eight 16-byte stores and the M1 Pro does two a cycle: outside the judge a loop of the stores alone takes about 4.2 cycles a block in the first-level cache, 0.08 ns per element, with one load beside them 4.4, and the kernel about 4.8. From 65,536 elements up a loop of the stores alone is no faster than the kernel, outside the judge; the recorded run has the kernel at 0.10 ns per element at 65,536 and 0.18 from 1,048,576. `stnp` in place of `stp` is slower in the first-level cache and no faster beyond it, and `dc zva` on the output lines ahead of the stores takes 10 to 70 cycles a block, so nothing is stored past the caches.
- **On the Apple M1 Pro the depth of the work costs, not only its amount.** A first version kept the byte counts and T in vector registers: 19 vector operations a block, seven deep from the load to the stores, with one add carried from block to block. It took 0.122 ns per element at 4,096, about 6.2 cycles a block, and as long with every `tbl` replaced by `eor` and every `uaddw` by `add`. Outside the judge, eight stores with 16 independent `eor` beside them take 4.4 cycles, with the same 16 two deep 4.9, four deep 5.7, and as one chain 6.5. Moving the counts to the general registers, which leaves the vector work two deep, was 1.07x by `compare`, and the order of the loop 1.04x.
- **Compared with x86-64.** The lookup the x86-64 kernel is built on was tried in this kernel's shape, the eight mask bits indexing 32-bit counts already spread, 8 KB of tables, and was slower on the M1 Pro: 0.90x [0.88 – 0.92] by `compare`, 0.83x to 0.87x up to 65,536 elements ([attempt 5](../kernels/cumsum_bool/arm64/attempts.md)). Four loads a block whose addresses come out of a multiply cost more there than four `tbl`. x86-64 brings the output to a multiple of 32 bytes first and stores past the caches from 24 MB; this kernel does neither. A 16-byte store straddles a cache line only when `out` is not a multiple of 16, and the result of a large call is left in the caches as an ordinary store leaves it. The misleading first table is seen here at a lower size, on a quiet machine: the kernel takes 52.7 µs at 262,144 elements in the first table (1.80x over the naive C) and 33.0 µs in the same run's second table (2.86x), where no NumPy call comes between. At 1,048,576 the first table has it at 2.06x.
- **With the output 16 bytes behind the input (ARM64).** In the recorded run the kernel is level there: 1.03x [1.00 – 1.08] over the table, 0.99x [0.98 – 1.00] at 4,096 elements and 0.99x at 16,384. The run the kernel's pull request recorded had 1.05x [1.04 – 1.07] at 4,096. In two runs with one other run on the machine it was slower: 0.94x [0.93 – 0.96] and 0.96x [0.95 – 0.96] at 4,096 elements (427.4 ns against 408.9 ns in the second) and 0.99x and 0.98x at 16,384, and 0.99x to 1.00x from 4,194,304 up. The loop already loads the next block ahead of the current block's stores, and no attempt was aimed at that table.
- **Sizes the judge does not time (ARM64).** Every size timed is a multiple of 16, so the one-element loop that takes the last 1 to 15 elements is checked at every count and never timed. Outside the judge 15 elements take 6.8 ns where 16 take 2.2, and 31 take 7.4. A version that does them as groups of eight, the last group ending exactly at the end of the array and writing again the outputs it shares with what was done, takes 2.3 ns for 15 elements and 3.7 for 31. `compare` read 0.999x [0.997 – 1.000] for it, so by the rule it is not in the kernel ([attempt 6](../kernels/cumsum_bool/arm64/attempts.md)); whether to keep a gain the judge cannot time is for a person to decide.
- **What a caller must know (ARM64):** nothing beyond the spec: every mask byte is 0 or 1. For a mask holding other bytes this kernel is not the x86-64 one. It adds the bytes as numbers only while a group of eight sums to at most 255; above that the byte counts of the group carry into one another. The binding reads the mask for other bytes first on both architectures, so no call reaches that. The count is 64 bits wide, so the size has no limit.
- **From Python (ARM64),** `uv run delivery time --only cumsum` on the Apple M1 Pro at a load average of 10.3: `np.cumsum(mask)` with half the mask True is 2.7x faster at 16 elements (1.11 µs to 405 ns), 12.1x at 4,096 (10.3 µs to 855 ns) and 19.3x at 1,048,576 (2.42 ms to 125 µs).

#### `np.where(c, a, b)`

`np.where(c, a, b)` with a boolean condition takes each element from `a` where the condition is True and from `b` where it is not. NumPy branches on every element: 2.7 ns per element at 65,536 with half the condition True, 0.54 ns with 95% True, on the Intel Core i5-14600KF. The naive C, which clang vectorises four elements wide, takes 0.21 to 0.24 ns from 1,024 to 65,536. The kernel serves int64 as well as float64: it only moves 64-bit elements.

- **Technique (x86-64).** Sixteen elements are one block. Their sixteen condition bytes are loaded once into both halves of a register and compared with zero, and one `vpshufb` per group of four spreads four of those bytes over four 64-bit lanes, giving the lane masks. A group's output is (mask and `b`) or (`a` and not mask): no floating-point instruction touches the data, so a NaN keeps its payload and its signalling bit and a zero its sign. The elements left after the whole blocks are one more block that ends at the end of the buffers, writing some elements a second time with the same values. From 32 elements one group of four is done where the output is and the blocks start where it is at a multiple of 32 bytes, so no store straddles two cache lines. The blocks ask for the cache lines 512 bytes ahead while those are inside the buffers.
- **Every element is 17 bytes read and 8 written,** so beyond the first-level cache the kernel moves memory. 1.4x to 1.65x over the naive C from 16 to 65,536 elements, where the data comes from the second-level cache; level with it at 262,144, in the third-level cache; and 1.2x to 1.47x from 1,048,576 up. From 3,145,728 elements, 24 MB of output, the output is stored past the caches. The two timing distributions take the same code; where they differ, the buffers were placed differently.
- **Two attempts were left for a person to decide** ([attempts.md](../kernels/where_f64/x86_64/attempts.md), 6 and 9): a select by a masked store, half a percent faster but with an instruction that is slow on older AMD processors, and stores past the caches from 762,600 elements, where everything the call moves fills the third-level cache rather than its output alone. The second is 1.36x to 1.46x faster at 1,048,576 elements, and leaves the result in no cache, unlike the rule the other kernels follow.
- **What a caller must know:** the output must not overlap the condition, `a` or `b`. Both kernels take any non-zero condition byte as True, as NumPy does, but the spec promises only 0 and 1, so the binding reads the condition for other bytes first. The binding writes into a new array, so the output cannot overlap. A scalar side, as in `np.where(c, a, np.nan)`, is spread over a buffer of 256 elements on the stack and the kernel runs a block of 256 at a time: such a call never stores past the caches.
- **From Python,** at a load average of about 3.3 (other judges were running), `np.where(c, a, b)` on float64 with half the condition True is 4.7x faster at 16 elements, 3.2x at 4,096 and 6.0x at 1,048,576; `np.where(c, a, np.nan)` with 95% True 4.4x, 3.0x and 2.5x. A first version of the binding spread the scalar over a buffer of all n elements, from `malloc` above 256: at 1,048,576 elements that was 1.01x, writing and reading back 8 MB more than the call needs.
- **The judge's run was on a busy machine** (load average 4.6): the sizes from 1,048,576 up share the third-level cache and memory with other work.
- **Technique (ARM64).** On the Apple M1 Pro clang leaves the naive C scalar, at about 0.36 ns per element up to 4,096 elements, and NumPy is level with it. Sixteen elements are one block. Their sixteen condition bytes are loaded once and made 0xff or 0x00 by `cmtst`; eight `tbl` spread each pair of those bytes over the two 64-bit lanes of a vector (the index table of `maskfill_f64`), and eight `bsl` take each lane from `a` or `b`. Nothing but loads, stores, `tbl` and `bsl` touches the elements. `a` and `b` are read with `ldnp`, the non-temporal form of `ldp`, which reads the same bytes. The elements left after the whole blocks are one more block that ends at the end of the buffers, as on x86-64; under 16 elements a scalar loop with `csel`. The output is not brought to a cache-line boundary, nothing is prefetched and nothing is stored past the caches.
- **Figures (ARM64),** recorded with `uv run judge run where_f64 --record` on the Apple M1 Pro on a quiet machine, at a load average of 1.6 to 1.8, at the top of the stack that added the ARM64 kernels: 1.82x [1.82 – 1.82] over the naive C across the judge's sizes and both distributions (half true 1.81x, 95% true 1.83x), 5.25x [5.25 – 5.26] over NumPy. 1.92x at 16 elements, 2.2x at 64, 2.6x to 2.7x from 256 to 4,096, 1.7x to 1.75x from 16,384 to 262,144, 1.32x to 1.34x at 1,048,576 and 1.14x to 1.23x from 4,194,304 up. Against NumPy at 1,048,576 and up, where the fixed cost of a call no longer matters, 1.44x to 1.56x. The kernel's pull request recorded 1.81x and 5.26x at a load average of 8 to 13, with 1.65x to 1.71x against NumPy at 1,048,576. The two distributions take the same code. With the output 16 bytes behind the input it is not slower over the table: 1.02x [1.01 – 1.02]. Its one slow size is 1,048,576, at 0.87x on the quiet machine as at 0.90x in the pull request's run, and there the naive C is 1.7 times slower than in the first table as well (689 µs against 411): the second table's placement of the buffers at that size, not other work on the machine.
- **What limits it (ARM64).** Up to 4,096 elements, the judge's pool of inputs, which for this kernel does not fit the first-level cache: about 0.13 ns per element, and the time does not follow the instruction count (32 elements a step and 8 a step were both slower, all loads of a block before its stores no faster). From 16,384 to 262,144, the second-level cache: about 0.21 ns per element. Beyond that, memory: 0.28 to 0.36 ns per element. `ldnp` for `a` and `b` was 1.06x overall, 1.14x at 16,384 and 1.26x at 1,048,576, and nothing in the caches.
- **Compared with x86-64.** This core offered no cheap store that skips reading the output line: `stnp` made no difference and zeroing the output ahead with `dc zva` was 0.47x, so the x86-64 question of from which size to store past the caches does not arise here. Bringing the output to a 128-byte boundary, which x86-64 kept for 32-byte stores, was 0.97x on the Apple M1 Pro, all of it lost at the small sizes; prefetching 1,024 bytes ahead, which x86-64 kept at 512, was 0.97x. Neither of the x86-64 attempts left for a person has a counterpart here. One result is not understood: two blocks of 16 per step, which only halves the loop's own instructions, was 0.87x, and 0.75x to 0.83x at every size from 16,384 up ([attempt 4](../kernels/where_f64/arm64/attempts.md)).
- **What a caller must know (ARM64):** as on x86-64. The output must not overlap the condition, `a` or `b`: the last block reads the inputs again for up to 15 elements already written. The binding writes into a new array. A condition byte is True when it is not zero, in the blocks and in the scalar loop; the judge sends only 0 and 1 and other bytes were not tested, and the binding sends none.
- **From Python (ARM64),** `uv run delivery time --only where` on the Apple M1 Pro at a load average of 8.0: `np.where(c, a, b)` on float64 with half the condition True is 4.8x faster than NumPy at 16 elements (390 ns to 81 ns), 3.3x at 4,096 and 1.7x at 1,048,576 (476 µs to 282 µs); `np.where(c, a, np.nan)` with 95% True 4.0x, 3.2x and 1.7x.

#### Running maxima: `np.maximum.accumulate` and `np.fmax.accumulate`

`np.maximum.accumulate(x)` gives each element the largest of the elements up to it; `np.fmax.accumulate(x)` the same, ignoring NaN. On the Intel Core i5-14600KF NumPy takes 1.5 ns per element for int64 and 1.8 to 2.3 for float64. The naive C is very different for the two. For int64 clang writes a compare and a branch per element, which costs 0.15 ns while the branches are predicted, as they are on data the judge repeats; for float64 it keeps a chain through the running value, 2.0 ns per element. Two kernels serve three calls: for integers `np.maximum` and `np.fmax` are one function, and both go to `cummax_i64`.

- **Technique, `cummax_i64` (x86-64).** AVX2 has no 64-bit integer maximum, and one built from `vpcmpgtq` and a blend costs more than the naive C's predicted branch. So the kernel avoids the maximum where it can see there is nothing to compute. In blocks of 16 elements, with M the maximum so far, a block is *flat* when no element is above M (one compare per vector; the block is M sixteen times), *rising* when no element is below the one before it and the element before the block is M (one compare per vector against the same load one element back; the block is a copy), and otherwise done four elements at a time in the integer registers with compares and conditional moves, the chain from group to group one compare and one move. Which way the next block is tried depends on how the last one went; no path has a branch that depends on a single element.
- **Technique, `cumfmax_f64` (x86-64).** `vmaxpd a, b` keeps b when the two are equal, which is NumPy's rule for zeros (the later of `+0.0` and `-0.0`), but returns a NaN b. So every element first goes through `vmaxpd` against minus infinity, which turns a NaN into minus infinity and changes nothing else; once the running value is a number that changes no output. The elements before the first number go one at a time. Then blocks of eight: the running maximum inside each vector in two steps, the second vector against the first's last lane, and both against the running value. The chain from block to block is one `vmaxpd` per eight elements, and the next block is loaded before this one is stored.
- **Both** bring the output to a multiple of 32 bytes first, so no store straddles two cache lines, and store past the caches from 24 MB of output.
- **What limits them.** `cummax_i64` runs at the speed of a copy in the second-level cache, 1.1x to 1.35x over the naive C up to 65,536 elements, level with it in the third-level cache, and 1.4x to 1.5x in memory, with a third less traffic. On data whose maxima a branch predictor cannot learn the naive C is far slower than the judge shows: 2.6 ns per element against the kernel's 0.72 with a new maximum at a random half of 2,000,000 elements ([attempts.md](../kernels/cummax_i64/x86_64/attempts.md)). The worst found is the other way: on 1 0 2 1 3 2 ..., which the branches predict, the kernel is 0.7x of the naive C. `cumfmax_f64` is held by its arithmetic, ten `vmaxpd` and six shuffles per block: 0.14 to 0.15 ns per element from 64 to 65,536 elements on every distribution, 13x to 14x over the naive C, 4x to 4.4x in memory.
- **At 1,048,576 elements the first table is misleading for `cummax_i64`**, as for `cumsum_bool`: 0.98x and 0.92x of the naive C there, where NumPy, timed between them, allocates 8 MB for its result on every call. The same run's second table has the kernel at 432 µs with the output 16 bytes behind the input and the naive C at 1.30 ms.
- **What a caller must know:** `out` must not overlap `x`, for both. `cumfmax_f64` must not see a signalling NaN. NumPy, through the C library's `fmax`, answers one with a quiet NaN and forgets the maximum so far; the kernel ignores it as it ignores any NaN. The spec leaves signalling NaNs out, and the binding reads a float64 array for them first: one that holds any goes to NumPy. The same read sends to NumPy an array that holds a negative zero, or NaNs of more than one bit pattern: there NumPy's own answer depends on its build. Which zero a running maximum of zeros of both signs is differs between NumPy's x86-64 and ARM64 builds (seen in CI on Apple Silicon), and so may which NaN an output is while everything so far is NaN; the kernel and the naive reference reproduce the x86-64 build. Both conditions are broader than needed (a negative zero matters only where a zero is the running maximum) and kept simple. The spec's domain is exactly the binding's condition (no signalling NaN, no negative zero, NaNs of one bit pattern), and its `prepare` makes every `-0.0` the judge generates a `+0.0` ([fmax-references-design.md](fmax-references-design.md)), so the judge checks the reference and the kernel on every architecture only where all of NumPy's builds agree. On ARM64 NumPy's loop is one `fmaxnm` per element, from `x[0]`. The read finds a NaN by comparing it with itself and a negative zero by comparing its bits, compiled for AVX2, and costs about 0.12 ns per element on this processor, near the kernel's own 0.15. Looking for signalling NaNs alone took 0.05 ns per element, and 0.17 before the binding of `np.fmax.reduceat` compiled it for AVX2 in place of comparing magnitudes as integers with SSE2, which has no 64-bit compare. `np.maximum.accumulate` on floats propagates NaN, which neither kernel does, and stays with NumPy. For two elements NumPy's x86-64 build computes the pair with its operands the other way round (the earlier of two zeros, the later of two NaNs); the kernel and the naive reference follow it, and the spec records it as a note outside its domain, which the judge does not check.
- **From Python,** `uv run delivery time --only accumulate` at a load average of about 1: `np.maximum.accumulate(x)` on int64 is 2.6x faster at 16 elements, 11.6x at 4,096 and 6.6x at 1,048,576 (5.3x in the recorded run on a quiet machine); `np.fmax.accumulate(x)` on float64 2.8x, 6.8x and 5.0x (7.8x and 5.2x at the two larger sizes when the read looked for signalling NaNs only, and 8.2x and 5.5x in the recorded run on a quiet machine, which was taken then). At 16 elements most of what is left is the method call; NumPy takes about 220 ns there whatever the size. The float64 figures include the read of the array before the kernel, about 0.5 µs of the 1.17 µs at 4,096 elements (the judge has the kernel alone at 0.59 µs). The flags the kernel's `vmaxpd` sets for a NaN are cleared after it, as after `clip`, since NumPy's own loop leaves none set; the first version of the binding did not, and `delivery check` now has cases that read the invalid flag after the call. Other ufuncs' `accumulate` pass through the hook after two pointer comparisons: `np.add.accumulate(x)` on int64 within 2% of NumPy at every size.
- **The judge's runs were on a quiet machine** (load average about 1).
- **On ARM64 both kernels have assembly.** The float64 one could be written only once its spec was narrowed to what the binding passes ([fmax-references-design.md](fmax-references-design.md)): before that its reference followed NumPy's x86-64 build for which of two equal zeros a running maximum keeps, NumPy's ARM64 build keeps the other, and the judge stopped at the reference ([issue 162](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/162)).
- **Technique, `cummax_i64` (ARM64).** NEON has no maximum of 64-bit lanes either: one is a `cmgt` and a `bit`, and the scalar one a `cmp` and a `csel`, which on one chain is the naive C's two cycles per element, 0.65 ns, on the Apple M1 Pro. The kernel keeps x86-64's three ways through a block of 16 elements, *flat*, *rising* and *general*, and its rules for which to try next, and differs in three places. The general block is done in the vector registers, not in the integer ones: a prefix maximum of the block in Sklansky steps (each vector against the input one element back, then the odd vectors against the one before, then two against one, then four against three), with M brought into vector 3 before the last step and into vectors 0 to 2 separately, so the chain from block to block is two maxima and a `dup`; 48 vector operations against 56 `cmp` and `csel` for the same block in the integer registers. The first block of the array is the same prefix maximum without M, and after it the flat loop is tried unless the block ends on M. The rising test builds half its vectors one element back with `ext` and loads the other half, since all-loaded and all-`ext` were each slower. The output is not brought to a multiple of 32 bytes first and nothing is stored past the caches; the flat and rising loops store with `stnp`, which on this processor still goes through the caches.
- **Figures (ARM64),** recorded with `uv run judge run cummax_i64 --record` on the Apple M1 Pro on a quiet machine, at a load average of 2.1 to 2.9, at the top of the stack that added the ARM64 kernels: 3.17x [3.16 – 3.18] over the naive C on `random`, 3.37x [3.33 – 3.38] on `rising`, 3.27x [3.24 – 3.28] over both, and 18.8x [18.67 – 18.87] over NumPy. 1.46x at 16 elements, where one vector block replaces 16 steps of the chain; 2.1x (`random`) and 3.1x (`rising`) at 64; 3.8x to 5.8x from 256 to 16,384; 3.4x to 3.5x at 65,536; 2.15x at 262,144; 2.8x to 3.2x from 1,048,576 up. Of two runs on the quiet machine this is the second; the first read 3.18x [3.14 – 3.19], with 2.5x at 65,536 and 2.1x at 262,144. The kernel's pull request recorded 3.49x [3.46 – 3.49] and 20.0x at a load average of about 3.0, with 3.6x to 3.8x at 65,536 and 262,144. With the output 16 bytes behind the input there is no stall: the kernel is as fast there as with the output clear, or faster (1.17x over the table in the recorded run, 0.99x at 1,048,576).
- **What limits it (ARM64).** Inside the first-level cache the flat loop takes 0.10 ns per element, about 5.3 cycles per block, held by its eight 16-byte stores more than by its 17 vector operations (outside the judge, dropping half of the stores gained 15%, half of the compares under 5%), and the rising loop 0.12. From the second-level cache out it runs at the speed of a copy: 0.17 to 0.18 ns per element at 65,536 elements and 0.19 to 0.22 from 1,048,576, against the naive C's 0.62 to 0.65 at every size. At 262,144 the figure moves between runs: 0.17 ns per element in the kernel's pull request, with another kernel run in progress, and 0.29 in both runs on the quiet machine (0.26 in the recorded run's second table, where no NumPy call comes between).
- **Where it is slower than the naive C (ARM64).** On data where a new maximum is at a random half of the elements, outside the judge, at 2,000,000 elements: 0.69 ns per element against the naive C's 0.63. Every block of such data is general, its last element is M half the time, and the branch that tries the rising loop after it goes either way at random once the array is too long for the predictor to learn, and the rising test then fails. At 16,384 elements, which the predictor learns, the same data takes 0.35. Trying the rising loop only after a block that rose throughout (attempt 11 of [the ARM64 log](../kernels/cummax_i64/arm64/attempts.md)) brings it to 0.33 ns at 2,000,000 elements, but costs the judge's `random` data 8% at 64 elements and 3% at 256 (0.996x overall), and data where every block is general goes from 0.28 to 0.33 ns per element; it is not in the kernel. Every other kind of data measured is 1.4x to 6.3x faster than the naive C: random, sorted and constant data 0.10 to 0.12 ns per element at 16,384, a noisy rising trend 0.22, 1 0 2 1 3 2 ... 0.29.
- **Compared with x86-64.** The x86-64 kernel keeps the general block in the integer registers; on the Apple M1 Pro the vector one is faster from the second general block on (attempt 10 of the ARM64 log: 1.27x at 64 random elements), though the first try, with M brought in before the prefix steps, made the chain from block to block about 30 cycles long and lost 12% at 256. x86-64 keeps stores past the caches from 24 MB of output; on this processor `stnp` gave no gain there, as for `cumsum_i64`, and none of its stores bypass the caches. The slow case found on x86-64 is a different one: 1 0 2 1 3 2 ..., which the naive C's branches there predict, where its kernel is 0.7x of the naive C.
- **Technique, `cumfmax_f64` (ARM64).** `fmaxnm` is the NaN-ignoring maximum in one instruction, scalar and vector, and NumPy's ARM64 loop is a chain of it from `x[0]`. On the spec's domain (no `-0.0`, one NaN bit pattern, no signalling NaN) any order of it gives that chain's bits, the all-NaN prefix included, so none of the x86-64 kernel's turning NaN into minus infinity or treating leading NaNs apart is needed. The kernel has `cummax_i64`'s three ways through a block of 16 elements, from `x[0]`, with M the maximum before the block. *General:* each vector against the input one element back, Sklansky steps over the eight vectors, then every vector against M; the next M is M against the block's last lane, so the chain from block to block is one `fmaxnm`, where `cummax_i64` needs two maxima and a `dup`. The first block takes `x[0]` for the element before it and skips the step against M. *Flat:* a tree of seven `fmaxnm` and an `fmaxnmp` gives the block's maximum, and if it leaves M as it was the block is M sixteen times. *Rising:* if the element before the block is M and every element is `fcmge` the one before it (a NaN fails), the block is the input. After a general block the next is tried rising if the block ends on M and flat otherwise; a block that fails a test goes the general way. While every element so far is NaN the flat test's compare is unordered, so such a prefix goes the general way. The flat and rising loops store with `stnp`. Under 16 elements, and after the last block, one vector of two elements at a time against the element before, then one scalar.
- **Figures, `cumfmax_f64` (ARM64),** recorded with `uv run judge run cumfmax_f64 --record --unlocked` on the Apple M1 Pro on a quiet machine at the top of the stack, at a load average of 2.0 to 2.1: 13.8x [13.49 – 13.83] over the naive C across the judge's sizes and three distributions (`random` 13.6x, `rising` 13.9x, 5% NaN 13.9x) and 28.0x [27.29 – 28.03] over NumPy. 5.3x at 16 elements, 12x to 14x at 64, 20x to 26x from 256 to 16,384, 13x to 15x at 65,536, 11x to 13x at 1,048,576 and 8.5x to 13x from 4,194,304 up. At 262,144 the figure is either about 9x or about 14x, by distribution and from one run to the next (two quiet runs: 9.3x, 8.6x and 14.8x, then 9.4x, 13.8x and 9.3x), which looks like where the buffers land in the second-level cache; the second run is the recorded one. The kernel's pull request, at a load average of about 12, had 14.4x over the naive C and 29.2x over NumPy. The naive C takes about 2.5 ns per element at every size: it writes the comparisons out to keep the later of two equal values, as NumPy's x86-64 build does, and clang makes that a chain of compares and selects through the running value. The survey's loop for this call, `m = fmax(m, x[i])`, compiles to one `fmaxnm` per element and took 0.64 ns, which is why the survey had NumPy 5.5x behind the C and the judge has it about 1.4x behind; against the survey's loop the kernel is about 6x faster from 256 to 16,384 elements.
- **What limits `cumfmax_f64` (ARM64).** The stores. From 256 to 16,384 elements the flat and rising loops take 0.10 to 0.12 ns per element, about 5.4 cycles a block on the Apple M1 Pro, the speed of a plain copy in the first-level cache (outside the judge four `ldp` and four `stp` of 128 bytes take 5.1 to 5.6 cycles); a general block, 33 vector operations, about 10. From the second-level cache out it runs at the speed of a copy, 0.17 ns per element at 65,536 and 0.18 to 0.23 from 1,048,576. `stnp` gained 1.36x at 16,384 elements, where the input fills the 128 KB first-level cache, and nothing elsewhere. Prefetching 1,024 bytes ahead lost (0.986x), a flat test comparing the tree with M as a vector and `umaxv` gained nothing (1.000x), and a rising test that loads all its shifted vectors lost (0.987x), as on `cummax_i64` ([attempts.md](../kernels/cumfmax_f64/arm64/attempts.md)). With the output 16 bytes behind the input there is no stall: 1.07x [1.06 – 1.08] over the same placement apart in the kernel's pull request, and 1.57x [1.57 – 1.58] in the quiet recorded run, where the close placement is 1.8x to 2.8x faster from 4,096 to 262,144 elements and level from 1,048,576 up (a placement effect in the caches that favours the kernel, not a stall).
- **An output that is not a multiple of 16 bytes costs `cumfmax_f64` (ARM64).** The blocks start at `x[0]` and keep the buffers' alignment. A version that started them at `x[1]`, 8 bytes off, had every other 32-byte `stp` cross a cache line, and its flat block took 7.6 cycles, not 5.4. Outside the judge, with `x` at a page boundary and `out` at a page boundary plus 0, 16, 32 and 8 bytes, 4,096 random elements take 0.094, 0.088, 0.099 and 0.155 ns per element. The binding allocates the output itself with NumPy's allocator, which gave a multiple of 16 bytes for every one of several thousand arrays tried, so a caller's slice of `x` does not put `out` there. The kernel does not bring the output to a boundary first: the judge times aligned buffers and could not credit it.
- **Compared with x86-64 (`cumfmax_f64`).** x86-64 has no NaN-ignoring maximum and pays per element to make one; with `fmaxnm` the prefix maximum is cheap, and the flat and rising loops of `cummax_i64`, which the x86-64 float kernel left untried, give the rest. x86-64 brings the output to a multiple of 32 bytes and stores past the caches from 24 MB; this kernel does neither, and prefetching, which x86-64 kept, lost here.
- **What a caller must know (`cumfmax_f64`, ARM64).** `out` must not overlap `x`, and outside the spec's domain the kernel does not give NumPy's answer. Measured against `np.fmax.accumulate` on this machine, arrays of 1 to 80 elements: with zeros of both signs 1,035 of 3,000 arrays differ, because the flat test takes `+0.0 == -0.0` for "M unchanged" and the rising test takes `-0.0 >= +0.0` for rising, so a `-0.0` can stand where NumPy's chain has `+0.0`. With NaNs of several bit patterns 371 of 3,000 differ, and 1,796 of 2,000 that start with NaNs of mixed patterns: in the all-NaN prefix the kernel gives a later element's NaN where NumPy keeps `x[0]`'s, since `fmaxnm` of two NaNs returns its first operand and the prefix steps put the later element first. Swapping those operands would probably match NumPy at no cost; it was not tried, since removing a caveat is not a kernel run's to decide. One NaN pattern, the negative one included, gave no difference: the default-NaN mode is off. With a signalling NaN 2,619 of 3,000 differ: NumPy's output becomes a quiet NaN and the maximum so far is forgotten, and the kernel mostly ignores it. The binding's read sends every such array to NumPy, so no call reaches these cases. `fmaxnm` raises the invalid flag for a signalling NaN only.
- **From Python (`cumfmax_f64`, ARM64),** `uv run delivery time --only accumulate` on the Apple M1 Pro at a load average of 11.5: `np.fmax.accumulate(x)` on float64 is 2.8x faster than NumPy at 16 elements (267 ns to 95 ns), 7.3x at 4,096 (14.7 µs to 2.01 µs) and 6.8x at 1,048,576 (3.69 ms to 540 µs), where with the C it was 2.04x, 1.22x and 1.20x. **Most of the call is now the binding's read, not the kernel.** Before the kernel the array is read for signalling NaNs, negative zeros and NaN bit patterns, by a plain C loop on ARM64. The call less the kernel's time in the judge is 1.45 µs at 4,096 elements (about 0.35 ns per element, some 90 ns of it the call) and 304 µs at 1,048,576 (0.29 ns per element); the read alone, timed outside the judge, takes 0.35 to 0.36 ns per element at both sizes, three times the kernel's 0.11 to 0.14. On x86-64 the same read is compiled for AVX2 and costs about 0.12. Without it the call at 4,096 elements would take about 0.6 µs, some 25x faster than NumPy.
- **The read made cheaper (ARM64).** The binding now reads the array on ARM64 a block of 32 elements at a time by the top 16 bits of each element, gathered with two `uzp2` per eight: they say whether the block holds a NaN or an infinity, or a top halfword of `0x8000`, which a negative zero has. Only such a block is read again for the NaN bit patterns or for negative zeros, so the answer is the plain loop's for every input; the x86-64 code is unchanged. Timed alone, it takes 0.074 ns per element with no NaN, where the loop took 0.350, and 0.22 with 5% of the elements NaN (Apple M1 Pro, first-level cache; 0.098 and 0.30 from the second-level cache). From Python, at a load average of 2.1, `np.fmax.accumulate(x)` on float64 went from 1.89 µs to 768 ns at 4,096 elements (7.5x to 18.5x faster than NumPy) and from 546 µs to 285 µs at 1,048,576 (6.6x to 12.6x); 16 elements are unchanged at 2.8x. Recorded at the top of the stack with `uv run delivery time --record` (load average 2.2, [delivery/results/apple-m1-pro.md](../delivery/results/apple-m1-pro.md)): 2.85x at 16 elements, 18.7x at 4,096 (14.3 µs to 764 ns) and 12.8x at 1,048,576 (3.59 ms to 282 µs).
- **What a caller must know (ARM64):** nothing beyond the spec: `out` must not overlap `x`. The judge times only multiples of 16 elements; the 0 to 15 elements after the last block go through groups of four in the integer registers and then one at a time, which `check` verifies at every count and does not time.
- **From Python (ARM64),** `uv run delivery time --only accumulate` at a load average of 2.8: `np.maximum.accumulate(x)` on int64 is 2.9x faster than NumPy at 16 elements, 18.5x at 4,096 and 14.5x at 1,048,576 (176 µs against 2.55 ms). `np.fmax.accumulate(x)` on float64, served by the C, 2.1x, 1.2x and 1.2x.

#### Maximum by runs: `np.fmax.reduceat`

`np.fmax.reduceat(x, starts)` gives, for each start, the NaN-ignoring maximum of the run from it to the next start. NumPy calls its inner loop once per run, at about 13 ns a run on data without NaN and 50 ns with 5% NaN, so on runs of 16 elements it takes 0.8 to 3.4 ns per element whatever the array's size. The kernel, `fmax_reduce_f64`, is the maximum of one run (`np.fmax.reduce(x)`, n at least 1), and the binding calls it once per run: the judge cannot describe a kernel whose second buffer holds indices into the first, and a call costs a few nanoseconds against a run of 16. NumPy gives a run of `reduceat` the bits `np.fmax.reduce` gives the same elements.

- **What NumPy returns is fixed by its loop in two cases.** The value of a maximum does not depend on the order of the elements; the sign of a zero maximum when zeros of both signs are present does, and so does which NaN a run of NaNs returns. NumPy's AVX2 loop for this reduction has four running maxima, one per lane, all starting at `x[0]`, takes the elements from `x[1]` four at a time, combines the lanes, and takes the `(n - 1) mod 4` leftover elements one at a time through the C library's `fmax`. In a lane the later of two equal values is kept, among the leftover elements the earlier. The naive reference reproduces that loop, found by experiment on this machine ([attempts.md](../kernels/fmax_reduce_f64/x86_64/attempts.md)), and the kernel matches it. The spec records the loop as a note outside its domain: since [fmax-references-design.md](fmax-references-design.md) the domain is what the binding passes for one run (no `-0.0` when the maximum is a zero, NaNs of one pattern when every element is NaN, no signalling NaN), and its `prepare` makes every `-0.0` a `+0.0` in a generated array whose maximum is a zero. On that domain every correct maximum has one bit pattern, so the judge checks the same reference on every architecture. On ARM64 NumPy's loop is one `fmaxnm` per element from `x[0]`: a zero maximum is `+0.0` if any `+0.0` is present, and an all-NaN array gives `x[0]`. **A NumPy that runs its AVX-512 loop, with eight lanes, answers those two cases differently, and NumPy picks the loop when it starts, by the processor.** So the binding does not rely on the kernel there: when `x` holds a negative zero and a result is a zero, or holds NaNs of more than one bit pattern and a result is a NaN, the whole call goes to NumPy. Everywhere else every correct maximum is one bit pattern, and the result is NumPy's whichever loop it runs.
- **Technique (x86-64).** Two ways through the array. The quick way, from five elements, takes the elements in any order, some twice: `vmaxpd v, m` returns m when v is a NaN, so with the maximum so far as m and minus infinity to start, one instruction per vector ignores NaN, and the bits of every element are or-ed together beside it. Its result is NumPy's unless it is a zero and some element has its sign bit set, or it is minus infinity (perhaps a run of NaNs); then NumPy's way, the four lanes, is taken from the start. 5 to 16 elements are four loads, two at the ends and two between; 17 to 127 blocks of four vectors, the last ending with the array; from 128 blocks of eight vectors into eight maxima, placed on 32-byte boundaries after the first. Up to four elements a masked load (`vmaskmovpd`) and a reduction keeping the earlier of equal values. None of these paths branches on n within its range, which matters when every call has another length.
- **What limits it.** Up to 16 elements the count of instructions, about 30: 1.5 ns. From 128 to 4,096 elements the ports, 16 `vmaxpd` and `vorpd` per 64 elements, 0.033 ns per element; 0.040 in the second-level cache, where NumPy is at 0.10. Data whose maximum is a zero with a sign bit somewhere, and runs of NaN, take both ways: twice the time. 3.4x over the naive C at 16 elements, 13x to 14x from 1,024 to 4,096, 2.2x in memory; 6.63x across the judge's sizes.
- **The binding.** The starts are read once each, in the loop over the runs, and tested there against `[0, n)`; one outside sends the call to NumPy, which raises. A run of one element, and a start not below the next, is copied without a call. The array is read for signalling NaNs (below) 2,048 elements at a time, just ahead of the runs that use them, so the kernel then reads them from the first-level cache; reading the whole array first made runs of 1,024 in 262,144 elements 0.6x of NumPy. The two other guards read the results, one per run, and read the array again only if a result is a zero or a NaN.
- **What a caller must know.** n at least 1. No signalling NaN: NumPy's leftover elements go through the C library's `fmax`, which answers one with a quiet NaN, and the kernel skips it; the binding sends an array that holds one to NumPy. The invalid-operation flag is set after any NaN, quiet ones included, and the binding clears MXCSR's flags after the runs. The masked load is fast on this processor and slow on some other x86-64 processors, and slow anywhere when a lane it masks off lies in a page that cannot be read (an array of at most three elements ending within 24 bytes of unmapped memory).
- **From Python,** `uv run delivery time --only reduceat` at a load average of about 0.5, on runs of 16 with 5% NaN: 2.7x faster at 16 elements (one run, 350 ns to 132 ns), 12.7x at 4,096 and 10.9x at 1,048,576. NumPy is slow on NaN here; on the same runs without NaN, measured the same way outside the tool, 2.9x at 4,096 and 2.2x at 1,048,576. The gain falls with the length of the runs: at 4,096 elements 2.2x on runs of 64 and 1.5x on runs of 1,024; at 262,144 elements 1.7x on runs of 64, level on runs of 512, and 0.8x on runs of 4,096, where NumPy reads the array once at 0.1 ns per element and the binding reads it once for signalling NaNs and once more from the first-level cache. `np.add.reduceat` passes through the hook after one pointer comparison, within 1.3% of NumPy.
- **The judge's run was on a quiet machine** (load average about 0.9).
- **Technique (ARM64).** One way through the array, not two. `fmaxnm` is the NaN-ignoring maximum in one instruction, and within the spec's domain every order of it gives the same bits: it returns `+0.0` for `+0.0` and `-0.0` in either order, and of two quiet NaNs the first operand. So the elements are taken in any order, some twice. Up to 32 elements there is no loop: the first and the last 2, 4, 8 or 16 elements are loaded as vectors that overlap in the middle and folded by a tree of `fmaxnm` and one `fmaxnmp`, the path chosen by the bits of n - 1. From 33 elements eight two-lane accumulators start as the first 16 elements and take 16 elements per step, and the last step is the last 16 elements, overlapping the one before it. NumPy's ARM64 loop is the same instruction in a chain from `x[0]`.
- **What limits it (ARM64).** On the Apple M1 Pro the loads: three 16-byte loads per cycle, so 2.67 cycles per 16 elements, and the loop takes 2.8 (0.054 ns per element from 1,024 to 16,384 elements). 1.3 ns at 16 elements, 9.6x over the naive C, which is NumPy's four-lane x86-64 loop compiled for this processor and runs at about 0.63 ns per element, the speed of a scalar chain; 11.9x from 1,024 to 16,384; 6.6x at 1,048,576 and 4.7x at 16,777,216, where the kernel runs at the speed of the caches and of memory and is level with NumPy (1.02x and 1.00x). 8.24x [8.24 – 8.24] across the judge's sizes on both distributions, 4.62x over NumPy, recorded `--unlocked` on a quiet machine at the top of the stack (load average 2.0 to 2.3); the kernel's pull request, with another kernel run in progress, had 8.23x and 4.66x. The x86-64 attempts that the M1 Pro did not repeat were about NaN and order, which `fmaxnm` makes unnecessary; of the ones it did try, `ldnp`, a loop on 64-byte boundaries, software prefetch, two steps per trip, a branchless path for 9 to 32 elements and `ld1` of four registers all gave nothing or lost ([attempts.md](../kernels/fmax_reduce_f64/arm64/attempts.md)). x86-64 kept its blocks on 32-byte boundaries; the loop on 64-byte boundaries was not faster here.
- **What a caller must know (ARM64).** n at least 1, and no signalling NaN, as on x86-64: `fmaxnm` answers one with a quiet NaN where it meets it in its tree, so the result depends on where it lies (of 2,490 arrays with one among numbers, 440 differed from NumPy). Outside the rest of the domain the kernel gives NumPy's ARM64 bits: on 2,490 arrays with zeros of both signs at a zero maximum and 2,490 all-NaN arrays of random quiet payloads and signs, 1 to 4,097 elements, none differed. The second holds because every `fmaxnm` in the kernel has the accumulator that began as `x[0]` as its first operand and `fmaxnmp` takes lane 0 first, so an all-NaN run returns `x[0]`, as NumPy's chain does; a rewrite that changes the operand order loses it. So on ARM64 the binding's guards for a negative zero and for NaN patterns are not needed by this kernel; they are kept, since they are shared with x86-64 and the spec's domain is written from them. When they fire on a result they cost a read of the array: on runs of 16, one all-NaN run takes a 1,048,576-element call from 295 µs to 538 µs and one zero result to 419 µs, still faster than NumPy's 688 µs. No flag is left set: `fmaxnm` raises the invalid flag only for a signalling NaN.
- **From Python (ARM64),** `uv run delivery time --only reduceat` at a load average of 2.6, on runs of 16 with 5% NaN: 3.5x faster at 16 elements (323 ns to 92 ns), 2.4x at 4,096 and 2.4x at 1,048,576 (703 µs to 298 µs), where with the C the call was 0.77x and 0.70x of NumPy. Runs of random length from 8 to 24 gain the same, 2.3x to 2.4x. **Long runs are slower than NumPy on ARM64:** measured the same way outside the tool, on runs of 64 the call is 1.1x at 4,096 elements and 0.94x at 262,144 and 1,048,576; on runs of 256 to 4,096, 0.57x to 0.70x at 4,096 elements and 0.46x to 0.59x at 262,144 and 1,048,576, with or without NaN. The kernel is not the cause, being level with NumPy over a whole array: the binding reads the array for signalling NaNs first, and that read, the plain C loop shared with `np.fmax.accumulate`, costs about 0.12 to 0.15 ns per element on the Apple M1 Pro (the call's time less the kernel's on runs of 4,096), more than the kernel's 0.054, where on x86-64 it is compiled for AVX2 and costs about 0.05.
- **The read made cheaper (ARM64).** The read for signalling NaNs now takes a block of 32 elements at a time by the top 16 bits of each, which are those of an infinity or a signalling NaN exactly when shifted left by one and added to `0x20` they fall below `0x10`; a quiet NaN does not, so data with NaN takes the same path. Only a block with an infinity or a signalling NaN is read element by element. Timed alone: 0.062 ns per element in the first-level cache, where the plain loop took 0.156, and 0.098 from the second-level cache; the x86-64 code is unchanged. From Python, at a load average of 2.0 to 2.3, `uv run delivery time --only reduceat` on runs of 16 with 5% NaN went from 2.4x faster than NumPy to 3.6x at 4,096 elements (1.24 µs to 834 ns) and from 2.3x to 3.1x at 1,048,576 (296 µs to 224 µs); recorded at the top of the stack with `uv run delivery time --record` (load average 2.2): 3.46x at 16 elements, 3.57x at 4,096 and 3.05x at 1,048,576 (692 µs to 226 µs). On longer runs, NumPy ÷ hotkernels with `timeit`, best of 15, before and after:

#### Any by rows: `m.any(axis=1)`

`m.any(axis=1)` says for each row of a two-dimensional boolean array whether it holds a True. NumPy pays a cost per row: on rows of 22 it takes 0.6 to 0.7 ns per element at every size on the Intel Core i5-14600KF, and 700 ns for a call with no row at all. The kernel, `any_rows_bool`, takes the matrix as `n` elements and a row width `cols`, writes one result per whole row and returns how many rows there were; the judge needed no new shape for it, the width being a scalar argument with a set of values the judge sweeps. The naive C is clang's loop over a row, vectorised 32 bytes wide, which pays its entry and exit on every row: 0.082 ns per element on rows of 22, 0.20 on rows of 4.

- **Technique (x86-64).** No division: a row is taken while `cols` elements are left, and the count returned is how far the output has advanced. The path is chosen once per call by the width. Rows of 1 to 32: one 32-byte load at the row's start, `vptest` against a vector whose first `cols` bytes are set, and `setnz` to the output, eight rows per step; the rows that start in the last 31 bytes are read from the bits of the last 32 bytes (`vpmovmskb`), and a matrix of fewer than 32 elements is gathered a byte at a time. Rows of 4 have a path of their own: a row is a 32-bit lane, and four vectors of rows are packed to one of results by three packs with signed saturation and a minimum with 1, each vector put together from two 16-byte loads 64 bytes apart so that no permute is needed. Rows of 33 to 64: the row's first 32 bytes or'ed with its last 32, four rows per step. Longer rows: 32-byte chunks or'ed together, the last ending at the row's end.
- **What limits it.** Rows of up to 32: one `vptest` per row, which needs port 0, and one `setnz` per row, which needs port 0 or 6 as the loop's branch does; 0.26 ns per row on rows of 22 in the first-level cache, about 1.4 cycles. Two ways around one flag per row (two rows per `vptest` through the zero and carry flags, and byte sums with `pext` and `pdep`) cost more vector instructions than they saved. Rows of 4 run at about four cycles per 128 elements: eight loads, or three packs on port 5. Over the naive C: 5.0x on rows of 22, 17.6x on rows of 4 and 3.8x on rows of 64 across the judge's sizes, 6.94x over all six tables. Rows of 1, 2 and 8, and narrow odd widths such as 3, 5 and 7, have no path of their own and take the one of rows up to 32, at about 1.4 cycles per row whatever the width: still far ahead of NumPy, but the judge times only widths of 22, 4 and 64, so a path for them could not be kept by `compare`. The kept attempt for rows of 4 that packs without a compare (attempt 6 in [attempts.md](../kernels/any_rows_bool/x86_64/attempts.md)) is a small gain, 1.002x, whose verdict was taken twice.
- **The binding.** Two hooks reach it, because NumPy routes the two spellings differently: the method hook of `any` takes `m.any(axis=1)`, and a function hook takes `np.any(m, axis=1)`, which for a plain `ndarray` calls `np.logical_or.reduce` itself and never reaches the method. `m.any()` with no argument, one of the commonest NumPy calls, goes to NumPy at the method hook's first test. The matrix is read for bytes other than 0 and 1 before the kernel sees it, 16,384 bytes at a time, each block just before the kernel reads it from the first-level cache; the check is compiled for AVX2 (the rule of `hk_has_signalling_nan`). It costs about 0.007 ns per byte where the kernel costs about 0.012 on rows of 22: from Python, `m.any(axis=1)` on a million elements takes 18.4 µs with it and 11.5 µs without it. It is bound by reading the second-level cache: a version with four accumulators of 32 bytes in place of the one the compiler makes was under 2% faster, and was not kept.
- **What a caller must know.** `cols` at least 1; `out` does not overlap `m`. Every mask byte is 0 or 1, and on x86-64 this is required, not a formality: a byte above 1 counts as True on every path but one, the path of fewer than 32 elements with a width other than 4, where the bits are gathered by shifting the bytes themselves and a byte above 1 spills into the bits of the rows beside it. The binding sends a matrix holding such a byte to NumPy.
- **From Python,** `uv run delivery time --only any` at a load average of about 0.4, on rows of 22 with 5% of the elements True: `m.any(axis=1)` is 5.1x faster at 16 elements (one row of 22, 510 ns to 99 ns), 16.2x at 4,096 and 42.2x at 1,048,576; `np.any(m, axis=1)` 8.0x, 16.8x and 42.2x. `m.any()` on a one-dimensional array passes through the hook 4 to 5 ns slower than NumPy (1.3% to 1.9%), as `x.sum()` passes through its own; `m.any(axis=0)` within 1.3% from 4,096 elements, and 20 ns at 16.
- **Technique (ARM64).** NEON has no `vptest` or `pmovmskb`, and on the Apple M1 Pro moving one result per row from a vector register to memory costs more than reducing it. So rows are reduced in the vector registers and their results stored sixteen at a time. Each row of 16 to 32 elements becomes 16 bytes, its first 16 or'ed with its last 16 (two overlapping loads), and a row of 33 to 64 its first 32 or'ed with its last 32; fifteen `umaxp` then turn sixteen such rows into the sixteen result bytes, in order, for one 16-byte store. The bytes being 0 or 1, the largest is the answer and no compare is needed. The sixteen rows are addressed from four positions four rows apart and fixed offsets of one, two and three `cols`, so the loads do not wait on a chain of adds; that alone was 1.46x to 1.55x on rows of 22 in the first-level cache. Rows of 4 have a path of their own: 64 bytes are sixteen rows, and three `umaxp` leave one byte per row, 32 rows per step. In every path the rows left under a block are done by one more block that ends at the last row and stores some results a second time, and a matrix of fewer than 16 rows by groups of 1, 2, 4 and 8 rows. From 8 MB the main loops use non-temporal loads (`ldnp`); rows of 16 to 32 then load each row as one pair from its start and mask the second half, since `ldnp` has no register offset. The width 4 is tested before the division, and its row count is `n >> 2`. The widths the judge does not time have paths of their own, set out below: a copy for width 1, a general register per row for 2 to 15, the block of sixteen rows up to 128, and an exit at the first True above that.
- **Figures (ARM64),** recorded with `uv run judge run any_rows_bool --record` on the Apple M1 Pro on a quiet machine, at a load average of 1.6 to 1.9, at the top of the stack that added the ARM64 kernels: 7.71x [7.71 – 7.71] over the naive C and 98.6x over NumPy over all six tables. Rows of 22: 7.62x (sparse) and 7.60x (dense), 12.9x at 4,096 elements, 8.68x at 1,048,576. Rows of 4: 35.5x for both, 68.2x at 4,096. Rows of 64: 1.70x for both, 2.52x at 4,096 and 1.48x at 1,048,576. The kernel's pull request recorded 7.67x and 99.9x at a load average of 15 to 20, with other kernel runs in progress; no figure moved by more than 2%. At 16 elements rows of 22 and 64 hold no row and both versions take the 1.3 ns of a call. With the output 16 bytes behind the input it is not slower: 1.00x [1.00 – 1.00] over the table.
- **What limits it (ARM64).** In the first-level cache, the loads: the M1 Pro does three 16-byte loads a cycle. Rows of 22 take two a row, 0.23 ns or about 0.74 cycles a row; rows of 64 take four, 1.33 cycles; rows of 4 eight per 32 rows. From 262,144 elements, what the second-level cache delivers: about 12 to 16 µs per million elements for all three widths. Rows of 64 stay at 1.7x because clang's loop is already good on long rows: 0.018 ns per element, against 0.13 on rows of 22 and 0.48 on rows of 4. Prefetching four blocks ahead was 0.92x on rows of 22, the prefetches taking load slots from a loop limited by its loads. Non-temporal loads everywhere were 1.16x at 16,777,216 elements and 0.88x at 1,048,576, which fits the 12 MB second-level cache, hence the threshold of 8 MB. The x86-64 kernel's step of eight rows with one flag per row has no counterpart here: the result of a NEON reduction has no cheap way to a flag or a general register, which is why the ARM64 kernel works in blocks of sixteen rows.
- **Widths the judge does not time (ARM64).** The judge checks seventeen widths and times three (22, 4 and 64), so `compare` cannot find a change to any other width's path significantly faster. The paths for those widths were kept on three conditions instead: `check` passing, `compare` against the kernel without them not significantly slower on the timed tables (1.000x [0.999 – 1.002]), and a timer outside the repository showing each path faster than the naive C at its width. Width 1: the result is the matrix, so a copy, 32 bytes per step. Widths 2, 3 and 5 to 15: a row in a general register, from two loads that start at its start and end at its end (8 bytes each for 8 to 15, 4 for 5 to 7, 2 and 1 for 2 and 3), or'ed and compared with zero, four rows per step. Widths 65 to 128: the block of sixteen rows of the widths 33 to 64, with the row's first 64 bytes or'ed with its last 64, and no exit: an exit after the first 64 bytes left sparse rows of 65 at 1.05x, the branch deciding it mispredicted. Widths from 129: one row at a time, 64 bytes per step, ending at the first step that holds a True, the last 64 bytes ending at the row's end. Against `naive.c` compiled as the judge compiles it (`clang -O3 -mcpu=native`), best of 15 repetitions, on data with 3%, 50% and no element True, on a quiet machine at a load average of 2.1 to 2.2 (the kernel's pull request took the same table at 16 to 20, with other kernel runs in progress, and no figure in it differs from this one by more than a tenth):

#### Counting codes: `np.bincount` on int16 and int64

`np.bincount(x)` counts how often each value from 0 to the largest appears. NumPy converts an int16 array to 64-bit integers, reads it for its smallest and largest values, and counts one element at a time: 0.78 ns per element at 262,144 int16 codes on the Intel Core i5-14600KF. The kernels, `bincount_i16` and `bincount_i64`, take the number of counters `bins` as an argument, zero the counters themselves and ignore any element outside `[0, bins)`, so they are safe on any input; the binding sizes `bins` from the data, so every element it sends is inside. The naive C is clang's loop, unrolled four times, of a load, a compare with `bins`, a branch and an increment in memory: 0.20 to 0.25 ns per element on uniform codes, 0.37 on four distinct values, where an increment waits for the previous one of the same counter.

- **Technique (x86-64).** Three paths, chosen once per call. Below 8,192 elements, and with more than 1,024 counters, the naive C's loop, unrolled four times, with the counters cleared two 32-byte stores at a time. From 8,192 elements with at most 64 counters, a table of pairs: 64 by 64 counters of 32 bits, 16 KB below the stack pointer, one increment per two elements, so that equal neighbouring elements wait for one another a sixteenth as often; an 8-byte load of int16 (four `or` of int64) tests four elements at once for being in 0..63. From 8,192 elements with 65 to 1,024 counters, a second table of `bins` counters below the stack pointer takes every other element, as `bincount_u8` does with four, but only when at least 32 of the first 256 elements equal their successor: on data that does not repeat the second table was slower than the C (0.90x to 0.95x). The pair table is kept below 2^32 elements, where no 32-bit counter can overflow.
- **What limits it.** One counter load and store per element on the paths without the pair table, where the kernel ties the C (1.00x to 1.13x). The pair table takes 0.15 ns per element for int16 and about 0.21 for int64 on both kinds of data; from 4,194,304 int64 elements reading 32 MB and more of input is most of the time, and the gain shrinks to 1.1x to 1.4x. Over the naive C across the four tables: 1.26x for int16 and 1.16x for int64; uniform codes into 1,000 counters 1.04x and 1.01x.
- **A verdict for a person.** The margin over the C is small: 1.26x and 1.16x overall, nothing below 8,192 elements, and most of it from the pair table on at most 64 counters. As for `bincount_u8`, keeping the assembly rather than shipping the C is for a reviewer to confirm.
- **The binding.** The function hook of `np.bincount`, beside `bincount_u8`. A contiguous one-dimensional int16 or int64 array of at least one element, no `weights`, `minlength` absent or a Python int of at least 0. NumPy sizes its result by the largest element and raises for a negative one, so the array is read once first for its largest element as an unsigned number, where a negative element reads as too large: one maximum answers both questions. The scan cannot go a block at a time ahead of the kernel, as the guards of `np.fmax.reduceat` and `m.any(axis=1)` do, because the kernel needs `bins` before its first element. It is compiled for AVX2 with four running maxima in 32-byte vectors: written as a loop over elements the compiler kept one maximum, and for int64, which has no vector maximum in AVX2, a compare and a blend in a chain through every four elements held it to about 0.3 ns per element, as long as the kernel; with four it costs about 0.1 ns per int64 element from the third-level cache. On ARM64 the scan is NEON with eight running maxima of 16 bytes: for int16 an unsigned maximum per eight elements, 0.014 ns per element in the first-level cache where the 32-byte vectors took 0.023, and 0.12 at 16 elements where their scalar reduction took 2.1 (so `np.bincount` on 16 int16 elements went from 115 ns to 81 ns from Python); for int64, which NEON has no 64-bit maximum for either, a 32-bit maximum per two elements, whose high words' maximum of 0 means every element is below 2^32 and the low words' maximum is the answer, with a second read one element at a time otherwise: 0.059 ns per element where a compare and a select took 0.090 (Apple M1 Pro). A result longer than 65,536 counts and longer than the array is left to NumPy: NumPy takes it from `calloc`, whose large blocks are zeroed by the system only when touched, so `np.bincount(np.array([10**9]))` costs NumPy almost nothing and would have the kernel write 8 GB. NumPy 2.5.5 itself writes outside its result for an array whose largest element is the largest int64 with no negative one; the binding leaves that call to NumPy unchanged, and `delivery check` cannot run it.
- **What a caller must know.** The pair path uses 16.6 KB below the stack pointer and the second table 12.3 KB, whose first store can land up to 12 KB below the caller's frame (`bincount_u8` uses 8.2 KB). The pair path is slower than the C, 0.63x to 0.70x, when many groups of four hold an element outside 0..63, which the binding never sends. `n` of 2^32 or more with at most 64 counters takes the paths with 64-bit counters; it was never run (8 GB and 32 GB of input). No `pext`, `pdep`, gather or masked store; `popcnt` in the sample that chooses the second table.
- **From Python,** `uv run delivery time --only bincount` at a load average of about 1.0, on codes uniform in 0..63: `np.bincount(x)` on int16 is 3.9x faster at 16 elements (358 ns to 92 ns), 3.7x at 4,096 and 4.6x at 1,048,576 (835 µs to 184 µs); on int64 2.3x, 1.5x and 1.9x (659 µs to 347 µs); the recorded run on a quiet machine has 1.9x at 4,096. The int64 call reads 8 MB twice, once for the scan, and NumPy does not convert an int64 array first. `np.bincount(x)` on int32 passes through the hook within 0.4% to 2.7% of NumPy.
- **Technique (ARM64).** On the Apple M1 Pro the naive C, which clang leaves scalar, takes 0.41 ns per element on int16 and 0.35 on int64 uniform codes, and 1.35 and 0.93 ns on four distinct values: there an increment waits on the store of the previous increment of the same counter far longer than on x86-64. The kernels keep x86-64's table of pairs and add a family of tables, chosen once per call. With at most 64 counters, from 4,096 int16 or 8,192 int64 elements: the 64 by 64 table of 32-bit pairs on the stack, one `tst` of an 8-byte word of four int16 against `0xffc0` in every element (for int64, the four or'ed together and compared with 64), the pair indexes from one `orr` with a shift. From 4 to 1,024 counters and 8 elements per counter: four tables of `bins` 32-bit counters on the stack, element k of each four into table k, as `bincount_u8` does, summed over `counts` at the end. A sample of the first 64 int16 or 32 int64 elements, compared with the element four places on, says whether the data repeats; if it does, the four tables are taken from 2 elements per counter, eight tables replace four from 8,192 elements, and the pair path adds a second pair table for the second pair of each four from 16,384 int16 or 32,768 int64 elements. Everything else goes straight into `counts`. Sixteen int16 elements are tested against `bins` by one vector compare, and four int64 elements by one `cmp` and three `ccmp`; a group that fails is counted one element at a time with its own test. The counters on the stack are 32 bits, so the paths that use them need n below 2^32.
- **Where ARM64 and x86-64 differ.** Four tables for every call with 4 to 1,024 counters from 8 elements per counter, which x86-64 did not keep: its second table cost 5% to 10% on data that does not repeat, while on the M1 Pro four tables were 1.03x to 1.10x faster than one on uniform codes from 65,536 elements up, and 0.95x only at the smallest sizes they are taken at (attempt 3 of the int16 log, attempt 2 of the int64 log). A second pair table and eight tables, which x86-64 tried only outside the judge or not at all, are kept here behind the sample; on every call they cost uniform codes 3% to 13% (attempts 5 and 7 of [the int16 log](../kernels/bincount_i16/arm64/attempts.md)).
- **Figures (ARM64),** recorded with `uv run judge run --record` on the Apple M1 Pro on a quiet machine, at a load average of 1.4 to 1.6, at the top of the stack that added the ARM64 kernels. `bincount_i16`: 2.45x [2.45 – 2.45] over the naive C across the four tables and 8.36x over NumPy; uniform codes into 64 counters 2.94x at 1,048,576 elements (141.8 µs against 417.4, the pair table at 0.135 ns per element), into 1,000 counters 1.71x (0.23 ns per element, four tables); four values 7.13x and 4.84x (0.18 and 0.27 ns per element). `bincount_i64`: 1.60x [1.60 – 1.60] and 6.23x; at 1,048,576 elements 1.56x and 1.25x on uniform codes (0.21 and 0.27 ns per element), 3.52x and 3.05x on four values. The kernels' pull request recorded 2.48x and 8.49x, and 1.60x and 6.25x, at a load average of 8 to 10; no figure moved by more than a tenth. Neither kernel is slower with the output 16 bytes behind the input (1.00x over the table).
- **What limits it (ARM64).** One counter load, add and store per element on every path but the pairs', 0.8 to 0.9 cycles per element; the pair table halves them. On int64 the input is twice the loads and the test three `orr`, so the same pair table takes 0.23 ns per element against int16's 0.14.
- **Where it is not faster (ARM64).** `bincount_i64` with uniform codes, 64 counters and 16 elements: 0.88x (18.4 ns against 16.2 in the recorded run), the set-up of a call the C does not have. In the recorded run four values into 1,000 counters read 0.99x and 0.97x at 256 and 1,024 int64 elements, where both run the same one-table loop; in the kernels' pull request 0.98x and 0.97x, and 1.00x and 0.98x in the run before it.
- **What a caller must know (ARM64).** The stack. The pair path moves the stack pointer down 32,768 + 256 bytes, whether it uses one pair table or two; the eight tables take up to 32 KB and the four up to 16 KB (1,024 counters). The frame is taken in one step and cleared from its bottom up, so the first store lands up to 33,024 bytes below the caller's stack pointer and the pages in between are not touched first. On macOS a thread's stack is mapped in full with one 16 KB guard page below it, so this is safe while more than 33 KB of stack is left; a caller on a thread with less left could have the first store pass over the guard page into whatever memory lies below it, where the x86-64 kernels' pair table is cleared from the top down. `bincount_u8` on ARM64 takes 4 KB, the x86-64 kernels 16.6 KB and 12.3 KB. Elements outside `[0, bins)` are ignored on every path; data where most groups hold one was not measured on ARM64 (each such group is counted one element at a time, which on x86-64 made the pair path 0.63x to 0.70x of the C), and the binding never sends one. `n` of 2^32 or more takes the one-table path with 64-bit counters and was never run. Both timed "1,000 counters" tables hold codes below 64 only, so the 1,000-counter paths are timed on data that touches 64 of their counters; shrinking the tables to the largest element would win those tables without helping a caller, whose `bins` the binding already sets from the largest element, and was not tried.
- **From Python (ARM64),** `uv run delivery time --only bincount` on the Apple M1 Pro at a load average of 11.3, on codes uniform in 0..63: `np.bincount(x)` on int16 is 3.2x faster than NumPy at 16 elements (375 ns to 117 ns), 5.6x at 4,096 and 9.3x at 1,048,576 (1.63 ms to 175 µs); on int64 2.7x, 3.8x and 4.2x (1.45 ms to 350 µs). `np.bincount(x)` on int32 passes through the hook within 2.6% of NumPy. With the binding's NEON scans for the largest element (above), at a load average of 2.0, the same statements before and after: int16 3.2x to 4.5x at 16 elements (115 ns to 81 ns), 5.5x to 5.6x at 4,096 and 9.3x to 9.4x at 1,048,576; int64 2.7x at 16 elements, 3.7x to 4.0x at 4,096 (1.46 µs to 1.34 µs) and 4.1x to 4.3x at 1,048,576 (337 µs to 318 µs). Recorded at the top of the fmax stack with `uv run delivery time --record` (load average 2.2): int16 4.48x, 5.63x and 9.44x; int64 2.71x, 4.02x and 4.26x.

#### Distinct labels: `np.unique` on int64

Added for the applications study's [issue 189](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/189) and designed in [unique-design.md](unique-design.md), not from the second shortlist. scikit-learn calls `np.unique` on every array of labels it validates; NumPy puts every element into a hash set and sorts the few values it finds, 6.4 ms for a million labels of two values on the Apple M1 Pro. `unique_i64(x, out, n, lo, span)` writes the distinct values of `x` in `[lo, lo + span)` to `out` in ascending order and returns how many; `out` has `span` elements and is scratch, so the kernel may use it as its table.

- **Technique (ARM64).** For a range of at most 64 values, value `lo + d` is bit `d` of a 64-bit set: NEON makes `1 << d` with `ushl`, two elements per vector, and ors `d` itself into a second set of accumulators; over a chunk of 64 elements the or of the `d` is below 64 exactly when each is, and a chunk where it is not is read again one element at a time. When `0 <= lo` and `lo + span <= 64`, which covers labels from zero, the elements are their own bit numbers and the subtraction goes. Above 64 values the last `span` bytes of `out` are a byte table, marked with `strb` and read out eight bytes at a time; the values written to the front never reach a table byte still to be read (the proof is in the header of `kernel.s`). The log is `kernels/unique_i64/arm64/attempts.md`.
- **Figures (ARM64),** `uv run judge run unique_i64 --unlocked --record` at a load average of 22, so rough: 1.44x [1.44 – 1.45] over the naive C and 41.6x over NumPy across the four timed cases. At 1,048,576 elements with ten values of range, 1.57x over the C and 39.5x to 40.0x over NumPy (160 µs); with 256 values of range 0.98x to 1.00x over the C and 25.4x over NumPy, both one store per element.
- **Where it is not faster (ARM64).** Ten values at 16 elements, 0.86x of the C (16.8 ns against 14.4), and a range of 256 from 262,144 elements, 0.97x to 1.00x.
- **Technique (x86-64).** The same two paths, built differently. AVX2's `vpsllvq` gives zero for a count of 64 or more, where `ushl` reads only the low byte of the count, so the set needs no test of a chunk and no second reading: four elements per vector are a subtraction, a shift of 1 and an or. When `0 <= lo` and `lo + span <= 64` the shift takes its counts straight from memory, two instructions per four elements, 0.21 cycles per element in the first-level cache on the Intel Core i5-14600KF. The loads are brought to a multiple of 32 bytes, which a set allows because reading an element twice changes nothing, and from 64 KB of input the loop asks for the lines 2,048 bytes ahead. Above 64 values a store per element is what the byte table costs, 0.77 cycles and more, so the table is kept as the way out: a **window** of 64 values, a set like the one above, takes the elements first, sixteen per chunk, with the or of the chunk's sixteen offsets tested for a bit from 64 up. A chunk that fails is marked in the table one element at a time, and the window moves to that chunk's values if they are less than 64 apart. While no chunk fails there is no table at all. A first chunk spread wider than a window, wide chunks close together, or a window that did not last sixteen chunks switch the window off for a stretch that grows. The log is `kernels/unique_i64/x86_64/attempts.md`.
- **Figures (x86-64),** `uv run judge run unique_i64 --record`, locked, on the Intel Core i5-14600KF at a load average of 2.6: 3.00x [2.97 – 3.04] over the naive C and 148x over NumPy across the four timed cases. With ten values of range, 3.3x to 5.6x over the C from 256 to 65,536 elements (0.041 ns per element at 4,096) and 1.5x to 1.9x at 1,048,576, inside wide intervals, where both read memory at its speed; with 256 values of range, 12x and 19x at 16 elements (3.4 and 5.2 ns against the 62 to 65 the C takes to clear and read its 2 KB table), 2.1x to 4.1x from 1,024 to 262,144 and 1.3x to 1.4x at 1,048,576. Against NumPy at 1,048,576 elements, 70x to 83x. The naive C is one 8-byte store per element behind a compare and a branch, 0.15 to 0.16 ns, and was already about 55x ahead of NumPy's hash set. With the output 16 bytes behind the input the kernel is 1.01x of the placement apart.
- **Where it is not faster (x86-64).** No timed size is slower than the C. What is not timed is labels spread over 65 or more values, which is what a range of 256 means to a caller more often than ten neighbouring values in it. There the window fails and every element is a store. In a benchmark outside the judge, on labels uniform over all 256 values, the kernel takes 91 ns at 256 elements against 121 for the C, 704 ns against 814 at 4,096 and 235 µs against 305 at 1,048,576: 1.2x to 1.3x. Two shapes are slower than the C at 256 elements, by a failed chunk or two before the window is given up: 100 evenly spread values, 72 ns against 67, and sorted labels with one element per value, 134 ns against 108. Sorted labels over all 256 values are 1.2x over the C at 4,096 elements and 1.7x at 65,536. The log has the table. See [What the judge does not see](#what-the-judge-does-not-see).
- **From Python (x86-64),** `uv run delivery time --only unique` at a load average of 2.4 to 2.6, with the assembly and then with the C it replaced: on two values 835 ns against 1.49 µs at 4,096 elements, 279 µs against 374 µs at 1,048,576, 93 ns against 98 at 16; against NumPy that is 12.5x faster at 16 elements, 49x at 4,096 and 39x at 1,048,576 (10.8 ms), and on ten values 12.5x, 50x and 39x. A wide range that goes to NumPy after the read: +3.0%, +0.6% and -2.9% at the three sizes, within the noise of that run. At a million elements the binding's read of the array for its range is now the larger half of the call. Not recorded under `delivery/results/`, whose x86-64 file predates this kernel.
- **The binding.** A function hook on `np.unique`. The call with the three `return_*` flags absent or `False`, `axis` None, `equal_nan` a bool and `sorted` absent or `True`, on an int64 `ndarray` with at least one element; one that is not C-contiguous is copied in C order first, as NumPy's `flatten` does. The array is read for its smallest and largest elements, and the kernel is called when the range is narrower than 256 values or than the array; anything wider is NumPy's. The result is a new array of exactly the count.
- **From Python (ARM64),** `uv run delivery time --only unique` at a load average of 4.3: on two values 13.2x faster than NumPy at 16 elements (1.28 µs to 96.5 ns), 19.6x at 4,096 and 17.7x at 1,048,576 (6.55 ms to 371 µs); on ten values 15.0x, 20.6x and 18.2x. A wide range that goes to NumPy after the read costs within the noise: +0.4% at 16 elements, -0.3% at a million. `sklearn_metrics` from the applications study, at a load average of 20: 1.33x faster with `install()` against 1.02x with the `np.unique` hook removed; all 67 of its `np.unique` calls are taken.

### Coverage gaps

Kernels for calls a production pipeline made that reached a hook and went to NumPy, because their element type, axis or dimension had no kernel ([issue 160](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/160)). Which calls get one is decided in [coverage-gaps-design.md](coverage-gaps-design.md). Each is written for x86-64 first and ships as its naive C on ARM64 until its ARM64 assembly exists. Every run is `--unlocked`, because each kernel's spec and reference arrived in the same pull request as its assembly, except `sum_f64`'s, whose spec and reference were on `main` first. `any_cols_bool`'s ARM64 assembly came after its x86-64 kernel, written against the same spec.

#### `np.searchsorted` on int64

The pipeline made 717,457 calls of `np.searchsorted(frames, f)` on a sorted int64 array with one `np.int64` needle, on either side, and the float64 kernels took none. `searchsorted_i64(a, v, out, n, needles)` and `searchsorted_right_i64` are the float64 kernels' specs with int64: `n` sorted elements, any number of needles, drawn from `a` as well as random. There is no NaN to order. The logs are `kernels/searchsorted_i64/x86_64/attempts.md`, which has the measurements, and `kernels/searchsorted_right_i64/x86_64/attempts.md`.

- **Technique (x86-64).** The float64 kernels' search with `vpcmpgtq` for the compare and `vpgatherqq` for the probes, and then three changes that do not depend on the element type. A pass over a chunk takes **two steps** of each of its searches between loading the positions and storing them, where the float64 kernels take one: 1.09x. The **first pass needs no gather**: every search probes the same element first, then one of two, then one of four, and the probe is chosen with the masks of the compares before it, an AND and an XOR against the difference of the candidates. It takes two steps or three, whichever leaves an even number: 1.06x, and 1.22x at 16 elements. And a chunk is **256 needles** while the array fits the second-level cache: 1.01x.
- **What limits it.** Not a dependency: the number of instructions. A step of four searches is about eighteen instructions' worth of the front end, thirteen of them the gather, and a loop of gathers alone runs at one per 2.2 cycles. Three or four steps in a pass were slower than two, because each gather of a pass waits for the one before and the scheduler fills. Searching one needle at a time in general registers, with the positions in the output between passes, was slower than the gathers for the same reason. From 524,288 elements, where the probes miss the second-level cache, a pass takes one step: two were 5% to 12% slower there with 1,000 needles, also for the early steps alone.
- **Figures (x86-64),** `uv run judge run --record --unlocked` on the Intel Core i5-14600KF at a load average of 0.7 to 1.0. `searchsorted_i64`: 5.63x [5.62 – 5.64] over the naive C and 3.68x over NumPy across both needle counts; `searchsorted_right_i64` 5.00x [4.99 – 5.00] and 3.71x. With 1,000 needles the left side is 11.1x over the C, and over NumPy 5.1x at 16 elements, 4.0x at 4,096, 2.0x at 262,144, 1.45x at 1,048,576 and 1.2x at 16,777,216. With 64 needles 2.86x over the C and 5.11x over NumPy, where NumPy's figure is mostly its call.
- **NumPy is closer here than on float64.** Its int64 search takes 6.5 ns per needle at 1,024 elements and 19 ns at 1,048,576 with 1,000 needles, about a third of what its float64 search takes. The kernel takes 1.6 and 13 ns. From a million elements both wait for memory.
- **Where it is not faster.** No timed size is slower than the C. With 64 needles at 1,048,576 elements it is level with it (1.01x and 1.03x): the judge repeats the same 64 needles, so the C's branches are learnt. Two sizes move from run to run with nothing changed, 4,096 elements by 14% and 262,144 by 25%, where the array is two thirds of the first-level cache and all of the second; the logs have the figures.
- **The binding.** `searchsorted`'s method hook, which serves float64. It now takes a one-dimensional contiguous int64 array with an int64 array of needles of any shape, an `np.int64`, or a Python int that fits int64; `side` either way; no `sorter`. The result is an `intp` array of the needles' shape, or an `np.intp` for one needle, as NumPy's is. Needles of any other type are NumPy's: an `np.int32`, an `np.uint64`, a float, a bool, a list, a zero-dimensional array, a Python int outside int64. NumPy converts those and the array to a common type by its own rules, which for a float or a `uint64` needle is float64. `np.digitize` on int64 bins and values is served through the same hook.
- **From Python (x86-64),** `uv run delivery time --only searchsorted` at a load average of 1.4. The pipeline's call, `np.searchsorted(a, f)` with one `np.int64`: 559 ns on NumPy and 264 ns with the hook at 16 elements, 561 and 269 at 4,096, 599 and 293 at 1,048,576, so 2.1x at every length; `side='right'` 1.9x. Nearly all of what is left is NumPy's Python function and the call to the method. With as many needles as elements 1.5x at 16, 3.3x at 4,096 and 1.2x at 1,048,576 (31.1 ms to 26.1 ms); with a million needles against 1,000 elements 1.9x, and 4,096 needles 3.9x. Needles of int32, which stay with NumPy, cost +2.6%, 0.0% and -0.7% at the three sizes. Not recorded under `delivery/results/`, whose x86-64 file predates these kernels.
- **No call is left to NumPy for its size.** By a script outside the repository, on lengths from 16 to 16,777,216 and from 1 to 100,000 needles, random and sorted, the hook was never slower than NumPy. Its smallest gain is four needles against 65,536 elements or more, 1.00x to 1.14x where one needle is 1.4x: four needles are one vector of searches, a chain of gathers, and the judge times no call with fewer than 64.
- **ARM64.** The kernels ship as their C there. Nothing was measured.
#### Any by columns: `m.any(axis=0)`

The pipeline made 11,779 calls of `valued[rows].any(axis=0)` on boolean masks of 30 rows by 31 columns, 30 by 30 and 1 by 31. NumPy takes 840 ns for the first of those on the Intel Core i5-14600KF and 0.33 ns per element at 1,048,576 elements in rows of 31; a C loop that or's each row into the results in memory takes 0.08 ns per element. `any_cols_bool(m, out, n, cols)` mirrors `any_rows_bool`: `n` elements in C order, `n // cols` whole rows, a partial last row ignored. It writes `cols` results, all False when there is no whole row, as NumPy answers for a matrix of no rows. The spec checks 30 widths from 1 to 1,000 and times four of them: 31, 8, 64 and 256. The log is `kernels/any_cols_bool/x86_64/attempts.md`.

- **Technique (x86-64).** The results stay in `ymm` registers for the whole call, or'ed with the rows by `vpor` with a memory operand, and are made 0 or 1 once, by `vpminub` with 1, when they are stored. **Rows of 32 and more** are taken in strips of 32 columns, each with a register of its own for a whole pass over the rows: eight strips in a pass while 256 columns are left, then four, two and one, and the columns left under 32 by one more strip that ends at the row's end and overlaps the one before. The rows are counted by address, so this path has no division. **Rows under 32** have one register for the whole call. A 32-byte load at a row's start holds as many whole rows as fit, and the loads are that many bytes apart, four to a pass of the loop; what a load holds beyond its whole rows belongs to rows with a load of their own and is never looked at. The rows that start in the last 31 bytes of the matrix cannot be loaded that way: the last 32 bytes are loaded once and moved down to where a load at the first of those rows would have put them, with zeros above. Then the rows inside the register are folded onto the first. Moving a register's bytes down by a count is two `vpshufb` with index vectors read from a table at the count. The results are stored as their first and last 16, 8 or 4 bytes, which overlap, and a matrix under 32 bytes is put in the register the same way, from two loads.
- **What limits it.** The loads. In the first-level cache a row of 31 takes 0.19 ns, one cycle, where two loads per cycle would allow half that: a 32-byte load every 31 bytes straddles two cache lines 31 times in 64, and such a load costs a cycle more. A row of 31 can be covered by a 32-byte load from two places one byte apart, so there is no choosing one that does not straddle. With the matrix on a 64-byte boundary rows of 8 take 16.5 ns per 4,096 elements, three bytes off it 26.9 ns. Beyond the first-level cache the kernel takes 0.009 ns per element in rows of 31 up to 1,048,576 elements and 0.013 at 4,194,304, which is what the caches deliver. A call of two rows of 31 takes 2.3 ns and the pipeline's 30 by 31 about 6 ns, of which the one division of the narrow path is 0.2 to 0.6 ns for one to eight rows and nothing from 30.
- **Figures (x86-64),** `uv run judge run any_cols_bool --record --unlocked` on the Intel Core i5-14600KF at a load average of 1.2 to 1.3: 5.72x [5.69 – 5.74] over the naive C across the eight tables and 71.2x over NumPy. Rows of 31: 8.55x and 8.48x in the two distributions, 13.7x at 4,096 elements (25.0 ns against 341 ns) and 8.9x at 1,048,576, and over NumPy 86x at 4,096 and 37x at 1,048,576. Rows of 8: 14.2x, 44x at 16,384. Rows of 64: 4.81x and 4.75x. Rows of 256: 1.71x and 2.00x, and 1.66x at 1,048,576, where the compiled loop's one or per 32 bytes in memory is already close to what the second-level cache delivers.
- **Where it is not faster.** Rows of 256 at 16 and 64 elements, where there is no row and the kernel stores 256 zeros: 4.5 ns against 4.2 to 4.6 for the reference, 0.94x to 1.01x. That figure moves between 3.2 and 4.7 ns with where the code lies. The binding never makes that call: a matrix of no rows is NumPy's.
- **Bytes other than 0 and 1.** A result is whether the column holds a byte that is not zero, on every path and in the C, and each result is exactly 0 or 1. That is what NumPy computes along this axis: 12,960 calls on 24 shapes, at three alignments, with non-zero bytes of every value and of 2, 16, 128, 254 and 255 alone, all agreed with `(bytes != 0).any(axis=0)` on NumPy 2.5.5. The judge's boolean data holds only 0 and 1, so the judge does not check it. `uv run delivery check` does, on ten shapes with every True stored as 2, as 255 and as bytes of every value, and CI runs those cases against the C on ARM64.
- **The binding.** The method and function hooks of `any`, which serve `axis=1` with `any_rows_bool`. They now take `axis` as the Python int 0 or -2 as well, with no other argument, on a C-ordered two-dimensional boolean array with at least one row and one column. The result is a new one-dimensional boolean array of one element per column. The matrix is not read for other bytes first, unlike for `axis=1`, so it is read once and the cost [issue 167](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/167) describes does not arise. The probe of `install()` stores one True as the byte 0x80.
- **From Python (x86-64),** at a load average of 0.6 to 0.7. By a script outside the repository, `m.any(axis=0)` on a mask of 30 by 31: 840 ns on NumPy and 95 ns with the hook, 8.9x; `np.any(m, axis=0)` 1,239 ns and 108 ns, 11.5x. 30 by 30: 8.5x and 11.5x. 1 by 31: 503 ns and 96 ns, 5.3x, and 8.6x for the function. 2,978 by 22: 29.6 µs and 782 ns, 38x. 4,096 by 256: 45.4 µs and 8.2 µs, 5.6x. By `uv run delivery time --only any` on rows of 31 with 5% of the elements True: 5.5x at 16 elements, which is one row, 17.9x at 4,096 (1.9 µs to 106 ns) and 37x at 1,048,576 (348 µs to 9.4 µs); the function form 8.6x, 19.6x and 36x. Nearly all of a small call is the call itself: the kernel's share of the 95 ns is 6. Not recorded under `delivery/results/`, whose x86-64 file predates this kernel.
- **Technique (ARM64).** The results stay in NEON registers across all rows and are stored once, each through `umin` with 1. Where x86-64 keeps one register for rows under 32 and strips of 32 columns above, the ARM64 kernel has a path per range of widths, each reading a row in as few 16-byte loads as cover it. Rows of 1 to 16: the whole rows as one stream of 16-byte loads that step by the largest multiple of the width under 16, so lane `i` of every load is column `i mod width`; eight accumulators; the last 16 bytes moved to the same lanes by `tbl`; at the end the lanes of a column folded onto the first `width` by `tbl` with shifts of width, 2, 4 and 8 width, where x86-64 folds the rows inside its register. Rows of 17 to 32: a row's first 16 bytes and its last 16, which overlap, four rows a step. 33 to 48: first 32 and last 16; 49 to 64: first 32 and last 32; 65 to 112: first 64 and last 48; 113 to 128: first 64 and last 64; 129 to 256: first 128 and last 128, sixteen accumulators. Wider rows: tiles of 16 rows and blocks of 128 columns, loaded from `out`, or'ed and stored back, the first tile from zero and the last through `umin`. One whole row wider than 16 is a copy through `umin`. From 8 MB the loops whose loads take an immediate offset use `ldnp`, and wide tiles are 8 rows. Unaligned loads cost nothing extra on the M1, so no load is placed to avoid a cache-line boundary, as x86-64's must. The log is `kernels/any_cols_bool/arm64/attempts.md`: attempts 1 to 23 were judged against an earlier spec of the ARM64 branch's own (bytes of every value fed by the judge, other timed widths), 24 to 31 against this one.
- **What limits it (ARM64).** In the first-level cache, the loads: three 16-byte loads a cycle on the Apple M1 Pro. Rows of 31 take two a row, about 0.7 cycles; rows of 8 run at about 43 bytes a cycle; rows of 64 at 1.37 cycles a row against 1.33 for four loads; rows of 256 sixteen loads a row. Beyond it, what the second-level cache delivers, about 80 GB/s, whatever the width. A prefetch took load slots and lost.
- **Figures (ARM64),** `uv run judge run any_cols_bool --unlocked --record` on the Apple M1 Pro at a load average of 2.1 to 2.3: 8.77x [8.76 – 8.77] over the naive C across the eight tables and 73.6x over NumPy. Rows of 31: 16.9x and 16.8x in the two distributions, 27.6x at 4,096 elements and 18.0x at 1,048,576 (221 µs to 12.3 µs), and over NumPy 40.5x at 1,048,576. Rows of 8: 26.8x, 37.3x at 1,048,576. Rows of 64: 4.81x. Rows of 256: 2.71x and 2.72x, 2.2x at 1,048,576. No size of any table is slower than the C or than NumPy; the smallest gain is rows of 64 with no row (16 elements), 2.17x. With the output 16 bytes behind the input: 1.00x, not significant.
- **Widths the judge does not time (ARM64),** against the naive C built with the judge's flags, by a timer outside the repository (best of 15 batches, 3% True, load average 2.2), at 930 / 4,096 / 1,048,576 elements: width 1 128x / 302x / 185x, 3 111x / 175x / 111x, 15 27.8x / 53.0x / 34.7x, 17 18.8x / 23.2x / 16.8x, 33 16.0x / 20.2x / 14.2x, 48 18.3x / 24.3x / 15.4x, 65 4.45x / 4.78x / 3.28x, 100 7.76x / 9.64x / 6.27x, 129 2.72x / 3.01x / 2.15x, 200 2.86x / 3.67x / 2.42x, 1,000 2.43x / 2.29x / 1.34x. None is slower than the C.
- **Bytes other than 0 and 1 (ARM64).** Every path or's raw bytes and stores the or through `umin` with 1. Outside the judge, which feeds 0 and 1, the kernel was checked after every attempt against a plain loop on arbitrary bytes (any value, rare 0x80, rare 2 to 255, all zero; 33 widths from 1 to 4,097, 14 sizes from 0 to 300,000, a guard byte after `out`): 1,848 cases pass. `delivery check` passes with the assembly behind the binding.
- **From Python (ARM64),** `uv run delivery time --only any` on the Apple M1 Pro on 20 November 2026, the assembly and then the C behind the same binding, at load averages of 1.9 and 1.9. `m.any(axis=0)` on rows of 31 with 5% True: 149 ns with the assembly and 168 ns with the C at 16 elements (one row; NumPy 663 ns), 173 ns and 907 ns at 4,096 (NumPy 2.64 µs), 12.5 µs and 218 µs at 1,048,576 (NumPy 497 to 499 µs). #### `np.sum` on float32

The pipeline sums float32 slices of 16 to 65,536 elements in a Python loop. NumPy does not add from left to right: `pairwise_sum` adds blocks of up to 128 elements, each with eight running sums combined in pairs at its end, and combines the blocks as a tree split at half the length rounded down to a multiple of 8. Its loop waits on each addition, four cycles per eight elements, and the naive C written in that order does the same. `sum_f32(x, n)` must return NumPy's bits, so it keeps the order of the additions and changes only when they are made. The log is `kernels/sum_f32/x86_64/attempts.md`; the spec and reference are the ARM64 session's, verbatim.

- **Technique (x86-64).** Under 8 elements one after another from `+0.0`; 8 to 128 one leaf, its eight running sums one `ymm`. Above that the tree is walked with an explicit stack of 16-byte frames below `rsp`, one per halving, at most 64 (1 KB) for any length that fits in memory; the kernel saves the six callee-saved registers and restores `rsp` before it returns. A node of 68 to 128 vectors away from the right edge has its eight leaves three levels down, of `q` or `q + 1` vectors: their starts are worked out as NumPy's halvings make them and the eight are summed in one loop, each in its own `ymm`, so the loop has eight independent chains and is held by loads, not by the latency of one chain. A leaf of `q` vectors adds a vector of `+0.0` where its neighbours add their last one. Nodes of 17 to 32 and 34 to 64 vectors are groups of two and four leaves in the same way. A leaf's eight sums are combined with `vmovshdup` and `vpermilps` in NumPy's order, which was 1.03x over two `vhaddps`, and the groups of two and four 1.04x over walking those nodes leaf by leaf. Only the last leaf of the right edge carries the 1 to 7 elements left over. A zero operand changes no sum that is not zero, so the `+0.0` added where NumPy adds nothing can only matter to a zero result, and the kernel ends with `+ 0.0` as NumPy does: it never returns `-0.0`. No `pext`, `pdep`, gather or masked store is used.
- **What limits it.** The loads. In the first-level cache the group's loop takes 0.67 to 0.79 cycles per vector (65 to 76.5 ns at 4,096 elements in the judge's runs) with the array 16 bytes past a 32-byte boundary, where every second load straddles two cache lines; the core allows 0.5, one load and one add per vector on two ports each. With the array on a 64-byte boundary 262,144 elements take about 6.6 us, against 8.4 to 8.7 us 16 bytes past one, which is where the judge and NumPy put a large array. Beyond the first-level cache it is within a tenth of a plain contiguous sum of the same bytes with eight accumulators, and from 1,048,576 elements it waits for memory. Two steps per trip in the group of eight, and 16-byte half loads to avoid the straddles, made no difference the judge could find.
- **Figures (x86-64),** `uv run judge run sum_f32 --record --unlocked` on the Intel Core i5-14600KF at a load average of 1.85 to 2.0, with two other agents running: 2.77x [2.76 – 2.78] over the naive C across its sizes and 14.4x over NumPy. 1.64x at 16 elements (1.3 ns against 2.2), 3.0x at 256, 6.4x at 1,024, 5.9x at 4,096 (76.5 ns against 450), 3.4x at 16,384, 4.5x at 65,536, 3.3x at 262,144, 1.6x to 1.7x at 1,048,576 and 4,194,304 and 1.26x at 16,777,216. Over NumPy 556x at 16 elements, which is NumPy's call, 17x at 4,096 and 1.5x at 16,777,216. The figures at 1,048,576 and 4,194,304 look depressed by the shared last-level cache: earlier runs that day, with fewer agents running, took 53 to 54 us at 1,048,576 (2.09x) where the recorded run took 70.6 us.
- **Where it is not faster.** No timed size is slower than the C. The gain is smallest at 16 elements, where the call is most of the time, and from 1,048,576, where both wait for memory. The judge times only 16, 64, 256 and powers of two from 1,024, which from 1,024 up are all groups of eight; the groups of two and four, single leaves of the walk, leaves of unequal length and the right edge's 1 to 7 elements are not timed. A differential test outside the repository checks them for bits: every length from 0 to 5,000 at every 4-byte start within 32 bytes and ending at a guard page, and 400 random lengths up to 8,388,608, on seven value distributions, 20,404 inputs with no failure; a copy split at the wrong place failed 9,411 of 20,024.
- **The binding.** The function and method hooks of `sum`, which serve uint8 and booleans, also take a contiguous, aligned, one-dimensional float32 array with no other argument, and return an `np.float32`. An array whose elements are not aligned to 4 bytes is NumPy's: NumPy copies it in blocks of 8,192 elements and adds the blocks' sums one after another, another order. A result that is not finite is thrown away and the call passed to NumPy, for its NaN's bits and its warning. After the kernel MXCSR's invalid, divide by zero, overflow and underflow flags are cleared, and the precision flag is left as the additions left it, which is what NumPy leaves; the kernel does not touch MXCSR's controls. The probe of `install()` sums 1,003 values whose float32 sum in NumPy's order differs from the sum one after another, from a tree split at half not rounded to a multiple of 8, from leaves of 64 or 256 elements and from leaves whose running sums are added in turn. The binding is the ARM64 session's, made to serve both architectures.

#### `np.sum` on float64

Not a call of the pipeline: the sibling the design added, since `np.sum` on float64 has NumPy's order of float32 and the same room, and it is the commonest reduction in NumPy. `sum_f64(x, n)` returns NumPy's bits; a leaf's eight running sums are two `ymm` of four. The log is `kernels/sum_f64/x86_64/attempts.md`; the spec and reference are the ARM64 session's, as on `main`.

- **Technique (x86-64).** Under 8 elements one after another from `+0.0`; up to 128 one leaf in two `ymm`; up to 247, and exactly 248 or 256, one pair of leaves straight from the entry. Longer arrays walk the tree with an explicit stack in a 1 KB frame (`sub rsp, 1024`, at most 55 of its 64 entries used for any length that fits in memory) and sum pairs and quads (four leaves, eight chains) side by side. Two of a quad's eight chains add with `vfmadd231pd s, one, [x]`, that is `x * 1.0 + s`, on the port the adds leave idle; `x * 1.0` is exact, so the result is the addition `s + x` bit for bit, signed zeros and denormals included (attempt 6, 1.011x; about 5% at 1,024 to 4,096 elements). A shorter leaf adds a block of `+0.0` from `.rodata` where its neighbours add their last vector. When `x` is 8, 16 or 24 bytes past a 32-byte boundary a quad loads aligned chunks and turns its leaves' lanes back afterwards; the first and last chunk of the array are read with the masked load `vmaskmovpd`, so nothing outside the buffer is read. No `pext`, `pdep`, gather or masked store. From 1,048,576 elements (8 MB) a quad's leaves are summed one after another as one stream, with `prefetcht0` 2 KB ahead.
- **What limits it (Intel Core i5-14600KF).** In the first-level cache the loads: a quad of 512 elements takes 12.9 ns warm against 11.3 ns for the same loop with plain loads. In the second-level cache about 32 bytes a cycle. Beyond it the third-level cache and memory, at the rate the naive C also gets. It gains less than `sum_f32` (2.77x): a float64 leaf is twice the loads.
- **Figures (x86-64),** `uv run judge run sum_f64 --record`, locked against the spec and reference on `main`, on the Intel Core i5-14600KF at a load average of 0.9 to 1.0 with no other judge running: 1.88x [1.88 – 1.89] over the naive C across its sizes and 10.8x over NumPy. 1.87x at 16 elements (1.3 ns against 2.5), 1.56x at 64, 3.0x at 256, 3.76x at 1,024, 3.81x at 4,096 (105.2 ns against 401.1), 2.0x at 16,384 and 65,536, 1.53x at 262,144, 1.20x at 1,048,576 (85.3 us against 102.0), 1.09x at 4,194,304 and 1.06x at 16,777,216.
- **Where it is not faster.** No timed size is slower than the C on a quiet machine, but at 1,048,576 elements, in the third-level cache, the gain is the machine's: the runs of the kernel's own building, at a load average of 1.5 to 1.9 with other judges sharing that cache, had 0.98x to 1.00x there. The judge places buffers 16 bytes past a 32-byte boundary or on one, so the 8- and 24-byte offsets (an odd-element slice such as `x[1:]`) are not timed; a micro-benchmark had them 3% slower than unaligned loads in the first-level cache and 24% faster at 16,384 elements. A differential test outside the repository compared bits with `np.sum` on every length from 0 to 5,000 at every 8-byte offset within 32 bytes, with guard pages on both sides, and 300 random lengths up to 8,388,608, on seven value distributions: 33,595 inputs, no failure. Copies with two of a quad's combining additions swapped, or one `vpermilpd` removed, failed 2,884 and 546 inputs.
- **The binding.** The `float32` binding's, for `float64`, as on ARM64: the function and method hooks of `sum` take a contiguous, aligned, one-dimensional float64 array with no other argument and return an `np.float64`; an array not aligned to 8 bytes, a matrix, a strided array and any argument go to NumPy; a result that is not finite is thrown away and the call passed to NumPy; after the kernel MXCSR's invalid, divide by zero, overflow and underflow flags are cleared. The kernel's `+0.0` blocks, masked lanes and multiplications by 1.0 raise no flag the additions would not. The probe of `install()` sums the same 1,003 values as for `float32`, whose float64 sum in NumPy's order also differs in each of the four wrong orders.
- **From Python (x86-64),** `uv run delivery time --only sum` at a load average of 0.9. `x.sum()`: 297 ns on NumPy and 38 ns with the hook at 16 elements (7.7x), 818 and 144 ns at 4,096 (5.7x), 136 and 85.3 us at 1,048,576 (1.6x). `np.sum(x)`: 675 and 29.5 ns (23x), 1.25 us and 133 ns (9.4x), 136 and 85.4 us (1.6x). `np.sum` on int64, which stays with NumPy, costs +1.4% or less. Not recorded under `delivery/results/`, whose x86-64 file predates this kernel.
- **ARM64.** A NEON kernel of its own, written separately: [`sum_f64` on ARM64](#sum_f64-on-arm64).

#### `np.nonzero` on a boolean matrix

The pipeline takes `np.nonzero(m)` of boolean masks of 30 to 50 rows by 22 columns. NumPy's result for a matrix is one (count, 2) int64 array of (row, column) pairs with two views of its columns, so `nonzero_2d_bool(m, out, n, width)` writes the pairs: two int64 per True element, row first, in C order, into a scratch output of 2n, and returns how many int64 it wrote. Element i is at row i / width and column i % width, so a partial last row is a row like the others. The naive C stores a pair for every element and advances only on a True. The spec and reference are the ARM64 session's; the log is `kernels/nonzero_2d_bool/x86_64/attempts.md`.

- **Technique (x86-64).** Blocks of 32 mask bytes, made a 32-bit word by `vpcmpeqb` and `vpmovmskb`. The row and column of each block's first element are carried from block to block (two `div` on entry, then a compare, `cmov` and `sbb` per block), so no position is divided by the width; that alone was 1.14x over a `mulx` per position. **Sparse:** a block with few True elements takes a fixed number of positions out of the word with `tzcnt` and `blsr`, and after a sparse block a window of two blocks, a 64-bit word, is taken at once when it holds at most six. **Dense:** eight elements at a time, the group's byte indexing a table of its positions, four registers of two pairs stored and the write position advanced by the count. **Rows of up to 64** have a 256-byte table of the row and column of each offset in the block, a byte each: a sparse position widens its two bytes with `vpmovzxbq` and stores its pair in one 16-byte store, and a dense group picks its pairs' bytes out of a 16-byte window of that table with one `vpshufb`. **Wider rows** add (row, column of the group) to (0, position) pairs, and a group that runs past a row's end adds (1, -width) to the positions past it. The last 1 to 31 elements, and matrices under 32 elements, go one element at a time, as the reference does. Read-only tables: 22 KB, of which a call uses one 256-byte table for its width, the 2 KB position table and the 4 KB index table. No `pext`, `pdep`, gather or masked store.
- **What limits it.** Dense: about 20 instructions per group of eight, about five cycles; taking out the stores, the shuffles or the chain of the write position each gained 15% at most. Sparse: five instructions per position and about 14 per window of 64 elements. Building the dense blocks' pairs through a compacted list in the red zone, to make fewer 32-byte stores, was 0.93x: the loads from the list span two of its stores and cannot be forwarded.
- **Figures (x86-64),** `uv run judge run nonzero_2d_bool --record --unlocked` on the Intel Core i5-14600KF at a load average of 0.8 to 1.2: 3.95x [3.94 – 3.95] over the naive C across the eight tables and 35.2x over NumPy. With 5% True, 7.4x to 8.5x over the C at the four widths (3, 22, 32 and 1,000), 11x to 13.5x at 1,024 elements, 9.7x to 12.2x at 1,048,576 and about 6x at 16,777,216; over NumPy 49x to 51x. With half True 1.95x to 2.06x, 3.4x to 4.0x at 1,024, 1.7x to 1.8x at 1,048,576 and 1.13x to 1.16x at 16,777,216; over NumPy 23x to 25x.
- **Where it is not faster.** Under 32 elements the kernel is the one-element loop: 1.00x to 1.10x at 16 elements, and level (not significant) at width 3 with 5% True. No timed width is slower than the C. The last 1 to 31 elements of a matrix that is not a whole number of blocks go one at a time, about 0.45 ns each: the pipeline's 40 by 22 ends in 16 of them, about 7 ns of a call of about 110 ns; the judge times no size where it shows. With the output 16 bytes behind the mask the kernel is 0.91x and 0.89x of itself at 4,096 and 65,536 elements and 1.00x overall. The binding's output is a buffer of its own, so it lands there only by chance, when its address falls 16 bytes behind the mask's within a 4 KB page.
- **The binding.** The method hook of `nonzero` and the function hook of `np.where(mask)`, which serve one dimension, also take a C-contiguous two-dimensional boolean array of any shape. The mask is read first for bytes other than 0 and 1, as for one dimension; one that holds any is NumPy's. The kernel writes into a buffer of the binding's own, on the stack when small, and the pairs are copied into a new (count, 2) array, of which the result is two views, as NumPy builds it, with the same strides, flags and base. `np.argwhere` and `np.transpose(np.nonzero(m))` reach the method through `np.nonzero`. Ported from the ARM64 session, which measured the copy at 0.13 ms of a 0.44 ms call at a million elements, half True, on the Apple M1 Pro, and found shrinking the buffer with `realloc` slower there.
- **From Python (x86-64),** `uv run delivery time --only nonzero` at a load average of 0.9. `np.nonzero(m)` on 40 rows of 22: 1.75 µs on NumPy and 355 ns with the hook with 5% True (4.9x), 2.29 µs and 588 ns with half (3.9x). In rows of 22 at 4,096 elements 11.4x and 7.3x, at 1,048,576 23.8x and 10.4x; `m.nonzero()` and `np.where(m)` the same at a million and up to 2.3x at 16 elements, `np.argwhere(m)` 17x. The naive C in the binding, by a script outside the repository with the package built from `naive.c` at a load average of 0.8: 711 ns against the kernel's 373 at 880 elements with 5% True and 762 against 511 with half, 2.26 µs against 601 ns and 2.74 against 1.72 µs at 4,092, and 503 µs against 78 and 782 against 538 at 1,048,564.

#### Masked assignment on float32, int32 and int64

The issue counted 36,856 calls of `x[mask] = v` and 30,604 of `x[mask]` left to NumPy without saying on which type. NumPy over a naive C loop on the Intel Core i5-14600KF (load average 0.15): `x[mask] = 0` on float32 or int32 with half selected 86x at 16 elements, 16x at 4,096 and 36x at 1,048,576; on int64 103x, 7.8x and 18x; `x[mask]` on int32 32x, 6.2x and 17x with half selected and 16x, 1.8x and 4.3x with 5%. The kernels move bits without computing on them, so int64 assignment goes to `maskfill_f64` and int32 selection to `compress_f32`, and float32 and int32 assignment needed `maskfill_f32(x, mask, n, value)`, the 32-bit sibling of `maskfill_f64`.

- **Technique (x86-64).** Eight elements per step: `vpmovzxbd` widens eight mask bytes, `vpcmpgtd` against zero makes a lane all ones where the byte is not zero, and `vmaskmovps` stores the value to those lanes only. No element of x is loaded and no unselected element is written. One masked store does the first eight elements, and x moves on to a 32-byte boundary, so no store of the loop straddles two cache lines; the last eight elements are one masked store that overlaps the step before it. Below 8 elements, a loop with a branch.
- **The compiler makes the same loop.** `clang -O3 -march=native` turns the naive C into `vpmovzxbd`, `vpslld` and `vmaskmovps`, four per iteration (GCC 15 keeps a branch per element). So the naive C here is 0.03 ns per element, not the 2.4 of `maskfill_f64`'s, and the kernel's margin is the alignment alone. The x86-64 `maskfill_f64` scheme with eight lanes (a blend, and a store-only path for blocks with at most four selected elements) was 0.86x of it; a prefetch ahead of the masked stores 0.98x.
- **Figures (x86-64),** `uv run judge run maskfill_f32 --record --unlocked` on the Intel Core i5-14600KF at a load average of 1.0: 1.06x [1.06 – 1.06] over the naive C across its sizes and both masks, 31.5x over NumPy. 1.3x to 1.4x at 16,384 and 262,144 elements, 1.1x at 16 and 1,048,576, level from 1,024 to 4,096.
- **Where it is not faster.** At 64 and 256 elements, 0.83x to 0.87x: the extra masked store of the alignment is not paid back. The kernel ships as assembly because it is significantly faster than the C over all sizes. The margin is the alignment alone: the Linux wheel compiles C kernels for x86-64-v3, where clang makes the same masked stores from the C, so shipping the C would also be defensible.
- **The binding.** `x[mask] = v`, `np.putmask` and `np.copyto(x, v, where=mask)` take a float32 array with a Python float that is a quiet NaN, an infinity or a zero or rounds to a normal float32 (one rounding of the double, as in NumPy's cast), a Python int of at most 2^24 in magnitude, or an `np.float32`; an int32 array with a Python int in range or an `np.int32`; an int64 array with a Python int in range or an `np.int64`, through `maskfill_f64`. Every other value is NumPy's: a finite float that overflows float32 (NumPy warns, or raises under `np.errstate`), one that would become a denormal or zero (the conversion would raise the underflow flag), a signalling NaN (the invalid flag), a larger int (float32 rounds it), an int out of range (NumPy raises `OverflowError`), bools, floats into an integer array (truncated, or refused by `np.copyto`), `np.float64` into float32 and every other NumPy scalar. `x[mask]`, `np.compress` and `np.extract` take int32 as they take float32. The calls count on the float rows of `report()`, as int64 selection does.
- **From Python (x86-64),** `uv run delivery time` at a load average of 0.3 to 0.4, half the mask True. `x[mask] = 0.0` on float32: 288 ns on NumPy and 37.5 ns with the hook at 16 elements (7.7x), 4.43 µs and 194 ns at 4,096 (23x), 3.2 ms and 102 µs at 1,048,576 (31x); with 5% 7.4x, 7.2x and 9.6x. `x[mask] = 0` on int32 7.4x, 23x and 32x; on int64 7.3x, 9.9x and 18x. `x[mask]` on int32 2.5x, 13x and 15x with half selected, 2.3x, 4.3x and 9.6x with 5%. A call left to NumPy, such as an `np.float64` value, is within 1.3% of NumPy.

#### `np.isin` on int32

The issue's `np.isin` calls on int32 went to NumPy, which has no kernel for them: NumPy over a naive C loop with eight test values on the Intel Core i5-14600KF (load average 0.15) is 289x at 16 elements, 2.9x at 4,096 and 2.8x at 1,048,576. `isin_i32(x, test, out, n)` is `isin_i64` for int32 elements and eight int32 test values; its spec and reference mirror `isin_i64`'s, value classes for padded sets included.

- **Technique (x86-64).** 32 elements per block. When the eight test values lie within 65,533 of each other, each element x becomes the key c - x, with c the smallest test value plus 32,766 and the subtraction wrapping at 32 bits, so the test keys run from -32,767 to 32,766. Two `vpackssdw` narrow 32 keys to 16 bits. Their saturation is what makes this exact without a check of the block: a key outside the test keys' range becomes -32,768 or 32,767, which no test key is, and the subtraction is one-to-one, so equal keys mean equal values. Sixteen `vpcmpeqw` and fourteen ORs test the 32 keys, and `vpacksswb`, one `vpermd` and `vpabsb` give 32 bytes in element order. When every test value is already from -32,767 to 32,766 the subtraction is skipped (1.07x). Any other set is compared at 32 bits, eight `vpcmpeqd` per eight elements, the compiler's loop by hand. The leftover is one more block that overlaps the one before; 8 to 31 elements go eight at a time at 32 bits.
- **What `isin_i64` did not have.** Its x86-64 narrow path needs every test value from 0 to 32,767 and a check of each block, because 64-bit to 16-bit narrowing is three packs without a saturation it could use. At 32 bits one saturating pack does it, and the wrapping subtraction moves the window to wherever the test values are, the second window its log left untried.
- **Figures (x86-64),** `uv run judge run isin_i32 --record --unlocked` at a load average of 0.4: 1.44x [1.44 – 1.44] over the naive C across the judge's sizes and both distributions, 61.4x over NumPy. Test values from 0 to 999: 1.76x, 2.2x from 1,024 to 262,144 elements, 2.05x at 1,048,576, 1.1x to 1.3x from 4,194,304, where memory holds both. Values over the whole int32 range: 1.16x at every size. With the output 16 bytes behind the input, no cost (1.04x).
- **What limits it.** The vector ports: 35 instructions per 32 elements on the narrow path on three ports, 11.7 cycles, measured at about 12; the wide path's 32 compares on two ports, 16 cycles, with 28 ORs beside them. The naive C compiled for x86-64-v3, as the Linux wheels build it, is already the 32-bit compare loop, 0.16 ns per element.
- **Left untried.** 8-bit keys for test values within 253 of each other, `np.isin(x, [1, 2, 3])` among them: a second saturating pack would halve the compares. Neither distribution of the spec has such a set, so the judge could not have kept it.
- **The binding.** The `isin` hook takes a contiguous int32 array of any shape, as it takes int64, with one to eight test values that are a contiguous one-dimensional int32 array or a list or tuple of Python ints that each fit int32; a shorter set is padded by repeating its first value. Left to NumPy: test values of any other type (an int64 array, which NumPy compares after promoting both sides, int16, uint32), a Python int outside int32 (it could only never match, but the list is not trimmed), bools and floats in the list, more than eight values or none, a strided or two-dimensional test array, and every spelling with `invert`, `assume_unique`, `kind` or keywords, as for int64. Calls count on a row of their own in `report()`.
- **From Python (x86-64),** `uv run delivery time --only isin` at a load average of 0.7, eight test values from 0 to 999: `np.isin(x, eight_values)` on int32 takes 9.25 µs on NumPy and 118 ns with the hook at 16 elements (78x), 13.1 µs and 445 ns at 4,096 (29x), 1.59 ms and 77.6 µs at 1,048,576 (20x).

### `np.sum` on floats, in NumPy's order

From the coverage gaps of [issue 160](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/160), recorded in [coverage-gaps-arm64.md](coverage-gaps-arm64.md#built-on-arm64).

#### The operation

- **NumPy's order is fixed.** `np.sum` on a contiguous, aligned float array is one call of `pairwise_sum` (NumPy's `loops_utils.h.src`, the same in NumPy 2.3 to 2.5). Fewer than 8 elements are added one after another from +0.0. Up to 128 are a leaf: eight running sums, element `i` into sum `i mod 8`, combined as `((r0 + r1) + (r2 + r3)) + ((r4 + r5) + (r6 + r7))`, then the last `n mod 8` elements one after another. More than 128 are split at `n / 2` rounded down to a multiple of 8 and the halves' sums added. The reduction starts from zero: the result is `0 + pairwise(x, n)`. A kernel that makes the same additions on the same operands gives the same bits, so the spec is exact, unlike the cumulative sums'.
- **In vectors of 8 the tree is regular.** With `n = 8m + t`, every split falls at `8 * floor(m / 2)` elements, so only the nodes on the right edge carry the `t` leftover elements and every other node is a whole number of vectors split into `floor(m/2)` and `ceil(m/2)`. A node of 17 to 32 vectors is two leaves, of 34 to 64 four, of 68 to 128 eight; 33 and 65 to 67 have leaves at two depths.
- **The leaves do not wait for each other.** Within a leaf each running sum is a chain of additions, three cycles each on the Apple M1 Pro, which is all a compiled loop can do: eight float32 per three cycles. Different leaves are independent, so a kernel that runs the leaves of a node side by side is limited by loads instead. No compiler finds that: the leaves come from a recursion.
- **The sign of a zero is the one freedom.** A zero operand changes no sum that is not zero, so the sign of an intermediate zero can only reach a result that is zero, and NumPy's result is never `-0.0`. Running sums may start at `+0.0` instead of the first vector, and the result needs one final `+ 0.0`. The operands of an addition may be swapped; nothing may be fused or reassociated.
- **The domain is the finite sums.** A NaN's bits depend on which operand of an addition came first, which is the compiler's choice in NumPy, and NumPy warns on overflow. Once an addition is not finite none after it is, so the spec covers inputs whose sum in NumPy's order is finite, by passing every generated array through a `prepare` that halves an array whose sum is not (after replacing its NaNs and infinities by zero), and the binding sends a call to NumPy when the kernel's result is not finite.
- **What the judge cannot sweep.** The tree's shape depends on `n` irregularly, and the judge's sizes are every size to 65 and powers of two with their neighbours, with fresh cases to 5,000; the spec format has no field for more sizes. Every kept `kernel.s` was also compared with `np.sum` outside the judge on every `n` from 0 to 5,000 at four alignments and on about 4,500 random `n` up to 6,000,000, in eight kinds of data; the log says how.

#### On ARM64

- **Technique.** NumPy's tree is walked with a stack below `sp`, split until a node is a group of 2, 4 or 8 leaves (or a lone leaf, on the right edge or at an irregular node). A group's leaves are summed in one loop, each in its own two registers; eight leaves are sixteen chains, enough to run at the core's three 16-byte loads per cycle. Leaves have `q` or `q + 1` vectors: after the loop the longer ones add their last vector and the shorter ones a vector of `+0.0`, a step skipped when all are equal. Leaf sums are combined across leaves with `faddp`, in the node's order. 8 to 128 elements are one leaf, a straight run of 15 steps entered at its length; one group of two (136 to 256 elements, a multiple of 8) and one of eight (544 to 1,024, a multiple of 64) skip the walk. The log is `kernels/sum_f32/arm64/attempts.md`.
- **The first-level cache dislikes the same offset in different 256-byte blocks.** The group loop alone ran at the load limit when its eight leaves started 544 bytes apart and 20% slower when they started 256, 512 or 1,024 bytes apart, as they do at every power of two. Taking eight steps in a diagonal order, leaf `j`'s step `k` beside leaf 0's step `k + j`, puts the loads issued together at different offsets and recovers it, 1.14x at 4,096 elements. Beyond the first-level cache, visits of 64 bytes, `ldnp`, and a prefetch of the next group's leaf starts gain 4% to 10% more.
- **Figures,** `uv run judge run sum_f32 --unlocked --record` on 20 November 2026 on a quiet machine, at a load average of 1.5: 2.11x [2.10 – 2.11] over the naive C and 18.7x over NumPy across the eleven sizes.

#### `sum_f64` on ARM64

- **Technique.** The same walk at twice the width: a vector of eight elements is four registers, so the largest group side by side is four leaves (sixteen chains); eight would need 32 registers. While the array fits the first-level cache, groups of four in the diagonal order; nodes of 2, 4 or 8 groups summed one after another so that the walk passes once per 4,096 elements. Beyond it, no groups side by side: a node's leaves (up to sixteen) one after another in the order of memory, each a straight run entered at its length, their sums combined in registers; above 16 MB with `ldnp`. The log is `kernels/sum_f64/arm64/attempts.md`.
- **Slower than the C, until the walk stopped mispredicting.** With groups of four beyond the first-level cache, and then with leaves one at a time as the C has them, the kernel was 0.97x of the naive C at 65,536 and 262,144 elements and 0.87x to 0.91x at 1,048,576 (outside timer and judge both). clang's leaf is NumPy's loop, as the kernel's is; the difference was the walk. The C recurses, and the processor predicts its returns; the kernel pops a node or a combine marker after every leaf, in an order that follows the bits of a counter, and mispredicts that test, and the leaf loop's exit, about once per leaf. Beyond the first-level cache each misprediction throws away the loads issued for the leaves after it. Summing a node's leaves as straight runs in one pass of the walk made it 1.06x to 1.10x over the C there; at 1,048,576 `ldnp` had also cost 13% inside the second-level cache, and now starts at 16 MB.

### bottleneck's NaN-aware reductions

`nanmean_cols_f64`, `nanstd_cols_f64`, `nanmin_cols_f64` and `nanmax_cols_f64` serve calls that libraries send to bottleneck, not to NumPy: `bn.nanmean(a, axis=0)` and the rest, on a two-dimensional float64 matrix whose rows' elements are adjacent and whose rows are `step` elements apart. The design, the measurements that led to it and the binding are in [bottleneck-design.md](bottleneck-design.md).

- **The oracle is bottleneck, not NumPy.** bottleneck sums each column in order from +0.0, skipping NaN, and divides by the count; `nanstd` makes a second such pass over the squared differences from the mean; `nanmin` and `nanmax` keep the last element that compares `<=` (or `>=`), so a zero result has the sign of the last zero. NumPy differs from it in the last bit of `nanstd`, along the last axis, and in the sign of a zero minimum.
- **Technique.** Each kernel ships as its naive C on both architectures: 32 columns at a time, one accumulator each, a row at a time. Every column keeps bottleneck's order, so the result is bottleneck's bit for bit, and the additions of a row are independent, which bottleneck's one column at a time does not allow; clang makes vector code of the row. A NaN adds +0.0 instead of being skipped, which leaves a sum from +0.0 unchanged.
- **Fused or not is the build's.** bottleneck's macOS ARM64 wheel computes `asum += d * d` as one fused multiply-add; a baseline x86-64 build cannot. `nanstd_cols_f64`'s reference follows the platform with an `#if`, as `interp_f64`'s does, and its oracle is the bottleneck of the machine the judge runs on; CI checks it on both. Removing the fusion on ARM64 fails the judge at the first case.
- **Figures (ARM64),** from Python, best of 15 calls on the Apple M1 Pro at a load average of 14 (other agents running), against bottleneck's own call at 252 × 4,789: `nanmean` 5.23x on rolling windows (1.578 ms to 0.302 ms) and 4.07x C-ordered; `nanstd` with `ddof=1` 7.25x (3.900 ms to 0.538 ms) and 5.49x; `nanmin` 4.44x and 3.42x; `nanmax` 4.45x and 3.39x. No `judge run` has been recorded: the specs are not on `main`.
- **Where the room is.** No assembly has been tried. NumPy's `np.fmin.reduce` along axis 0 is 1.5x faster than the C `nanmin`, so a NEON kernel for `nanmin` and `nanmax` has that much at least to gain. `nanmean` and `nanstd` in one dimension, which the kernels do not take, can gain at most about 1.6x over bottleneck bit for bit: one chain of additions, at the latency of an add.

### Stable `argsort`

Kernels from [sort-design.md](sort-design.md), which took them from the applications study: half of a scikit-learn metrics run is `np.argsort(scores, kind="stable")`.

#### The operation

- **What NumPy requires.** The indices that sort the array, equal elements in their original order. NaN sorts last, and two elements are equal when neither is less than the other: `-0.0` and `+0.0`, and any two NaNs. A stable sort has exactly one right answer, which is what makes it possible to replace NumPy's sort with another.
- **What NumPy does.** Timsort for float64, int64 and uint64: runs found in the input, extended by insertion and merged. 124 ns per element at a million random float64, which is about twenty comparisons each, most of them mispredicted. On input already in order it is one pass, 1 ns per element.
- **What a C loop does.** A merge sort of indices is level with NumPy on integers and half as fast on float64 ([survey](survey-design.md#results-on-arm64)). There is no naive loop to vectorise here. The gain has to come from not comparing.

#### What was built

- **A least-significant-digit radix sort.** Each element becomes an unsigned key that orders like NumPy orders the element, and the keys are sorted by counting, one digit of eleven bits at a time from the lowest up. Every pass is stable, so the whole is, and the result is NumPy's bit for bit.
- **Only the bits that differ are sorted on.** One pass ORs and ANDs the keys. Integers below 1,000 differ in ten bits and are sorted in one pass, 1.8 ms for a million; keys that differ in all 64 take six.
- **One word per element.** A pass has to move the key and the index. Once a digit is sorted on, its bits are dead, so the element travels as the key bits still to come above its index, in 64 bits. Where the window is too wide for that, the first pass sorts on as many low bits as must go, up to 13, reading the keys from the input and writing packed words. From 16 bytes per element per pass to 8: 11.5 ms to 8.6 ms at a million float64.
- **Counts taken once, by a loop written out per number of passes.** The pass that counts every digit was 40% of the time while its shifts and masks were read from memory: 3.5 ms of 8.6. With the last pass writing the indices straight into the result, 7.0 ms.
- **The float key subtracts.** A positive number's key is 2^63 plus its magnitude, a negative number's 2^63 minus it. Inverting the bits of a negative number orders the same way, and turns the zero low bits of a whole number into ones: whole numbers of both signs then differ in all 64 bits and took 15.2 ms a million, against 4.7 ms.

#### What did not work

- **Assembly for the scatter loop.** Four elements per iteration, shifts and masks ahead of the count updates, with and without a prefetch of the source: 1.00x to 1.03x over the compiler's loop at every size from 4,096 to 4,194,304 words. clang emits the same seven instructions per element. Each store depends on a count the element before may have written, and past the second-level cache the pass runs at the speed of its 2,048 write streams.
- **Ranking the sign and exponent of a float.** Numbers of both signs differ in all 64 key bits, so from 16,384 elements they go through pairs for two passes: 9.5 ms a million against 7.5 for uniform data. Their top twelve bits take about forty values, and sorting on the rank of those narrows the window by six bits. It gained 5% to 11% on normal data between 16,384 and 262,144 elements, and cost data of one sign 5% to 19% at every size, for a store per element in a pass that otherwise only reads and two table lookups later.

#### What it costs

- **Memory.** Three words of output per element, of which the first is the result, and the binding adds a fourth for a copy of the input: 32 bytes per element while the call runs.
- **Stack.** 112 KB for the counts.
- **Ordered input.** A radix sort takes the same time whatever the order. The binding answers sorted and strictly descending arrays itself and leaves nearly sorted ones to NumPy ([delivery-design.md](delivery-design.md#which-hook-serves-which-kernel)).
- **Between 17 and 255 elements** the kernel is correct and no faster than NumPy's insertion sort. The binding takes calls from 256.

## Where the result is narrower than the number

The second item is about the reference and holds on every architecture. The others describe the ARM64 kernels and do not arise on x86-64, which has its own, listed after them.

- **`clip`: how fast depends on the bounds and, for a zero lower bound, on the data.** The two-operation path is taken when neither `lo` is `+0.0` nor `hi` is `-0.0`, because `fmax(-0.0, +0.0)` is `+0.0` where NumPy keeps `-0.0`. A lower bound of `+0.0`, as in `np.clip(x, 0, 1)`, has a three-operation path of its own: 1.32x to 1.34x over naive C at 4,096 elements on data without negative zeros, against 1.55x to 1.60x for the two-operation path. That path cannot be exact on its own, and no exact form in fewer than the four operations of compare-and-select was found, so it checks each chunk for a -0.0 and redoes the chunk when it finds one. A redo costs about 75 ns, so the path is quick to give up, and it is never tried again in the same call: one -0.0 among the first 7,264 float32 elements (3,632 for float64) sends the rest of the array to compare-and-select, however long the array is. Later than that, a single one costs one redo and the path carries on. At 65,536 float32 elements a call takes 6.1 microseconds with no negative zero, 7.5 with one at index 7,000, which is the speed of naive C, and 6.2 with one at index 8,000. On data with a negative zero that early the kernel runs at the speed of naive C from about 1,000 elements up, and up to about 14% slower at the smallest sizes that take the path, 256 elements for float64 and 512 for float32. Rounded data is such data: a value between -0.5 and 0 rounds to -0.0. The specs now time both kinds, which is where the figures come from. The judge also gained a value class with a single negative zero among ordinary values: without it nothing reached the redo of a chunk followed by more fast chunks, and a kernel whose redo stored nothing passed the check. An upper bound of `-0.0` still takes compare-and-select.
- **`clip`: defined for ordered bounds only.** For `lo > hi` NumPy returns `hi`, and for a NaN bound it returns NaN. The reference does neither. Compared outside the judge over 100 pairs of bounds per type, the reference matches NumPy bit for bit on every ordered pair and on none of the others. The specs now say so, the judge checks twelve ordered pairs (zeros of both signs, equal bounds, infinite, denormal and near-largest bounds), and the binding must send any other call to NumPy.
- **`clip`: reads and writes the floating-point status register.** To stay exact when the data holds a signalling NaN, the kernel reads FPSR after the fast loop and redoes the array if the invalid-operation flag was raised. If the caller left that flag set, it is cleared first and set again afterwards. No rule forbids this, flags are only ever added, and it is how a two-operation path can be exact at all; it is still more than "a pure function over its buffers" suggests, so it is listed under "Decisions".
- **`isin_i64`: fast only for test values between 0 and 65,534, or within 32,767 of the first one**, as above.
- **`cummax_i64`: slower than the naive C on one kind of data the judge does not time.** With a new maximum at a random half of 2,000,000 elements it takes 0.69 ns per element against the naive C's 0.63, as set out [under running maxima](#running-maxima-npmaximumaccumulate-and-npfmaxaccumulate). A version without the case exists in its log and is not adopted, being 0.4% slower on the judge's data.

**On x86-64:**

- **`isin_i64`: fast only for test values from 0 to 32,767.** Any other set takes the 64-bit compare, which is level with the naive C (1.08x). The ARM64 kernel's second window, around the first test value, is not there.
- **`isin_i32`: fast for test values within 65,533 of each other.** Any other set takes the 32-bit compare, 1.16x over the naive C.
- **`searchsorted` and `interp` with 64 needles against a large array.** The judge repeats the same needles, so a branching search learns them: at 16,777,216 elements the kernels are level with NumPy with 64 needles, and 2.6x (searches) and 6.9x (`interp`) ahead with 1,000.
- **`bincount_u8`: the gain on the judge's data is small** (1.04x to 1.2x over the naive C) and is 4x on bytes that are all equal, which the judge does not time.
- **`clip`: none of the three ARM64 items.** One path for every pair of ordered bounds and every kind of data.
- **`histogram_f64`: from 2^19 bins** the kernel is the naive C in assembly.

## What the judge does not see

Items 1, 2, 4 and 5 are about the judge and hold on every architecture. The measurements quoted in them, and items 3, 6 and 7, come from the ARM64 kernels and from the references as compiled for ARM64.

1. **Scalar values nobody listed.** The judge used to pass one value per scalar. That hid the clip reference being wrong for reversed and NaN bounds and the clip kernel's dependence on the bounds, and it was the reason the faster `histogram` path could not be judged. A spec now lists further combinations ([judge-design.md](judge-design.md#scalar-sets)): twelve pairs of bounds for `clip`, sixteen bin counts and ranges for `histogram`, six values for masked assignment. All four kernels and their references pass them. What remains unseen is any combination that is not listed, and data that depends on the scalars: nothing places elements on a histogram's bin edges. Both let the `histogram_f64` overflow through. No listed range was near the largest float64 values, and the two sets added there since would not have caught it either, because the only values the judge generates inside such a range sit on its outer edge.
2. **One input per size from 4,096 elements up.** Small sizes cycle through a pool of inputs; large sizes repeat one. A branch predictor memorises a repeated input, so code with data-dependent branches looks faster than it is up to about 16,384 elements. Among the references only naive `maskfill_f64` branches on every element, so the speedup reported for masked assignment at those sizes is understated. The naive `compress` and `nonzero` loops are branchless and are not affected. It also hides the mask kernels' own mispredictions when they switch between their dense and sparse paths.
3. **Densities and distributions between the ones declared.** The mask kernels are timed at 50% and 5%, and are weakest near 10%. `bincount_u8` is timed on uniformly random bytes; all-equal bytes run at 2.1 ns per byte below 8,192 elements.
4. **Paths behind a size cap.** `interp_f64` above 65,537 elements.
5. **A wide tolerance at large sizes for float32.** The `cumsum` bound grows with the element count. Past some tens of thousands of float32 elements it no longer notices a single skipped element. Small sizes are not affected.
6. **Prefetch addresses.** The two count kernels prefetch up to 256 KB past the end of the array when it is larger than 16 MB. A prefetch never faults and reads nothing, so the judge passes it.
7. **Sizes between the powers of four.** Every size the judge times is a multiple of 16, so the tail of `cummax_i64`, 1 to 15 elements after its last block of 16, is checked at every count and never timed; nor is data that is neither flat nor rising, where its one slow case is. The same holds for `cumsum_bool`, whose tail goes one element at a time: 15 elements take three times as long as 16 outside the judge, and a faster tail read 0.999x in `compare`, as set out [under `cumsum` on booleans](#cumsum-on-booleans).

**On x86-64**, beyond the items above that hold everywhere:

1. **Paths behind a size.** The stores past the caches (from 24 MB of output, in `cumsum`, `clip` and `cast`) and the 512 searches in step (from 262,144 elements, in the three search kernels) are timed by the judge and not reached by its correctness cases, which stop far below. Each was tested outside the judge against the reference C.
2. **Densities between the ones declared.** The mask kernels' per-block switch was measured outside the judge from 1% to 100% and has no weak spot; a second sparse tier for 15% to 25% could not be judged, because `compare` times 5% and 50%.
3. **The naive C's own time varies between runs** for `bincount_u8`, by up to 2x at single sizes, with where its buffers land. The kernel's did not.
4. **One reference could not be judged locked:** `interp_f64`, above.
5. **`unique_i64` with a range of 256 is timed on ten neighbouring values only.** Both of its timed inputs fit one window of 64 values, so `compare` sees the window and never the table behind it, nor the rule by which the window is given up. Labels spread over 65 to 256 values, the commoner meaning of such a range, are one store each. A set of two 64-bit words would take 65 to 128 values in about half the time, by the arithmetic of the kernel's attempt 5, and a loop that tests sixteen elements at once before storing them was rated 1.04x faster by `compare` while being 5% to 14% slower on spread labels outside it (attempt 4). Neither can be judged until the spec times such data ([issue 241](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/241)). The window's moves and its switch-off were tested outside the judge, against the naive C: 1,400,000 random calls over nine shapes of data, none wrong.

## Decisions

Each decision says which architecture it applies to. One taken for an ARM64 kernel is not taken for x86-64 by default.

Decided since the first version of this document:

- **`histogram_f64` takes the faster path.** 1.30x more, relying on an error-bound proof instead of performing NumPy's corrections on every element. It was adopted once the judge could test other bin counts and ranges. *ARM64. The proof is rechecked before an x86-64 kernel relies on it.*
- **The judge has scalar sets.** Several combinations of scalar values per spec, checked on kernel and reference alike and optionally timed. *Every architecture.*
- **`clip` has a path for a zero lower bound**, timed by the judge on data with and without negative zeros. *ARM64.*

Still open:

1. **`maskfill_f64`: is rewriting unselected elements acceptable?** It is the whole of the gain on dense masks. Recommended: yes for arrays the caller owns exclusively, which is NumPy's normal case, with the caveat documented. *ARM64. On x86-64 a store that leaves unselected elements alone may make the question unnecessary.*
2. **`clip`: is using FPSR acceptable?** Without it the kernel is the compare-and-select loop, which ties the C in cache. Recommended: yes, since the register is per thread and flags are only added, but it should be a stated exception to the kernel rules. *ARM64. On x86-64 the question may not arise.*
3. **Float `cumsum`: one overflow test per block instead of four?** About 12% faster from 256 to 16,384 elements. The proof that infinities land where NumPy has them would no longer hold for a running sum within rounding distance of the largest finite value. Recommended: no. *ARM64. It comes up again for any kernel that keeps the scheme.*

Open, from the x86-64 kernels. Each is a person's to decide; the kernels as committed take the conservative side of each.

4. **`maskfill_f64` on x86-64: adopt the masked store?** 1.6x faster up to 4,096 elements with half selected, 0.86x at 65,536 and 1,048,576, and unselected elements are never written. [Issue 70](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/70).
5. **`clip` on x86-64: record that the three ARM64 constraints do not hold, and let the binding pass in-place calls?** The kernel has one path, reads no status register and is right when `out` is `x`. The binding sends in-place `clip` to NumPy on both platforms today.
6. **Four borderline verdicts:** `bincount_u8` (1.04x over the naive C; 4x on repeated bytes), `count_nonzero_f64` (1.06x), `clip_f64` (1.09x) and `cast_u8_f32` (1.21x; level with NumPy in the middle sizes). Assembly is committed for all four. Turning one back into C no longer needs a change to the release build: the Linux wheel is built with clang since `unique_i64`, which shipped as C on x86-64 until it had assembly there, as the `argsort` kernels and bottleneck's four still do ([release-design.md](release-design.md#more-than-one-platform)).
7. **Stores past the caches below 24 MB of output?** They are 1.3x faster from 2 MB in a micro-benchmark, and leave an output that would otherwise sit in the third-level cache out of every cache. The kernels use them from 24 MB, the size of that cache.
8. **`interp_f64`'s reference.** Corrected in a pull request of its own ([issue 73](https://github.com/sofeikov/hotkernels/issues/73)) and merged. CI checks it on both architectures (157,062 cases pass on each), and the x86-64 kernel's figures are from a locked run. Nothing is left to decide.

- Re-run every kernel locked after this is merged, record the results and generate the leaderboard.
- The binding and `install()` are done on both platforms: [delivery-design.md](delivery-design.md). What it showed the kernels still need is listed there.
- On x86-64: the open decisions 4 to 8 above; `uv run judge run interp_f64` locked once its reference is merged; and the second runs the logs point to (aligned loads and a prefetch for `argmax`, a window that is not at zero for `isin`, a second sparse tier for the mask kernels once the judge times a density in between).
- Ideas found on x86-64 that do not depend on it, for a later ARM64 run: the multiply by a precomputed scale in place of `histogram`'s divide, and its last bin on the fast path; accumulators that are never reset and one branch per block in `argmax`, which bounds the second scan; a compare of magnitudes in place of the sum of squares in float `cumsum`.
